<h1>Omega-3 (ALA)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) is an essential omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid that must be obtained from the diet, as humans lack the delta-12 and delta-15 desaturase enzymes needed for its synthesis. It is the most widely consumed omega-3 fatty acid, primarily found in plant-based sources such as flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts, and hemp seeds.</p>
<p>ALA serves as a precursor to the longer-chain omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA through a series of elongation and desaturation steps. However, this conversion is highly inefficient in humans -- typically only 5-10% of ALA converts to EPA and less than 5% to DHA. Conversion rates are further reduced by high omega-6 intake, as linoleic acid competes for the same delta-6 desaturase enzyme. Women of reproductive age show modestly higher conversion rates, likely due to estrogen upregulation of the pathway.</p>
<p>Despite limited conversion, ALA has independent health benefits. Epidemiological studies associate higher ALA intake with reduced cardiovascular mortality, improved vascular endothelial function, and anti-inflammatory effects. ALA also contributes to cell membrane fluidity and serves as an energy source. The adequate intake is 1.1 g/day for women and 1.6 g/day for men, though ALA should not be considered a reliable substitute for preformed EPA and DHA.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Serves as an essential fatty acid that must be obtained from the diet</li>
<li>Provides the precursor substrate for EPA and DHA biosynthesis</li>
<li>Contributes to cell membrane structure and fluidity</li>
<li>Demonstrates independent anti-inflammatory activity</li>
<li>Supports cardiovascular health and vascular endothelial function</li>
<li>Provides metabolic energy through beta-oxidation</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Flaxseed and flaxseed oil (richest source)</li>
<li>Chia seeds</li>
<li>Walnuts</li>
<li>Hemp seeds and hemp oil</li>
<li>Canola (rapeseed) oil</li>
<li>Edamame and soybeans</li>
<li>Perilla oil</li>
<li>Brussels sprouts and spinach (small amounts)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>ALA deficiency is rare in isolation but contributes to broader essential fatty acid deficiency, manifesting as dry, scaly skin, dermatitis, poor wound healing, and increased susceptibility to inflammatory conditions. Because ALA is the only essential omega-3 (the body cannot synthesize it), prolonged deficiency impairs the limited endogenous production of EPA and DHA.</p>
<p>Excess ALA intake at very high levels (from concentrated oils) may theoretically increase bleeding risk, though this is less clinically significant than with EPA/DHA. High ALA intake may cause gastrointestinal discomfort (bloating, diarrhea). Some epidemiological studies suggested an association between high ALA and prostate cancer risk, but subsequent meta-analyses have not confirmed a consistent relationship.</p>
