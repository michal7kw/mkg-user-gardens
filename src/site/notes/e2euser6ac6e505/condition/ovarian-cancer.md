<h1>Ovarian Cancer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Ovarian cancer is a collection of malignancies arising from the ovary, fallopian tube, or peritoneal surface. The dominant and most lethal subtype is <strong>high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC)</strong>, the majority of which is now thought to originate in the fallopian tube fimbriae rather than the ovarian surface itself. Other epithelial subtypes (low-grade serous, endometrioid, clear cell, mucinous) and non-epithelial tumors (germ cell, sex cord-stromal) behave distinctly, with different molecular drivers, prognoses, and treatment responses.</p>
<p>Clinically, ovarian cancer is often termed the &quot;silent killer.&quot; Early disease produces only vague, nonspecific symptoms — bloating, abdominal or pelvic discomfort, early satiety, and urinary changes — that are easily attributed to benign gastrointestinal or menopausal causes. As a result, roughly two-thirds of patients are diagnosed at an advanced (FIGO stage III-IV) stage, with disease already disseminated across the peritoneal cavity. This late presentation is the principal reason ovarian cancer is the most fatal of the gynecologic cancers.</p>
<p>A defining feature of HGSC is its strong link to defects in the homologous recombination DNA-repair pathway, most notably germline and somatic BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations. This biology underlies both inherited risk and a therapeutic vulnerability: tumors with homologous recombination deficiency are highly sensitive to platinum chemotherapy and to PARP inhibitor maintenance, which has reshaped management over the past decade.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The classic symptom cluster is persistent bloating, pelvic or abdominal pain, difficulty eating or early satiety, and urinary urgency or frequency — particularly when these are new, frequent (occurring on most days), and persistent over several weeks. Advanced disease frequently presents with abdominal distension from <strong>malignant ascites</strong>, a palpable pelvic or omental mass, and sometimes pleural effusion. Because symptoms overlap with common benign conditions, a low threshold for evaluation (pelvic exam, imaging, CA 125) in symptomatic women is essential.</p>

<p>CA 125 is the principal marker but is nonspecific (elevated in endometriosis, fibroids, pregnancy, and other conditions), so <strong>HE4</strong> and the combined <strong>ROMA index</strong> improve discrimination of malignant from benign masses, especially in premenopausal women. Definitive diagnosis and staging require surgery with histopathology.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant non-modifiable risks are increasing age and inherited predisposition — germline <strong>BRCA1/BRCA2</strong> mutations confer a substantially elevated lifetime risk, and Lynch syndrome raises risk of endometrioid and clear cell types. Reproductive factors that increase lifetime ovulation (nulliparity, early menarche, late menopause) raise risk, whereas combined oral contraceptive use, multiparity, and breastfeeding are protective. Endometriosis predisposes to clear cell and endometrioid subtypes.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Standard treatment combines <strong>cytoreductive (debulking) surgery</strong> — aiming for no macroscopic residual disease — with <strong>platinum-taxane chemotherapy</strong> (carboplatin plus paclitaxel), given as adjuvant or, for bulky disease, neoadjuvant therapy. <strong>PARP inhibitor maintenance</strong> has become central, especially for BRCA-mutated or homologous-recombination-deficient tumors, markedly prolonging progression-free survival. Antiangiogenic therapy (bevacizumab) is used in selected patients. Treatment of recurrence is guided by the platinum-free interval, distinguishing platinum-sensitive from platinum-resistant disease.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Advanced and recurrent disease commonly causes malignant ascites, bowel obstruction from peritoneal carcinomatosis, pleural effusions, and venous thromboembolism. Cumulative chemotherapy toxicity and the eventual emergence of platinum-resistant, treatment-refractory disease drive late-stage morbidity and mortality.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis depends strongly on stage and on the completeness of surgical cytoreduction. Disease confined to the ovary (stage I) has a favorable five-year survival, but because most patients present with advanced peritoneal spread, overall survival is poor. BRCA-mutated/HRD tumors tend to respond better to platinum and PARP inhibitors, conferring a relative survival advantage.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Ovarian cancer is less common than breast or endometrial cancer but causes more deaths than any other gynecologic malignancy. Incidence rises with age and peaks in the postmenopausal years. There is no effective population-wide screening test, which contributes to the persistently advanced stage at diagnosis.</p>
