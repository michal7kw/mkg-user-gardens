<h1>Iron</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Iron is an essential mineral and the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide, affecting over 1.6 billion people. It exists in two dietary forms: heme iron (from animal sources, 15-35% absorption) and non-heme iron (from plant sources, 2-20% absorption). Iron is a critical component of hemoglobin, which transports oxygen from the lungs to tissues, and myoglobin, which stores oxygen in muscle cells.</p>
<p>Beyond oxygen transport, iron serves as a cofactor in cytochrome enzymes of the electron transport chain (essential for ATP production), in DNA synthesis enzymes (ribonucleotide reductase), and in immune cell proliferation. Iron metabolism is tightly regulated by hepcidin, a liver-produced hormone that controls intestinal absorption and macrophage iron recycling. This regulation prevents both deficiency and toxic iron overload.</p>
<p>Iron absorption is enhanced significantly by vitamin C (ascorbic acid), which reduces ferric iron (Fe3+) to the more absorbable ferrous form (Fe2+). Conversely, calcium, tannins (tea, coffee), phytates (grains, legumes), and oxalates impair non-heme iron absorption. Iron needs are highest during pregnancy, infancy, adolescence, and in menstruating women.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Transports oxygen via hemoglobin in red blood cells</li>
<li>Stores oxygen in muscle tissue via myoglobin</li>
<li>Enables mitochondrial energy production through cytochrome enzymes</li>
<li>Serves as a cofactor for DNA synthesis and cell proliferation</li>
<li>Supports immune function through lymphocyte and macrophage activity</li>
<li>Participates in neurotransmitter synthesis (dopamine, serotonin)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Red meat (beef, lamb)</li>
<li>Organ meats (liver, kidney)</li>
<li>Oysters, clams, and mussels</li>
<li>Spinach and dark leafy greens</li>
<li>Lentils, chickpeas, and kidney beans</li>
<li>Fortified cereals and bread</li>
<li>Tofu and tempeh</li>
<li>Pumpkin seeds and quinoa</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Iron deficiency progresses through three stages: depletion of iron stores (low ferritin), iron-deficient erythropoiesis (low transferrin saturation), and iron-deficiency anemia (low hemoglobin). Symptoms include fatigue, pallor, brittle nails (koilonychia), restless legs syndrome, cold intolerance, and pica. Cognitive impairment and impaired exercise capacity are common.</p>
<p>Iron overload, whether from hereditary hemochromatosis (HFE gene mutations) or chronic transfusions, causes oxidative damage via Fenton chemistry. Excess iron deposits in the liver (cirrhosis), heart (cardiomyopathy), pancreas (diabetes), and joints (arthropathy). Supplemental iron can cause constipation, nausea, and abdominal pain. Iron supplements should only be taken when deficiency is documented.</p>
