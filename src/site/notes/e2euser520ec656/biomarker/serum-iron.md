<h1>Serum Iron</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Serum iron is a direct measurement of the concentration of iron bound to transferrin (the principal iron-transport protein) circulating in the bloodstream at the moment of phlebotomy. It is one of four classic parameters used jointly to evaluate iron status, alongside serum ferritin (the storage-iron proxy), total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) or transferrin, and transferrin saturation (the calculated ratio of serum iron to TIBC). Together these form a standard iron panel.</p>
<p>As a standalone marker, serum iron has substantial limitations: it exhibits marked diurnal variation (morning values can be 30% higher than evening), is heavily influenced by recent dietary intake (requiring a 12-hour fast for reliability), and fluctuates acutely during inflammation or infection (transferrin is a negative acute-phase reactant). For these reasons, modern guidelines (British Society for Haematology 2022; WHO/CDC) recommend interpreting serum iron only in conjunction with ferritin and transferrin saturation rather than as a solitary result. Nonetheless, serum iron remains essential for calculating transferrin saturation -- the most sensitive non-invasive marker for early iron overload detection in hereditary hemochromatosis.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (well-validated, guidelines-based use as part of iron panel).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Significant diurnal variation: AM samples ~30% higher than PM</li>
<li>Fasting required (12 h) for reliable interpretation</li>
<li>Reference range is assay-dependent (check local lab)</li>
<li>Always interpret with transferrin saturation and ferritin</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Serum Iron:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Recent iron ingestion (dietary or supplemental) -- requires 12-h fast</li>
<li>Iron overload states (hereditary hemochromatosis, transfusional siderosis)</li>
<li>Acute hepatocellular damage (release of stored iron from hepatocytes)</li>
<li>Hemolytic anemia, ineffective erythropoiesis</li>
<li>Acute iron poisoning (pediatric ingestion)</li>
<li>Oral contraceptives / estrogen therapy (elevate transferrin)</li>
<li>Apost-transfusional state</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Serum Iron:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Iron deficiency (most common cause)</li>
<li>Chronic inflammation / infection (anemia of chronic disease; interleukin-6 drives hepcidin-mediated iron sequestration)</li>
<li>Recent menstruation</li>
<li>Pregnancy (hemodilution + fetal demand)</li>
<li>Malabsorption (celiac disease, H. pylori, atrophic gastritis, bariatric surgery)</li>
<li>Chronic blood loss (GI, menstrual)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hereditary hemochromatosis</strong> (HFE C282Y/C282Y genotype) -- transferrin saturation &gt;45% (women) or &gt;50% (men) at fasting is the earliest non-invasive marker; serum iron itself is high</li>
<li><strong>Transfusional iron overload</strong> (beta-thalassemia major, MDS, sickle cell) -- serial monitoring guides chelation therapy</li>
<li><strong>Acute viral hepatitis / cirrhosis</strong> -- release of stored iron from damaged hepatocytes</li>
<li><strong>Iron poisoning</strong> (pediatric) -- serum iron &gt;500 mcg/dL is severe</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency</strong> (absolute or functional) -- low serum iron + low ferritin + high TIBC is the classic triad</li>
<li><strong>Anemia of chronic disease</strong> (low serum iron + low TIBC + high ferritin, normal/high storage iron)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (physiologic hemodilution; does not by itself indicate deficiency)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Iron deficiency anemia workup (alongside ferritin, transferrin saturation, CBC)</li>
<li>Hereditary hemochromatosis screening and therapeutic phlebotomy monitoring</li>
<li>Iron supplementation monitoring (peak 3-6 months post-initiation)</li>
<li>Iron overload chelation therapy (thalassemia)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Iron supplementation</strong> (oral ferrous sulfate, ferrous glycinate, ferrous fumarate) -- 30-100 mg elemental iron daily for deficiency. Take on empty stomach if tolerated; alternate-day dosing improves absorption per Mayo Clinic 2020 RCT (PMID: 31869294).</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C co-supplementation</strong> (200-500 mg with iron) -- enhances non-heme iron absorption by reducing Fe3+ to Fe2+ and forming absorbable chelate; especially useful in iron-deficient women</li>
<li><strong>Heme-iron dietary emphasis</strong> -- red meat, organ meats, shellfish (oysters, clams). Heme iron is ~25% absorbed vs ~5% for non-heme.</li>
<li><strong>Cast-iron cookware</strong> -- increases iron content of acidic foods (e.g., tomato sauce) up to 16-fold; evidence-based intervention in iron-deficient populations</li>
<li><strong>Avoid calcium, coffee, tea, bran at the same meal as iron-rich foods</strong> -- these inhibit non-heme iron absorption. Separate by 1-2 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Investigate and treat underlying cause:</strong> celiac disease (serology + biopsy), H. pylori eradication, menorrhagia control, GI bleeding workup</li>
</ol>
