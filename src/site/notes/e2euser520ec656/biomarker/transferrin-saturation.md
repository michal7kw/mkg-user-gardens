<h1>Transferrin Saturation</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Transferrin saturation (TSAT) is the calculated percentage of iron-binding sites on transferrin that are occupied by iron, derived from the formula: <strong>TSAT (%) = (Serum Iron / TIBC) × 100</strong> or equivalently (Serum Iron / (Transferrin × 1.4)) × 100. TSAT integrates two iron panel measurements (serum iron and TIBC or transferrin) into a single value that reflects the balance between iron supply and iron-binding capacity in the circulation. It is widely regarded as the <strong>most clinically useful single marker</strong> of iron status -- better than serum iron alone (which is highly variable), better than ferritin alone (which is an acute-phase reactant), and better than TIBC alone (which is non-specific).</p>
<p>In <strong>iron deficiency</strong>, serum iron is low and TIBC is high (the liver up-regulates transferrin to maximize absorption), so the ratio (TSAT) falls sharply. A TSAT &lt;20% in the appropriate clinical context (e.g., microcytic anemia) is essentially diagnostic of iron deficiency. In <strong>iron overload (hereditary hemochromatosis)</strong>, serum iron is high and TIBC is low (transferrin down-regulated), so TSAT rises sharply. <strong>TSAT &gt;45% (women) or &gt;50% (men) at fasting is the earliest non-invasive marker of hereditary hemochromatosis</strong>, often abnormal years before ferritin rises -- making TSAT the recommended screening test in patients with family history or unexplained elevated ferritin.</p>
<p>In <strong>anemia of chronic disease</strong>, both serum iron and TIBC fall (hepcidin-mediated sequestration and down-regulation), so TSAT remains low-normal or low. The combination of low-normal TSAT with normal/high ferritin differentiates anemia of chronic disease from iron deficiency (where TSAT is low and ferritin is low).</p>
<p>In <strong>dialysis patients</strong>, TSAT &lt;20% plus ferritin &lt;200 ng/mL defines absolute iron deficiency per KDIGO; TSAT &lt;20% plus ferritin &gt;800 ng/mL defines functional iron deficiency (iron trapped in macrophages despite adequate stores). TSAT is monitored monthly in hemodialysis patients to guide IV iron and ESA dosing.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational hematology test; KDIGO-recommended for dialysis patients; universal clinical use).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fasting morning sample essential</strong> -- postprandial iron raises TSAT falsely (iron rises 30-50% after a meal)</li>
<li><strong>Acute inflammation lowers TSAT</strong> (hepcidin sequesters iron); repeat when inflammation resolves</li>
<li><strong>Diurnal variation:</strong> morning TSAT ~10-15% higher than afternoon</li>
<li><strong>Hemolysis</strong> falsely lowers TSAT</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> TSAT physiologically rises (iron demand + estrogen)</li>
<li><strong>Acute hepatitis</strong> falsely raises TSAT (iron release from damaged hepatocytes)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase TSAT (hyperferremia / iron overload):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hereditary hemochromatosis</strong> (HFE C282Y homozygous; TSAT &gt;45-50% at fasting is the earliest marker)</li>
<li><strong>Transfusional iron overload</strong> (beta-thalassemia major, MDS, sickle cell)</li>
<li><strong>Excessive iron supplementation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Hemolytic anemia</strong> (iron release from RBC destruction)</li>
<li><strong>Sideroblastic anemia, ineffective erythropoiesis</strong> (thalassemia, MDS)</li>
<li><strong>Acute hepatitis / hepatocellular necrosis</strong> (iron release from damaged hepatocytes)</li>
<li><strong>Post-prandial / non-fasting sample</strong> (false elevation)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease TSAT (hypoferrremia / iron deficiency):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency anemia</strong> (the most common cause)</li>
<li><strong>Anemia of chronic disease</strong> (hepcidin-mediated iron sequestration)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic blood loss</strong> (GI, menstrual)</li>
<li><strong>Malabsorption</strong> (celiac disease, gastrectomy, gastric bypass, H. pylori)</li>
<li><strong>Inadequate dietary iron intake</strong></li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (increased iron demand; usually normal TSAT though, due to estrogen effects)</li>
<li><strong>Functional iron deficiency in CKD/dialysis patients on ESA</strong></li>
<li><strong>Acute inflammation</strong> (hepcidin surge)</li>
<li><strong>Recent surgery, trauma, infection</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated TSAT is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hereditary hemochromatosis:</strong> TSAT &gt;45% (F) or &gt;50% (M) at fasting is the recommended screening threshold per AASLD 2022 and EASL 2022 guidelines. TSAT &gt;60% (F) or &gt;70% (M) is essentially diagnostic</li>
<li><strong>Transfusional iron overload</strong> in thalassemia, MDS, sickle cell -- serial monitoring guides chelation therapy</li>
<li><strong>Acute hepatitis</strong> -- transient elevation from hepatocyte release</li>
<li><strong>Idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria</strong> (rare)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Low TSAT is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency anemia:</strong> TSAT &lt;20% with low ferritin is diagnostic; the principal clinical use</li>
<li><strong>Anemia of chronic disease:</strong> TSAT &lt;20% with normal/high ferritin (distinguishes from iron deficiency)</li>
<li><strong>Functional iron deficiency in dialysis:</strong> TSAT &lt;20% with ferritin &gt;800 ng/mL (iron trapped in macrophages); treated with IV iron</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Affected by acute inflammation</strong> (hepcidin surge lowers TSAT; repeat when stable)</li>
<li><strong>Diurnal and postprandial variation</strong> -- fasting morning sample essential</li>
<li><strong>Inter-assay variability</strong> in serum iron and TIBC propagates into TSAT</li>
<li><strong>Cannot replace ferritin for body iron stores</strong> -- ferritin reflects stores; TSAT reflects circulating iron availability</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Anemia workup (with ferritin, MCV, TIBC, reticulocytes)</li>
<li>Hemochromatosis screening and monitoring (with ferritin, liver enzymes, genetic testing)</li>
<li>Dialysis anemia management (monthly with ferritin)</li>
<li>Iron supplementation monitoring (target TSAT normalization &gt;25%)</li>
<li>Pre-operative anemia optimization (TSAT &gt;20% before elective surgery)</li>
<li>IV iron / ESA dose adjustment in CKD/dialysis</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>TSAT reflects underlying iron metabolism; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For low TSAT (iron deficiency):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Oral iron supplementation:</strong> ferrous sulfate 325 mg TID (65 mg elemental iron TID) on empty stomach; alternate-day dosing improves absorption and reduces side effects (Stoffel 2017 RCT)</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C</strong> 200-500 mg with iron enhances non-heme iron absorption</li>
<li><strong>Heme-iron dietary emphasis:</strong> red meat, organ meats, shellfish (oysters, clams)</li>
<li><strong>Cast-iron cookware</strong> (acidic foods absorb iron)</li>
<li><strong>Avoid calcium, coffee, tea, bran</strong> at the same meal as iron</li>
<li><strong>IV iron</strong> (ferric carboxymaltose, ferric gluconate, ferumoxytol) for malabsorption, severe deficiency, CKD/dialysis, or oral iron intolerance</li>
<li><strong>Investigate and treat underlying cause:</strong> celiac disease, H. pylori, menorrhagia, GI bleeding</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For elevated TSAT (iron overload):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hereditary hemochromatosis:</strong> confirm with HFE genetic testing (C282Y/C282Y is most common); therapeutic phlebotomy (1 unit weekly until ferritin &lt;50 ng/mL; then maintenance phlebotomy q3-4 months)</li>
<li><strong>Iron chelation</strong> for transfusional overload: deferoxamine (IV), deferasirox (oral), deferiprone (oral) -- for thalassemia, MDS, sickle cell</li>
<li><strong>Avoid iron supplementation, vitamin C with iron, alcohol</strong> (which enhance iron absorption)</li>
<li><strong>Limit dietary iron:</strong> avoid red meat, fortified cereals, iron supplements; limit vitamin C with iron-rich meals</li>
<li><strong>Hepatology and cardiology monitoring</strong> for end-organ damage (cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For functional iron deficiency (CKD/dialysis):</strong> IV iron ( ferric carboxymaltose, ferric gluconate) to replete; ESA dose optimization</li>
<li><strong>Pre-operative optimization:</strong> iron therapy before elective surgery to raise TSAT &gt;20% and reduce transfusion need</li>
</ol>
