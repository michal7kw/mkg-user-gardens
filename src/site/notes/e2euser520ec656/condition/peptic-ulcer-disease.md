<h1>Peptic Ulcer Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) refers to mucosal breaks greater than a defined depth in the stomach (gastric ulcers) or the proximal duodenum (duodenal ulcers) that penetrate through the muscularis mucosae. Ulcers form when aggressive acid-peptic factors overwhelm mucosal defense and repair mechanisms. The historical view that ulcers were primarily caused by stress and excess acid was overturned by the recognition that <strong>Helicobacter pylori infection</strong> and <strong>nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)</strong> account for the overwhelming majority of cases.</p>
<p>H. pylori colonizes the gastric mucosa and induces chronic inflammation that disrupts acid regulation and mucosal integrity, predisposing to both duodenal and gastric ulcers. NSAIDs and aspirin cause ulcers by inhibiting cyclooxygenase-derived prostaglandins that maintain mucosal blood flow, mucus, and bicarbonate secretion. Cigarette smoking, alcohol, and physiological stress are contributory, and rare hypersecretory states such as Zollinger-Ellison syndrome must be considered in atypical or refractory cases.</p>
<p>Clinically, PUD matters chiefly because of its complications. While many ulcers heal with acid suppression and removal of the underlying cause, bleeding, perforation, and gastric outlet obstruction remain important sources of morbidity and mortality, particularly in older adults on antiplatelet or anti-inflammatory drugs.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The classic symptom is epigastric pain described as burning or gnawing. Duodenal ulcer pain is often relieved by eating and recurs hours later or at night, whereas gastric ulcer pain may be aggravated by food and can accompany weight loss. Many ulcers — especially those caused by NSAIDs — are clinically silent until a complication such as bleeding (hematemesis, melena) or perforation occurs.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Upper endoscopy (EGD) is the diagnostic gold standard, allowing direct visualization, assessment of complications, and biopsy. Gastric ulcers warrant biopsy to exclude malignancy. All patients should be tested for H. pylori using stool antigen testing, the urea breath test, or biopsy-based methods, and a careful drug history identifies NSAID/aspirin exposure.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The two dominant risk factors are H. pylori infection and NSAID/aspirin use, which act synergistically. Additional contributors include cigarette smoking, heavy alcohol use, and physiological stress (notably in critically ill patients). The rare gastrin-secreting tumor of Zollinger-Ellison syndrome causes refractory and multiple ulcers.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>First-line therapy is acid suppression with a proton pump inhibitor to promote healing. When H. pylori is present, eradication with a combination antibiotic-plus-PPI regimen is essential and markedly reduces recurrence. NSAIDs and aspirin should be stopped where clinically feasible, or co-prescribed with gastroprotection. Actively bleeding ulcers are managed with endoscopic hemostasis plus high-dose PPI, while perforation, refractory hemorrhage, or obstruction may require surgery.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The major complications are upper gastrointestinal bleeding (the most common), perforation leading to peritonitis (a surgical emergency), gastric outlet obstruction from scarring or edema, and penetration into adjacent structures such as the pancreas. Bleeding and perforation carry significant mortality, especially in elderly and comorbid patients.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>With acid suppression and treatment of the underlying cause, uncomplicated peptic ulcers heal in the majority of patients, and H. pylori eradication dramatically lowers recurrence. Prognosis worsens with complications, advanced age, and ongoing NSAID/antiplatelet use.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>The lifetime prevalence of peptic ulcer disease has declined in many regions alongside falling H. pylori prevalence and more judicious acid-suppression and NSAID prescribing. However, it remains common globally, with the balance of causes shifting toward NSAID-related ulcers in populations with low H. pylori burden.</p>
