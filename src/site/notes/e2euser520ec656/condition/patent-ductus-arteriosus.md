<h1>Patent Ductus Arteriosus</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The ductus arteriosus is a normal fetal blood vessel connecting the <strong>pulmonary artery</strong> to the <strong>descending aorta</strong>, allowing oxygenated placental blood to bypass the fetal lungs (which are not yet functional). After birth, the increased oxygen tension and decreased prostaglandin levels trigger <strong>functional closure within 12-72 hours</strong> and <strong>anatomical closure within 2-3 weeks</strong> (forming the ligamentum arteriosum).</p>
<p><strong>Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)</strong> occurs when this vessel fails to close. It is one of the most common congenital heart defects, accounting for ~5-10% of all congenital heart disease.</p>
<p><strong>Pathophysiology:</strong> The open ductus allows blood to shunt from the high-pressure aorta to the lower-pressure pulmonary artery (<strong>left-to-right shunt</strong>). This increases pulmonary blood flow, pulmonary venous return to the left atrium, and left ventricular volume load. Small PDAs are hemodynamically insignificant; large PDAs cause:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Left-to-right shunting</strong> -&gt; pulmonary overcirculation</li>
<li><strong>Left heart volume overload</strong> -&gt; left atrial and ventricular dilation</li>
<li><strong>Heart failure</strong> (in infants): tachypnea, poor feeding, failure to thrive</li>
<li><strong>Pulmonary hypertension</strong> (long-standing large PDA) -&gt; eventual right-to-left shunting (Eisenmenger syndrome)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Auscultatory hallmark:</strong> continuous &quot;machinery&quot; murmur at the left upper sternal border (both systolic and diastolic, peaking at S2). Bounding peripheral pulses and widened pulse pressure (from diastolic runoff into the pulmonary circuit) are also characteristic.</p>
<p><strong>Two distinct clinical contexts:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>PDA in premature infants:</strong> very common (~60% of infants &lt;28 weeks); due to immature smooth muscle and high prostaglandin sensitivity; often closes with indomethacin/ibuprofen or conservative management</li>
<li><strong>PDA in term infants/adults:</strong> less common; usually requires percutaneous or surgical closure; associated with genetic syndromes</li>
</ol>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Premature infants:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Conservative:</strong> fluid restriction, PEEP optimization; many close spontaneously</li>
<li><strong>Pharmacological closure:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Indomethacin</strong> 0.1-0.3 mg/kg IV q12-24h x 3 doses</li>
<li><strong>Ibuprofen</strong> 10 mg/kg then 5 mg/kg q24h x 2 doses (equally effective; fewer renal side effects)</li>
<li><strong>Paracetamol/acetaminophen</strong> 15 mg/kg q6h x 3 days (emerging evidence; similar efficacy; fewer renal side effects)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Surgical ligation</strong> (if pharmacological closure fails or contraindicated)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Term infants, children, and adults:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Percutaneous device closure</strong> (transcatheter; preferred for most):
<ul>
<li>Coil occlusion (for small PDAs &lt;3 mm)</li>
<li>Amplatzer duct occluder (for moderate-large PDAs)</li>
<li>Success rate &gt;95%; low complication rate</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Surgical ligation or division</strong> (thoracotomy or VATS; for very large PDAs or failed percutaneous attempt)</li>
<li><strong>Observation</strong> (small &quot;silent&quot; PDA in adults; no endocarditis risk if no murmur and no shunt)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Endocarditis prophylaxis:</strong> recommended for unrepaired or residually defective PDA before dental procedures (amoxicillin 50 mg/kg)</p>
