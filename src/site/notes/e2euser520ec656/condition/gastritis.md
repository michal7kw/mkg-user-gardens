<h1>Gastritis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Gastritis is inflammation of the gastric mucosa, the lining of the stomach. It is a histologic diagnosis that encompasses a spectrum of conditions ranging from transient, self-limited acute inflammation to chronic, progressive mucosal damage. The term is often used loosely in clinical practice to describe upper-abdominal discomfort, but true gastritis is defined by inflammatory changes in the mucosa identified on biopsy.</p>
<p>The two dominant causes are infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori and the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). H. pylori colonizes the gastric epithelium and provokes a chronic immune response that, over years, can drive mucosal atrophy and intestinal metaplasia. NSAIDs and aspirin damage the mucosa by inhibiting cyclooxygenase and depleting protective prostaglandins, producing erosive and sometimes hemorrhagic gastritis. Other causes include excess alcohol, bile reflux, severe physiologic stress, and autoimmune destruction of acid-secreting parietal cells.</p>
<p>Gastritis matters clinically because of where it can lead. Erosive forms can bleed acutely, chronic H. pylori gastritis is the principal precursor lesion for gastric adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma, and autoimmune atrophic gastritis causes pernicious anemia through loss of intrinsic factor and impaired vitamin B12 absorption. Recognizing and treating the underlying cause — most importantly eradicating H. pylori and removing mucosal irritants — is the foundation of management.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Many people with chronic gastritis are entirely asymptomatic. When symptoms occur, they are usually dyspeptic: epigastric pain or burning, nausea, early satiety, and bloating. Erosive gastritis can present dramatically with upper gastrointestinal bleeding, manifesting as melena or hematemesis. Atrophic and autoimmune forms may present indolently with fatigue and the neurologic or hematologic features of vitamin B12 or iron deficiency.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Definitive diagnosis requires upper endoscopy (EGD) with biopsy of the gastric mucosa, allowing histologic classification under systems such as the updated Sydney classification. Testing for Helicobacter pylori is central and can be performed non-invasively (stool antigen test, urea breath test) or on biopsy specimens (histology, rapid urease test, culture). When autoimmune gastritis is suspected, serum gastrin (typically elevated), parietal-cell and intrinsic-factor antibodies, and vitamin B12 levels guide the diagnosis.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The leading risk factors are H. pylori infection and chronic NSAID/aspirin use. Additional contributors include heavy alcohol consumption, older age, autoimmune predisposition, and severe physiologic stress (as in critically ill or burn patients, who develop &quot;stress gastritis&quot;). Bile reflux and certain chronic acid-suppression scenarios can also contribute to specific subtypes.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is directed at the cause. Confirmed H. pylori infection is treated with eradication regimens that combine acid suppression with two or more antibiotics, after which cure should be confirmed. Removing offending agents — discontinuing NSAIDs and alcohol — is essential. Symptomatic and mucosal healing is supported by proton pump inhibitors or H2-receptor antagonists. In autoimmune atrophic gastritis, lifelong vitamin B12 (and often iron) repletion is required, and endoscopic surveillance is advised when atrophy or intestinal metaplasia is documented because of cancer risk.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Untreated or severe gastritis can progress to peptic ulcer disease and cause acute upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Chronic H. pylori-associated gastritis can advance through atrophy and intestinal metaplasia toward gastric adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma. Autoimmune atrophic gastritis leads to pernicious anemia.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Acute gastritis from a removable cause typically resolves once the trigger is addressed. The prognosis of chronic gastritis depends on its cause and stage: successful H. pylori eradication halts progression and reduces cancer risk, whereas established atrophy and intestinal metaplasia confer a persistent, though modest, elevation in gastric cancer risk that warrants surveillance.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Gastritis is among the most common gastrointestinal conditions worldwide, largely because H. pylori remains highly prevalent — historically infecting roughly half of the global population, with higher rates in lower-income regions. NSAID-related gastritis is common in older adults and those with chronic pain or cardiovascular indications for antiplatelet therapy.</p>
