<h1>Human Papillomavirus</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a small, non-enveloped, double-stranded circular DNA virus (~8 kb genome) of the family <em>Papillomaviridae</em>. More than 200 genotypes are recognized, conventionally split into cutaneous and mucosal tropisms and into low-risk (e.g. HPV-6, HPV-11, causing genital warts) and high-risk oncogenic types (HPV-16, -18, -31, -33, -45, -52, -58). High-risk HPV is the established necessary cause of virtually all invasive cervical cancer and contributes to anal, vulvar, vaginal, penile, and oropharyngeal cancers.</p>

<h2>Ecology &amp; Transmission</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Body Sites:</strong> stratified squamous epithelium of the cervix, anus, oropharynx, and skin</li>
<li><strong>Transmission:</strong> primarily sexual/skin-to-skin contact; most infections are transient and cleared within 1-2 years</li>
<li><strong>Genome:</strong> ~8 kb dsDNA encoding early (E1, E2, E4-E7) and late (L1, L2 capsid) proteins</li>
</ul>
<h2>Pathogenesis &amp; Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Persistent infection with a high-risk genotype is required for malignant transformation. The viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 drive carcinogenesis: E6 targets p53 for degradation and E7 inactivates the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), abrogating cell-cycle checkpoints and promoting genomic instability. HPV-16 and HPV-18 together account for roughly 70% of cervical cancers. The disease burden is highly preventable through prophylactic L1 virus-like-particle vaccines and cervical screening (de Martel et al. 2017; de Sanjosé et al. 2018).</p>
