<h1>Triglycerides</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Triglycerides are esters formed from one glycerol molecule and three fatty acid chains, and they constitute the body's principal energy-storage lipid. In the bloodstream they are carried within triglyceride-rich lipoproteins — chylomicrons (assembled in the intestine from dietary fat) and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL, secreted by the liver). The serum triglyceride concentration measured on a standard lipid panel therefore integrates dietary intake, hepatic de novo lipogenesis, and the rate at which lipoprotein lipase hydrolyzes circulating triglycerides for uptake into muscle and adipose tissue.</p>
<p>Triglyceride metabolism is tightly coupled to carbohydrate and insulin physiology. Insulin normally suppresses adipose lipolysis and activates lipoprotein lipase; in insulin-resistant states this regulation fails, the liver overproduces VLDL, and triglyceride clearance slows, producing the elevated fasting triglycerides that are a defining feature of metabolic syndrome. Excess carbohydrate and alcohol intake drive hepatic triglyceride synthesis, while hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, poorly controlled diabetes, and certain genetic disorders (e.g., familial hypertriglyceridemia, lipoprotein lipase deficiency) can cause marked elevations.</p>
<p>Clinically, triglycerides are measured to assess cardiovascular risk, to screen for and monitor metabolic syndrome and diabetes, and to identify patients at risk of acute pancreatitis when values are very high (≥500 mg/dL). Because postprandial lipemia can raise values substantially, fasting measurement (9–12 hours) is preferred for diagnosis, although non-fasting triglycerides are increasingly used for routine cardiovascular risk screening. Triglycerides are interpreted alongside LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and apolipoprotein B for a complete atherogenic-lipid picture.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Values are for fasting specimens (9–12 hours). Non-fasting triglycerides above ~175 mg/dL are considered elevated for cardiovascular risk screening.</li>
<li>Triglycerides are usually reported in mg/dL in the US; multiply by 0.0113 to convert to mmol/L (SI units).</li>
<li>When triglycerides exceed ~400 mg/dL the Friedewald equation for calculated LDL becomes inaccurate, and a direct LDL or apolipoprotein B measurement is preferred.</li>
<li>Recent fat intake, alcohol, acute illness, and pregnancy can transiently raise values; repeat testing after a stable state is recommended before treatment decisions.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Triglycerides:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>High intake of refined carbohydrates and sugars (drives hepatic de novo lipogenesis)</li>
<li>Alcohol consumption (stimulates VLDL production)</li>
<li>Insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (reduced clearance, increased VLDL output)</li>
<li>Obesity and excess visceral adiposity</li>
<li>Hypothyroidism (reduced lipoprotein lipase activity)</li>
<li>Nephrotic syndrome and chronic kidney disease</li>
<li>Medications such as corticosteroids, estrogens, beta-blockers, and retinoids</li>
<li>Genetic disorders (familial hypertriglyceridemia, lipoprotein lipase deficiency)</li>
<li>Recent high-fat meal (postprandial lipemia)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Triglycerides:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Weight loss and reduced caloric intake</li>
<li>Aerobic and resistance exercise (enhanced lipoprotein lipase activity)</li>
<li>Reduced carbohydrate, sugar, and alcohol intake</li>
<li>Omega-3 fatty acid supplementation (reduced hepatic VLDL secretion)</li>
<li>Treatment of underlying hypothyroidism or diabetes</li>
<li>Fibrates, niacin, and high-dose prescription omega-3 therapy</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular Disease</strong> — triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and their remnants are independently atherogenic; elevated triglycerides predict increased risk of myocardial infarction and all-cause mortality even when LDL is controlled.</li>
<li><strong>Metabolic Syndrome</strong> — high triglycerides combined with low HDL is a core diagnostic criterion and a hallmark of insulin resistance.</li>
<li><strong>Insulin Resistance / Type 2 Diabetes</strong> — elevated fasting triglycerides frequently precede overt hyperglycemia and track with worsening glucose control.</li>
<li><strong>Pancreatitis</strong> — very high triglycerides (≥500 mg/dL, especially &gt;1000 mg/dL) are a recognized cause of acute pancreatitis and require urgent lowering.</li>
<li><strong>Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD)</strong> — hepatic triglyceride accumulation accompanies systemic hypertriglyceridemia.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Very low values (&lt;50 mg/dL) are generally favorable but may occasionally reflect malnutrition, malabsorption, or hyperthyroidism.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Cardiovascular risk stratification as part of the standard lipid panel</li>
<li>Screening and follow-up of metabolic syndrome and diabetes</li>
<li>Assessing response to diet, exercise, fibrates, niacin, or omega-3 therapy</li>
<li>Identifying patients at risk of triglyceride-induced pancreatitis</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Reduce refined carbohydrates and added sugars</strong> — limiting fructose and high-glycemic foods directly lowers hepatic triglyceride synthesis and is often the single most effective dietary lever.</li>
<li><strong>Limit or eliminate alcohol</strong> — alcohol potently stimulates VLDL production; abstinence can dramatically reduce very high triglycerides.</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> — 2–4 g/day of combined EPA/DHA (or prescription icosapent ethyl) reduces triglycerides by roughly 20–30%, with larger reductions at higher baseline values.</li>
<li><strong>Achieve and maintain a healthy weight</strong> — modest weight loss (5–10% of body weight) substantially lowers triglycerides.</li>
<li><strong>Regular aerobic and resistance exercise</strong> — enhances lipoprotein lipase activity and triglyceride clearance.</li>
<li><strong>Berberine (500 mg 2–3×/day)</strong> — improves triglycerides and other metabolic-syndrome components in adults with insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes.</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying conditions</strong> — optimizing thyroid function and glycemic control corrects secondary hypertriglyceridemia.</li>
</ol>
