<h1>Drug-Induced Liver Injury</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Drug-Induced Liver Injury (DILI) is hepatic damage resulting from exposure to medications, herbal and dietary supplements, or other xenobiotics. It is one of the most common causes of acute liver failure in Western countries and the single most frequent reason that drugs are withdrawn from the market or receive boxed warnings. The clinical spectrum is broad, ranging from incidental, asymptomatic elevations of liver enzymes to fulminant hepatic failure requiring transplantation.</p>
<p>DILI is conventionally divided into two mechanistic categories. <strong>Intrinsic (dose-dependent) DILI</strong> is predictable and reproducible, with acetaminophen (paracetamol) overdose being the prototypical example. <strong>Idiosyncratic DILI</strong> is rare, largely dose-independent, and arises from a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility (notably HLA haplotypes), adaptive immune responses, and reactive metabolite formation; it is far harder to predict and typically has a variable latency of days to months after starting the drug.</p>
<p>Because the <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/organ/liver/" class="internal-link">Liver</a> is the principal site of xenobiotic metabolism, it is uniquely exposed to reactive intermediates and is therefore especially vulnerable. Injury is phenotyped using the R-value, calculated from serum <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/biomarker/alanine-transaminase/" class="internal-link">Alanine Transaminase</a> and <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/biomarker/alkaline-phosphatase/" class="internal-link">Alkaline Phosphatase</a>, into hepatocellular, cholestatic, or mixed patterns — a distinction that guides both differential diagnosis and prognosis.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Many patients are asymptomatic and detected only through routine biochemistry showing raised aminotransferases. When symptomatic, presentations include fatigue, nausea, anorexia, right upper quadrant discomfort, jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, and pruritus. Cholestatic injury tends to produce more prominent jaundice and itching, whereas hepatocellular injury may progress rapidly to coagulopathy and encephalopathy in severe cases. Hypersensitivity features (fever, rash, eosinophilia) suggest immune-mediated or DRESS-associated DILI.</p>
<h2>Mechanism and Pathophysiology</h2>
<p>Hepatotoxicity is most often mediated by reactive metabolites generated during phase I metabolism by cytochrome P450 enzymes. In acetaminophen toxicity, the reactive intermediate NAPQI depletes hepatic <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/supplement/glutathione/" class="internal-link">Glutathione</a> and binds covalently to cellular proteins, triggering oxidative stress and centrilobular necrosis. Idiosyncratic injury additionally involves mitochondrial dysfunction, disruption of bile salt export, and activation of innate and adaptive immune pathways, with specific HLA alleles predisposing to drug-specific T-cell responses. Persistent or severe injury can drive a wound-healing response that overlaps with chronic <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/condition/inflammation/" class="internal-link">Inflammation</a> and may culminate in fibrosis.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is one of exclusion and relies on a compatible temporal relationship, a recognised hepatotoxic agent, and the elimination of competing causes such as viral hepatitis, autoimmune disease, and biliary obstruction. Structured causality tools — the Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM) and the newer RECAM — formalise this judgement. The biochemical signature is monitored through the liver enzyme panel, including <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/biomarker/aspartate-aminotransferase/" class="internal-link">Aspartate Aminotransferase</a>, <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/biomarker/bilirubin-total/" class="internal-link">Bilirubin Total</a>, <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/biomarker/ggt/" class="internal-link">Ggt</a>, and <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/labtest/albumin/" class="internal-link">Albumin</a>. <strong>Hy's Law</strong> — hepatocellular injury with ALT &gt;3x the upper limit of normal together with bilirubin &gt;2x ULN in the absence of cholestasis — is a critical prognostic marker associated with substantially higher mortality.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Major risk factors include supratherapeutic or accidental high-dose <a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/drug/acetaminophen/" class="internal-link">Acetaminophen</a>, polypharmacy, chronic alcohol use, older age, female sex for certain agents, and pre-existing chronic liver disease. Genetic predisposition through HLA haplotypes and drug-metabolising enzyme polymorphisms (e.g., NAT2, CYP2E1) modulates idiosyncratic susceptibility. Specific high-risk drug classes include anti-tuberculosis agents, certain antibiotics, antiepileptics, methotrexate, and statins.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>The cornerstone of management is prompt identification and withdrawal of the suspected agent (dechallenge), which alone produces recovery in the majority of cases. <strong>N-acetylcysteine (<a href="/garden/itest05db0ba3/supplement/nac/" class="internal-link">NAC</a>)</strong> is the specific antidote for acetaminophen toxicity, restoring glutathione stores and limiting necrosis, and is most effective when given early. Supportive care, correction of coagulopathy, and serial monitoring of liver function are essential. Corticosteroids may benefit immune-mediated or hypersensitivity-associated DILI. Fulminant cases with encephalopathy and worsening coagulopathy require evaluation for urgent liver transplantation.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Outcomes are generally favourable once the offending drug is stopped, with most patients recovering fully over weeks to months. However, hepatocellular injury meeting Hy's Law carries an approximate 10% risk of death or liver transplantation, and a minority of patients develop chronic injury, cholestasis, or progression toward cirrhosis. Anti-tuberculosis DILI in particular is an important cause of drug-related acute liver failure worldwide.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Idiosyncratic DILI is rare in absolute terms, with population-based incidence estimates around 14–19 cases per 100,000 person-years, but it is a leading cause of acute liver failure in high-income settings. Antimicrobials (especially amoxicillin-clavulanate and anti-tuberculosis drugs) and herbal/dietary supplements account for a large share of cases, and supplement-associated injury has risen markedly in recent years.</p>
