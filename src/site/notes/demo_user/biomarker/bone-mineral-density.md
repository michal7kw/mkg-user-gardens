<h1>Bone Mineral Density</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Bone mineral density (BMD) quantifies the amount of mineral content -- chiefly calcium hydroxyapatite -- per unit area of bone, expressed in grams per square centimeter (g/cm^2). It is the principal measurable surrogate for bone strength and the central biomarker used to <strong>diagnose osteoporosis</strong> and to estimate fracture risk. BMD is most often assessed by <strong>dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA/DXA)</strong>, the clinical gold standard, typically measured at the <strong>lumbar spine</strong> (L1-L4 anteroposterior) and <strong>proximal femur</strong> (femoral neck and total hip). Some protocols also include the distal radius (one-third radius) when hip/spine are uninterpretable.</p>
<p>Results are reported primarily as standardized scores. The <strong>T-score</strong> compares the patient's BMD to the mean of a young healthy adult reference population of the same sex (in standard deviations) and is the basis for diagnostic thresholds in <strong>postmenopausal women and men &gt;= 50 years</strong>. The <strong>Z-score</strong> compares BMD to an age-, sex-, and sometimes ethnicity-matched reference and is preferred in <strong>children, premenopausal women, and younger men</strong>, where a low value (Z-score &lt;= -2.0) prompts a search for secondary causes.</p>
<p>BMD is a strong, continuous predictor of fragility fracture -- each standard-deviation decrease roughly <strong>doubles</strong> fracture risk. It is widely used both to make the diagnosis of osteoporosis (per WHO criteria) and to monitor response to anti-osteoporotic therapy over time (typical monitoring interval is 1-2 years; LSC -- least significant change -- defines the threshold for &quot;real&quot; change).</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (universally accepted; WHO diagnostic criteria; ACR/NOF/IOF guideline-endorsed).</p>

<p><strong>Z-score (children, premenopausal women, men &lt;50):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Z-score &gt;= -2.0: within expected range for age</li>
<li>Z-score &lt;= -2.0: below expected range for age; search for secondary causes</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>WHO T-score criteria apply principally to <strong>postmenopausal women and men &gt;= 50 years</strong></li>
<li><strong>T-score discordance between spine and hip is common</strong> -- use the lower of the two for diagnosis</li>
<li><strong>Lumbar spine BMD can be falsely elevated</strong> in older adults by osteophytes, facet arthropathy, vertebral compression fractures, aortic calcification, and degenerative changes -- hip measurement is often more reliable in elderly</li>
<li><strong>Vertebral compression fractures elevate apparent spine BMD</strong> (less bone per area measurement) -- review images</li>
<li><strong>Serial monitoring interval</strong> typically 1-2 years; LSC (least significant change) ~3-5% depending on site and manufacturer</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting BMD</h2>
<p><strong>Decrease BMD (osteoporosis risk):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Postmenopausal estrogen deficiency</strong> (most common cause in women)</li>
<li><strong>Age-related bone loss</strong> (men and women)</li>
<li><strong>Male hypogonadism</strong> (low testosterone)</li>
<li><strong>Glucocorticoid therapy</strong> (&gt;=5 mg prednisone for &gt;=3 months)</li>
<li><strong>Hyperparathyroidism</strong> (primary or secondary)</li>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong></li>
<li><strong>Cushing syndrome</strong></li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D deficiency</strong></li>
<li><strong>Inadequate calcium intake</strong></li>
<li><strong>Malabsorption</strong> (celiac disease, IBD, gastric bypass)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic kidney disease</strong></li>
<li><strong>Chronic liver disease</strong></li>
<li><strong>Smoking</strong> (especially in women)</li>
<li><strong>Alcohol excess</strong> (&gt;2-3 drinks/day)</li>
<li><strong>Low body weight</strong> (BMI &lt;21)</li>
<li><strong>Immobilization, prolonged bed rest</strong></li>
<li><strong>Anorexia nervosa</strong></li>
<li><strong>Rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory disease</strong></li>
<li><strong>Diabetes mellitus</strong> (especially type 1)</li>
<li><strong>Drugs:</strong> aromatase inhibitors, androgen deprivation therapy, PPIs (long-term), anticonvulsants, heparin, excess thyroid hormone replacement</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Increase BMD:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Weight-bearing exercise</strong> (resistance + impact)</li>
<li><strong>Adequate calcium</strong> (1000-1200 mg/day dietary+supplement)</li>
<li><strong>Adequate vitamin D</strong> (2000-5000 IU D3 daily; target 25-OH-D 30-50 ng/mL)</li>
<li><strong>Antiresorptive therapy</strong> (bisphosphonates, denosumab, raloxifene, estrogen, calcitonin)</li>
<li><strong>Anabolic therapy</strong> (teriparatide, abaloparatide, romosozumab)</li>
<li><strong>Weight gain</strong> (in low-BMI patients)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy / lactation</strong> (variable)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low BMD is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis diagnosis:</strong> per WHO criteria (T-score &lt;= -2.5 at spine, total hip, or femoral neck)</li>
<li><strong>Osteopenia diagnosis:</strong> T-score -1.0 to -2.5; high-risk group for progression to osteoporosis</li>
<li><strong>Fracture risk:</strong> each SD decrease roughly doubles fracture risk; FRAX score incorporates BMD</li>
<li><strong>Secondary causes:</strong> Z-score &lt;= -2.0 in younger patients prompts workup (hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, hypogonadism, malabsorption, etc.)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated or falsely-high BMD:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Lumbar spine osteophytes, facet arthropathy, aortic calcification</strong> -- spurious elevation in elderly; hip more reliable</li>
<li><strong>Vertebral compression fractures</strong> -- elevate apparent density</li>
<li><strong>Sclerosing bone dysplasias</strong> (osteopetrosis)</li>
<li><strong>Fluorosis, renal osteodystrophy</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>BMD is one component of fracture risk</strong> -- clinical risk factors, FRAX, vertebral fracture assessment (VFA) are complementary</li>
<li><strong>T-score discordance between sites is common</strong> -- use the lower value</li>
<li><strong>Serial monitoring requires the same manufacturer and ideally the same machine</strong> for valid LSC</li>
<li><strong>BMD does not capture bone quality</strong> (microarchitecture, turnover) -- trabecular bone score (TBS) and bone turnover markers (P1NP, CTX) are complementary</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Osteoporosis screening (women &gt;= 65, men &gt;= 70, or younger with risk factors per USPSTF)</li>
<li>Osteoporosis diagnosis (per WHO T-score criteria)</li>
<li>Anti-osteoporotic therapy monitoring (q1-2 years)</li>
<li>Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis monitoring</li>
<li>Secondary osteoporosis workup</li>
<li>Vertebral fracture assessment (VFA) performed with DEXA</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>BMD is highly modifiable through lifestyle, nutrition, and pharmacologic interventions:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Weight-bearing exercise</strong> (resistance training 2-3x/week + impact activities like walking, jogging, dancing 30+ min 5x/week) -- the most impactful lifestyle intervention for BMD</li>
<li><strong>Adequate calcium</strong> 1000-1200 mg/day (diet + supplement) -- necessary substrate for bone mineralization</li>
<li><strong>Adequate vitamin D</strong> 2000-5000 IU D3 daily (target 25-OH-D 30-50 ng/mL) -- enhances calcium absorption</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin K2 (MK-7)</strong> 100-180 mcg daily -- modest BMD improvement in postmenopausal women (Knapen 2013)</li>
<li><strong>Antiresorptive therapy:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bisphosphonates:</strong> alendronate 70 mg weekly, zoledronic acid 5 mg IV annually, risedronate 35 mg weekly (first-line for most osteoporosis)</li>
<li><strong>Denosumab</strong> 60 mg SC q6 months (RANKL inhibitor; powerful antiresorptive)</li>
<li><strong>Raloxifene</strong> 60 mg daily (SERE; reduces vertebral fractures and breast cancer risk)</li>
<li><strong>Hormone therapy</strong> (estrogen) for early menopause</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Anabolic therapy (for severe osteoporosis or treatment failure):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Teriparatide</strong> 20 mcg SC daily (PTH 1-34; builds new bone)</li>
<li><strong>Abaloparatide</strong> 80 mcg SC daily (PTH-related peptide analog)</li>
<li><strong>Romosozumab</strong> 210 mg SC monthly (sclerostin inhibitor; dual anabolic + antiresorptive)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Avoid smoking</strong> and <strong>excessive alcohol</strong></li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> bone-toxic medications when possible (chronic glucocorticoids, PPIs, excess thyroid hormone)</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying causes</strong> of secondary osteoporosis (hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, hypogonadism, malabsorption, Cushing's)</li>
<li><strong>Fall prevention</strong> (home safety evaluation, balance exercises, vision correction, reduce sedatives)</li>
<li><strong>Bone turnover marker monitoring</strong> (P1NP, CTX) to confirm therapy response at 3-6 months (earlier than DEXA changes)</li>
</ol>
