<h1>Omega-3 (DHA)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) is a long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid that constitutes approximately 40% of the polyunsaturated fatty acids in the brain and 60% of those in the retina. It is the most abundant omega-3 fatty acid in neural tissue and is critical for brain development during fetal life and infancy, as well as for maintaining cognitive function throughout the lifespan.</p>
<p>DHA is obtained directly from marine sources (fatty fish, algae) or produced endogenously from ALA, though conversion is extremely limited (&lt;5%). During pregnancy and lactation, maternal DHA requirements increase substantially to support fetal and neonatal brain and retinal development. Adequate DHA status during the third trimester is associated with improved infant visual acuity, cognitive development, and reduced risk of preterm birth.</p>
<p>Beyond neurodevelopment, DHA exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects through the generation of specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) including resolvins, protectins (particularly neuroprotectin D1), and maresins. These lipid mediators actively resolve inflammation rather than merely suppressing it. DHA also modulates cell membrane fluidity, gene expression through PPAR and RXR nuclear receptor activation, and synaptic plasticity. The triglyceride form of supplemental DHA shows approximately 70% higher bioavailability than the ethyl ester form, particularly when consumed with a fat-containing meal.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Provides structural integrity to neuronal cell membranes and synapses</li>
<li>Constitutes a major structural component of retinal photoreceptor cells</li>
<li>Generates specialized pro-resolving mediators (resolvins, protectins, maresins)</li>
<li>Supports fetal and infant brain development during pregnancy and lactation</li>
<li>Modulates gene expression via nuclear receptor activation (PPARs, RXR)</li>
<li>Maintains synaptic plasticity and neurotransmitter signaling</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring, anchovies)</li>
<li>Algae oil (vegan DHA source)</li>
<li>Fish oil supplements (triglyceride form preferred)</li>
<li>Krill oil</li>
<li>Fortified eggs (from hens fed DHA-rich diets)</li>
<li>Cod liver oil</li>
<li>Caviar and roe</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>DHA deficiency is associated with cognitive decline, poor memory and learning difficulties, dry eyes, increased risk of depression and mood disorders, and impaired visual function. In infants, insufficient DHA during development is linked to reduced visual acuity and slower cognitive milestones. Low DHA status in the elderly correlates with increased risk of Alzheimer's disease and dementia.</p>
<p>Excess DHA at very high doses (&gt;3 g/day combined EPA+DHA) may increase bleeding risk, cause fishy aftertaste and belching, and lead to gastrointestinal upset (nausea, diarrhea). High-dose fish oil may also transiently raise LDL cholesterol in some individuals. Very high supplementation may suppress immune surveillance, though clinically relevant immunosuppression is rare at typical doses.</p>
