<h1>Thalassemia</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Thalassemia is a group of inherited disorders of hemoglobin in which the synthesis of one of the globin chains is reduced or absent, producing an imbalance between alpha and beta chains. The excess unpaired chains precipitate within red-cell precursors, causing their premature destruction in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis) and hemolysis in the periphery. The result is a microcytic, hypochromic anemia whose severity depends on which and how many globin genes are affected.</p>
<p>Alpha-thalassemia arises from deletion or mutation of the alpha-globin genes (HBA1/HBA2); beta-thalassemia results from mutations in the beta-globin gene (HBB). Clinically the spectrum runs from asymptomatic carrier states (thalassemia minor/trait), through thalassemia intermedia, to severe transfusion-dependent thalassemia major. Thalassemia is one of the most common monogenic diseases globally, concentrated in populations from malaria-endemic regions where carriers have relative protection against malaria.</p>
<h2>Diagnosis</h2>
<p>A microcytic anemia (low MCV) with a normal or elevated red-cell count and a normal or high ferritin distinguishes thalassemia trait from iron-deficiency anemia, the principal differential. Hemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC quantifies hemoglobin fractions (an elevated HbA2 supports beta-thalassemia trait), and genetic testing confirms the molecular defect. Importantly, the anemia of thalassemia is not iron-responsive, and inappropriate iron supplementation can contribute to iron overload.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Mild forms require no treatment beyond genetic counseling and avoidance of unnecessary iron. Severe forms are managed with regular transfusion, which in turn necessitates iron-chelation therapy to prevent transfusional iron overload affecting the heart, liver, and endocrine organs. Allogeneic stem-cell transplantation and emerging gene therapies can be curative in selected patients.</p>
