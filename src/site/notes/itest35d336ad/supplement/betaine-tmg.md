<h1>Betaine (TMG)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Betaine, also called trimethylglycine (TMG), is a naturally occurring compound found in foods such as beets, spinach, and whole grains. It plays two physiological roles: as a methyl donor in one-carbon metabolism and as an organic osmolyte protecting cells from osmotic stress. As a supplement, its best-established use is lowering plasma homocysteine, an amino acid whose elevation is associated with cardiovascular and neurological risk.</p>
<p>Betaine remethylates homocysteine to methionine through the enzyme betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase (BHMT), an alternative to the folate/vitamin B12-dependent methionine synthase pathway. This makes it particularly relevant when the folate pathway is constrained—for example in carriers of reduced-activity MTHFR variants (such as C677T)—because betaine can lower homocysteine via a parallel route. Randomized controlled trials in healthy volunteers show clear, dose-dependent reductions in fasting and post-methionine-load homocysteine. A notable caveat is that higher doses can modestly raise LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, so lipid monitoring is prudent.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> — Consistent randomized trials demonstrate dose-dependent homocysteine lowering; the lipid trade-off at higher doses is also well documented.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p><strong>1. Homocysteine remethylation (primary):</strong> Betaine donates a methyl group to homocysteine in a reaction catalyzed by BHMT (primarily in the liver and kidney), forming methionine and dimethylglycine. This lowers circulating homocysteine independently of folate and vitamin B12.</p>
<p><strong>2. Methylation support:</strong> By regenerating methionine, betaine helps maintain levels of S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe), the universal methyl donor for DNA, protein, and neurotransmitter methylation. This is the mechanistic basis for its relevance in MTHFR-related impaired methylation.</p>
<p><strong>3. Osmolyte function:</strong> Betaine accumulates intracellularly as a compatible osmolyte, stabilizing protein structure and protecting cells (especially renal medulla and liver) against osmotic and oxidative stress.</p>
<h2>Dosage</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Homocysteine lowering:</strong> 0.5-3 g/day (maintenance typically ~1-1.5 g/day), often divided</li>
<li><strong>Homocystinuria (inborn errors):</strong> up to 6 g/day under medical supervision</li>
<li><strong>Timing:</strong> With water, with or without meals</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring:</strong> Check homocysteine and a lipid panel, especially at doses above ~3-4 g/day, since LDL and triglycerides may rise</li>
<li><strong>Synergy:</strong> Often combined with folate, vitamin B12, and vitamin B6, which address complementary steps of homocysteine metabolism</li>
</ul>
