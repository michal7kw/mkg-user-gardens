<h1>Cortisol</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cortisol is the body's primary glucocorticoid, a steroid hormone synthesized from cholesterol in the zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex. Its release is governed by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis: hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) stimulates pituitary adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which in turn drives adrenal cortisol secretion, with cortisol exerting negative feedback at both the hypothalamus and pituitary. As a biomarker, cortisol measured in blood, saliva, or urine provides a direct window into HPA-axis function and the body's physiological stress response.</p>
<p>Cortisol has a pronounced circadian rhythm, peaking in the early morning roughly 30-45 minutes after waking (the cortisol awakening response) and falling to a nadir around midnight. This diurnal pattern is as diagnostically important as any single value, which is why timing of the sample is critical. Cortisol acts on virtually every tissue, raising blood glucose through gluconeogenesis, mobilizing fatty acids and amino acids, modulating immune and inflammatory responses, supporting blood-pressure maintenance, and influencing mood, memory, and sleep. Roughly 90% of circulating cortisol is bound to cortisol-binding globulin (CBG) and albumin; only the free fraction is biologically active, which is why salivary cortisol (which reflects free hormone) and 24-hour urinary free cortisol are valuable complements to total serum cortisol.</p>
<p>Clinically, cortisol is measured to investigate suspected Cushing syndrome (chronic excess), adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease, deficiency), pituitary disorders, and stress-related HPA dysregulation. Because total serum levels are affected by CBG (which rises with estrogen and pregnancy and falls in critical illness), interpretation often requires confirmatory or dynamic testing such as the overnight dexamethasone suppression test, late-night salivary cortisol, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, or the ACTH stimulation test rather than reliance on a single measurement.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Serum values are method-dependent; immunoassay and LC-MS/MS reference intervals differ, so always interpret against the issuing laboratory's range.</li>
<li>Total serum cortisol rises with elevated CBG (estrogen therapy, oral contraceptives, pregnancy) and falls when CBG or albumin is low (critical illness, nephrotic syndrome), potentially misleading without free-cortisol measures.</li>
<li>A single random cortisol is rarely diagnostic; the diurnal pattern and dynamic testing carry the clinical weight.</li>
<li>No universally standardized cutoff exists across all assays for salivary and urinary measurements; reference intervals are platform-specific.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Cortisol:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute physical or psychological stress (HPA-axis activation)</li>
<li>Chronic stress and depression (sustained HPA drive)</li>
<li>Cushing syndrome / pituitary or ectopic ACTH-secreting tumors (autonomous overproduction)</li>
<li>Exogenous corticosteroids and some assay cross-reactivity (measured artifact)</li>
<li>Pregnancy and estrogen/oral-contraceptive use (raised cortisol-binding globulin elevates total cortisol)</li>
<li>Acute illness, surgery, trauma, hypoglycemia, and intense exercise (stress response)</li>
<li>Sleep deprivation and disrupted circadian rhythm</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Cortisol:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Primary adrenal insufficiency / Addison's disease (adrenal cortex failure)</li>
<li>Secondary adrenal insufficiency from hypopituitarism (low ACTH drive)</li>
<li>Chronic exogenous glucocorticoid use causing HPA-axis suppression</li>
<li>Low cortisol-binding globulin states (critical illness, nephrotic syndrome) lowering total cortisol</li>
<li>Adaptogenic interventions and effective stress reduction (modest lowering of elevated levels)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cushings Syndrome</strong> -- chronic hypercortisolism causing central obesity, hypertension, glucose intolerance, skin thinning, and proximal myopathy; confirmed with low-dose dexamethasone suppression, late-night salivary cortisol, or 24-hour urinary free cortisol.</li>
<li><strong>Pituitary Disorder</strong> -- ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma (Cushing disease) is the most common endogenous cause of hypercortisolism.</li>
<li><strong>Depression</strong> -- a subset of patients show HPA-axis hyperactivity with elevated cortisol and blunted dexamethasone suppression.</li>
<li><strong>Hypertension</strong> and <strong>Type 2 Diabetes</strong> -- chronic cortisol excess contributes to elevated blood pressure and insulin resistance.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Addisons Disease</strong> -- autoimmune or infiltrative destruction of the adrenal cortex producing low cortisol with compensatory high ACTH; confirmed by an inadequate response to the ACTH (cosyntropin) stimulation test.</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal Disease</strong> -- structural adrenal damage or hemorrhage impairing cortisol output, carrying risk of life-threatening adrenal crisis.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Diagnosis and follow-up of Cushing syndrome and adrenal insufficiency</li>
<li>Assessment of HPA-axis suppression after prolonged corticosteroid therapy</li>
<li>Investigation of stress-related disorders, fatigue, and sleep disturbance via diurnal salivary profiling</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>These strategies aim to normalize an over-active stress response (lowering chronically elevated cortisol); true endocrine disease requires medical diagnosis and treatment.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Ashwagandha</strong> -- standardized root extract (300 mg twice daily of a high-concentration extract) has reduced serum cortisol in chronically stressed adults in randomized controlled trials (PMID:23439798).</li>
<li><strong>Phosphatidylserine</strong> -- supplementation (around 400-600 mg/day, or up to 800 mg in stress protocols) blunts the cortisol response to physical and psychological stress (PMID:1325348, PMID:2170852).</li>
<li><strong>Sleep optimization</strong> -- consistent sleep timing and adequate duration restore the normal diurnal cortisol rhythm; chronic sleep loss elevates evening cortisol.</li>
<li><strong>Mind-body practices</strong> -- meditation, mindfulness, and breathwork lower perceived stress and HPA-axis output.</li>
<li><strong>Regular moderate exercise</strong> -- improves stress resilience, though acute intense exercise transiently raises cortisol.</li>
<li><strong>Caffeine and alcohol moderation</strong> -- both can elevate cortisol; limiting late-day intake supports the evening nadir.</li>
</ol>
