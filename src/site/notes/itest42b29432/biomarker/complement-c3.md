<h1>Complement C3</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Complement component C3 is the most abundant complement protein in human serum (~1.3 mg/mL) and the central convergence point of all three complement activation pathways: classical (immune complex-triggered), lectin (mannose-binding lectin-triggered), and alternative (spontaneous hydrolysis-triggered). When any of these pathways is activated, the C3 convertase enzyme cleaves C3 into C3a (a small anaphylatoxin) and C3b (a large fragment that opsonizes pathogens and forms the C5 convertase). Because C3 lies downstream of all three pathways, it represents the common effector of complement activation and is consumed in any active immune-complex or alternative-pathway disease.</p>
<p>Clinically, low serum C3 is the most useful single marker of complement consumption. The principal clinical uses fall into three categories: (1) <strong>SLE disease activity monitoring</strong> -- immune complexes in SLE activate the classical pathway, consuming C3 and C4. Falling C3 levels (with falling C4 and rising anti-dsDNA) precede clinical flares, particularly lupus nephritis, by weeks to months. Serial monitoring every 3-6 months is standard in active SLE; (2) <strong>Glomerulonephritis workup</strong> -- low C3 is characteristic of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), C3 glomerulopathy, and lupus nephritis. C3 is also low in cryoglobulinemia; (3) <strong>Hereditary angioedema and inherited complement deficiency workup</strong> -- C3 is typically normal in HAE (low C4 only), but low in C3 deficiency (recurrent pyogenic infections).</p>
<p>The <strong>C3:C4 pattern</strong> provides diagnostic discrimination:</p>
<ul>
<li>Low C3 + low C4: classical pathway activation (SLE, cryoglobulinemia, post-strep GN, MPGN type I)</li>
<li>Low C3 + normal C4: alternative pathway activation (C3 glomerulopathy, MPGN type III, factor H deficiency)</li>
<li>Normal C3 + low C4: early classical activation or hereditary angioedema (C1-INH deficiency)</li>
<li>Normal C3 + normal C4: complement consumption less likely</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (well-validated; ACR/EULAR classification criteria component; universal clinical use in SLE).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range is assay-specific (nephelometry, turbidimetry)</li>
<li>C3 is an acute-phase reactant -- can be normal or elevated despite consumption if inflammation is concurrent (false reassurance)</li>
<li>Genetic C3 deficiency is rare (~30 families reported); recurrent pyogenic infections</li>
<li>Pregnancy: C3 rises physiologically; interpret in pregnancy-specific reference range</li>
<li>Serial changes are more informative than absolute values</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Decrease C3 (complement consumption):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Active SLE (immune complex classical pathway activation)</li>
<li>Lupus nephritis (proliferative Class III/IV)</li>
<li>Cryoglobulinemia (mixed types II/III)</li>
<li>Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (low for 6-8 weeks)</li>
<li>Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN)</li>
<li>C3 glomerulopathy (alternative pathway dysregulation)</li>
<li>Subacute bacterial endocarditis</li>
<li>Shunt nephritis</li>
<li>Serum sickness</li>
<li>Severe sepsis (massive complement consumption)</li>
<li>Hereditary C3 deficiency (rare; recurrent pyogenic infections)</li>
<li>Factor H or factor I deficiency (alternative pathway dysregulation)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Increase C3 (acute-phase response):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute infection (bacterial, viral)</li>
<li>Acute inflammation (RA, IBD, vasculitis)</li>
<li>Acute myocardial infarction</li>
<li>Major surgery, trauma</li>
<li>Pregnancy</li>
<li>Malignancy (some)</li>
<li>Obesity, metabolic syndrome</li>
<li>Corticosteroid therapy (acute)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low C3 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Active SLE / lupus nephritis:</strong> the principal clinical use; falling C3 with falling C4 precedes clinical flares by weeks to months; persistent low C3 in lupus nephritis predicts worse renal outcomes</li>
<li><strong>Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis:</strong> low C3 (with low CH50, normal C4) for 6-8 weeks after infection; persistent low C3 beyond 8 weeks suggests alternative diagnosis</li>
<li><strong>Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis:</strong> persistently low C3; differentiates from other glomerulonephritides</li>
<li><strong>C3 glomerulopathy:</strong> very low C3; alternative pathway dysregulation</li>
<li><strong>Cryoglobulinemia (mixed):</strong> low C3 with very low C4</li>
<li><strong>Sepsis / multi-organ failure:</strong> massive complement consumption</li>
<li><strong>Hereditary C3 deficiency:</strong> very rare; recurrent pyogenic infections</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated C3 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute-phase response (infection, inflammation, MI)</li>
<li>Pregnancy</li>
<li>Malignancy</li>
<li>Metabolic syndrome (chronic low-grade inflammation)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acute-phase reactant</strong> -- concurrent inflammation can normalize or elevate C3 despite ongoing consumption (false reassurance)</li>
<li><strong>Reference range varies by lab</strong> -- interpret against lab-specific cutoffs</li>
<li><strong>Genetic variants</strong> influence baseline C3 levels</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>SLE diagnosis and disease activity monitoring (alongside C4, anti-dsDNA, CH50)</li>
<li>Lupus nephritis activity (q1-3 months)</li>
<li>Glomerulonephritis workup (alongside C4, CH50, ASO titer)</li>
<li>Hereditary angioedema screening (C4 low; C3 typically normal)</li>
<li>C3 deficiency workup in recurrent infections</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>C3 levels reflect underlying immune activation; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>SLE:</strong> hydroxychloroquine 200-400 mg daily (the anchor of SLE therapy; reduces flares and complement consumption); corticosteroids for acute flares; immunosuppressives (mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, azathioprine) for severe disease; biologics (belimumab, anifrolumab, rituximab, obinutuzumab)</li>
<li><strong>Lupus nephritis:</strong> mycophenolate mofetil + corticosteroids (first-line); voclosporin (FDA-approved 2021); rituximab or obinutuzumab for refractory disease</li>
<li><strong>Post-strep GN:</strong> supportive care; treat streptococcal infection; usually self-resolves</li>
<li><strong>MPGN / C3 glomerulopathy:</strong> nephrology referral; eculizumab (anti-C5 monoclonal) considered in some cases</li>
<li><strong>Cryoglobulinemia:</strong> treat underlying cause (HCV with direct-acting antivirals; autoimmune with immunosuppression); rituximab for severe disease</li>
<li><strong>C3 deficiency:</strong> aggressive treatment of infections; consider prophylactic antibiotics; vaccination</li>
<li><strong>Hereditary angioedema (normal C3, low C4):</strong> C1-INH replacement, icatibant, lanadelumab</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying infection</strong> (sepsis) -- restores complement through resolution</li>
</ol>
