<h1>Lp(a)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Lipoprotein(a), abbreviated Lp(a) and pronounced &quot;L-P-little-a&quot;, is a genetically-determined LDL-like particle consisting of an LDL particle covalently bound to apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)]. Apo(a) is structurally homologous to plasminogen and contains multiple &quot;kringle&quot; domains (kringle IV type 2 repeats, varying in number from 3 to &gt;40 copies). The LPA gene kringle IV type 2 repeat polymorphism is the principal genetic determinant of Lp(a) levels: smaller apo(a) isoforms (fewer kringle repeats) translate to higher Lp(a) levels. Approximately 70-90% of inter-individual Lp(a) variation is genetically determined, and levels are largely fixed throughout life (concentrations are largely established by age 2-3 and remain stable thereafter).</p>
<p>Elevated Lp(a) &gt;125 nmol/L (&gt;50 mg/dL) is among the most atherogenic lipoprotein abnormalities, conferring ~2-3x increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) independent of LDL-C. Lp(a) is the only biomarker currently endorsed by the 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) dyslipidemia guideline for &quot;risk-enhanced&quot; classification in primary prevention, and the 2018 ACC/AHA cholesterol guideline lists Lp(a) as a &quot;risk-enhancing factor&quot; that should prompt consideration of statin or PCSK9 inhibitor therapy in intermediate-risk patients. Lp(a) also predicts calcific aortic valve stenosis (CAVS) -- a disease for which there is no medical therapy, only surgical valve replacement.</p>
<p>The major clinical challenge with Lp(a) is that levels are largely unresponsive to lifestyle interventions and most lipid-lowering drugs. Statins modestly lower Lp(a) (~10%) or may paradoxically increase it; ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors have variable effects. The only currently-available therapy with meaningful Lp(a)-lowering (~30-40%) is apheresis (reserved for very high-risk patients). However, <strong>antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) and siRNA therapies targeting apo(a) mRNA</strong> -- pelacarsen (TQJ230, Novartis/Akcea/Ionis), olpasiran (Amgen), zerlasiran (Silence Therapeutics), muvalaplin (Eli Lilly) -- are demonstrating 80-95% Lp(a) reduction in phase 2 trials (2022-2024), with the Lp(a) HORIZON cardiovascular outcome trial of pelacarsen expected to report in 2026. If positive, these therapies will transform Lp(a) management.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (universally accepted as a CV risk marker; ASCVD guideline-endorsed; novel therapeutic class in phase 3).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Use nmol/L (mass-independent molar units) when possible; mg/dL conversion is method-dependent</li>
<li>Single measurement is sufficient (levels are stable over decades) -- no need for serial monitoring</li>
<li>90th percentile cutoff (&gt;125 nmol/L) identifies ~20% of the global population with elevated Lp(a)</li>
<li>African-descent populations have 2-3x higher median Lp(a) than European/Asian populations</li>
<li>Most assays are not yet standardized across labs (IFCC reference material is emerging)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Lp(a):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Genetics (LPA kringle IV type 2 repeat polymorphism; &gt;70% of variance)</li>
<li>African ancestry (population-level higher median)</li>
<li>Post-menopausal status (modest increase)</li>
<li>Nephrotic syndrome, chronic kidney disease (impaired clearance)</li>
<li>Hypothyroidism (modest)</li>
<li>Pregnancy (transient)</li>
<li>Growth hormone therapy</li>
<li>Inflammation (variable)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Lp(a):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Niacin (~30% reduction; large doses)</li>
<li>PCSK9 inhibitors (alirocumab, evolocumab -- variable, 20-30%)</li>
<li>LDL apheresis (~50-60% acutely; serial therapy)</li>
<li>Estrogen therapy / oral contraceptives (~10-15%)</li>
<li>ASO/siRNA therapies (pelacarsen, olpasiran, muvalaplin -- investigational, 80-95% reduction)</li>
<li>Hyperthyroidism (modest reduction)</li>
<li>Cirrhosis (impaired synthesis)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Factors that DO NOT substantially affect Lp(a):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Diet (low-fat, low-cholesterol, Mediterranean, etc.)</li>
<li>Statin therapy (variable, often paradoxically raises Lp(a))</li>
<li>Ezetimibe</li>
<li>Bempedoic acid</li>
<li>Exercise</li>
<li>Weight loss</li>
<li>Omega-3 fatty acids</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated Lp(a) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD):</strong> ~2-3x risk at &gt;125 nmol/L; risk increases continuously above ~50 mg/dL without lower threshold. Independent of LDL-C.</li>
<li><strong>Calcific aortic valve stenosis (CAVS):</strong> Lp(a)-derived oxidized phospholipids deposit in aortic valve leaflets, accelerating calcification; Lp(a) is a CAVS risk marker</li>
<li><strong>Ischemic stroke:</strong> ~2x risk for large-artery atherosclerotic stroke</li>
<li><strong>Peripheral arterial disease:</strong> elevated Lp(a) predicts PAD</li>
<li><strong>Thrombotic events:</strong> apo(a) competes with plasminogen, impairing fibrinolysis</li>
<li><strong>Progression in established ASCVD:</strong> patients with prior MI and elevated Lp(a) have higher recurrence risk</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Levels largely unresponsive to lifestyle</strong> -- unlike most biomarkers, can be discouraging for patients to learn</li>
<li><strong>Most current lipid-lowering drugs do not lower Lp(a)</strong> effectively</li>
<li><strong>Not yet in primary prevention screening</strong> universally (ESC 2022 recommends once-in-lifetime testing; ACC/AHA 2018 lists as risk-enhancing factor)</li>
<li><strong>Insurance coverage variable</strong> for the test itself</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Once-in-lifetime screen in adulthood (ESC 2022 recommendation; emerging standard of care)</li>
<li>Risk stratification in intermediate-risk ASCVD (ACC/AHA &quot;risk-enhancing factor&quot;)</li>
<li>Family history of premature ASCVD with normal LDL-C</li>
<li>Recurrent ASCVD events despite optimized statin therapy</li>
<li>Pre-treatment baseline for novel ASO/siRNA therapies (research/emerging)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>Lp(a) is largely non-modifiable through lifestyle. Management focuses on aggressive control of modifiable CV risk factors and emerging targeted therapies:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Aggressive LDL-C lowering:</strong> high-intensity statin + ezetimibe ± PCSK9 inhibitor to achieve LDL-C &lt;55 mg/dL (ESC) or &lt;70 mg/dL (ACC/AHA) -- the rationale is to minimize the modifiable risk component when Lp(a) is non-modifiable</li>
<li><strong>PCSK9 inhibitor (alirocumab, evolocumab):</strong> these do modestly lower Lp(a) (~20-30%) and are reasonable choices when elevated Lp(a) coexists with elevated LDL-C</li>
<li><strong>Niacin:</strong> lowers Lp(a) ~30% at doses 1-3 g/day; largely abandoned due to side effects (flushing, hyperglycemia) and lack of CV outcome benefit in AIM-HIGH and HPS2-THRIVE. Used selectively.</li>
<li><strong>LDL apheresis:</strong> ~50-60% acute reduction; reserved for very high-risk patients with Lp(a) &gt;60 mg/dL and progressive ASCVD despite maximal therapy (German criteria; insurance coverage varies)</li>
<li><strong>ASO/siRNA therapy (investigational, 2024-2026):</strong> pelacarsen (subcutaneous monthly), olpasiran (q3 months), muvalaplin (oral daily) -- all reduce Lp(a) by 80-95%; awaiting CV outcome trials (HORIZON, OCEAN)</li>
<li><strong>Aspirin 81-100 mg daily:</strong> for primary prevention in elevated Lp(a) patients with intermediate ASCVD risk (per ASCVD primary prevention guidelines)</li>
<li><strong>Aggressive control of all other risk factors:</strong> blood pressure (&lt;130/80), glycemic control (HbA1c &lt;7%), smoking cessation, weight management, exercise</li>
</ol>
