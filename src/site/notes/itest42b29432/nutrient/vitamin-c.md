<h1>Vitamin C</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin and potent antioxidant that humans cannot synthesize due to a mutation in the L-gulonolactone oxidase (GULO) gene. This makes it an essential dietary nutrient. Vitamin C serves as an electron donor for at least eight enzymatic reactions, most notably the prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases required for collagen biosynthesis -- which is why severe deficiency (scurvy) causes connective tissue breakdown.</p>
<p>As an antioxidant, vitamin C directly scavenges reactive oxygen species (superoxide, hydroxyl radical, hypochlorous acid) and regenerates oxidized vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol radical back to alpha-tocopherol), creating a synergistic antioxidant network. In immune function, vitamin C accumulates in neutrophils and lymphocytes at concentrations 10-100 times higher than plasma levels, supporting chemotaxis, phagocytosis, reactive oxygen species generation for pathogen killing, and lymphocyte proliferation.</p>
<p>Vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption by reducing ferric iron (Fe3+) to the more soluble ferrous form (Fe2+) in the gastrointestinal tract, making it particularly important for individuals relying on plant-based iron sources. Bioavailability is dose-dependent: nearly 100% at doses up to 200 mg, declining to approximately 33% at 1,250 mg. This is why divided doses throughout the day are more effective than single large doses. Liposomal vitamin C formulations may improve absorption at higher doses by bypassing saturable intestinal transporters.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Essential cofactor for collagen synthesis (skin, bone, cartilage, blood vessels)</li>
<li>Scavenges free radicals and regenerates vitamin E as a network antioxidant</li>
<li>Enhances neutrophil and lymphocyte function for immune defense</li>
<li>Reduces ferric iron to ferrous iron, dramatically improving non-heme iron absorption</li>
<li>Cofactor for dopamine beta-hydroxylase (norepinephrine synthesis)</li>
<li>Supports carnitine biosynthesis for fatty acid transport into mitochondria</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Citrus fruits (oranges, grapefruit, lemons)</li>
<li>Bell peppers (especially red and yellow)</li>
<li>Strawberries and kiwifruit</li>
<li>Broccoli and Brussels sprouts</li>
<li>Tomatoes</li>
<li>Guava and papaya</li>
<li>Kale and spinach</li>
<li>Acerola cherry (one of the richest sources)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Scurvy, the classic vitamin C deficiency disease, develops after 1-3 months of intake below 10 mg/day and presents with bleeding gums, petechiae, poor wound healing, joint pain, corkscrew hairs, and perifollicular hemorrhages. Marginal deficiency (more common) causes fatigue, malaise, impaired immunity, and depression. Smokers require 35 mg/day more vitamin C than non-smokers due to increased oxidative stress.</p>
<p>Excess vitamin C from supplements (&gt;2,000 mg/day) can cause osmotic diarrhea, nausea, and abdominal cramps. In susceptible individuals, high-dose vitamin C increases urinary oxalate excretion, potentially increasing kidney stone risk (particularly calcium oxalate stones). High-dose intravenous vitamin C can cause falsely elevated blood glucose readings on point-of-care glucometers. Despite popular claims, megadose vitamin C has not been proven to prevent the common cold, though modest doses (200-1,000 mg/day) may reduce duration and severity of symptoms.</p>
