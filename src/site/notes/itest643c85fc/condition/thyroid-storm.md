<h1>Thyroid Storm</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis) is a <strong>rare, life-threatening decompensation of thyrotoxicosis</strong> characterized by <strong>hyperpyrexia, severe tachycardia, central nervous system dysfunction, and multi-organ failure</strong>. It represents the extreme end of the thyrotoxicosis spectrum, typically triggered by a <strong>precipitating event</strong> (infection, surgery, trauma, iodine load, or abrupt cessation of antithyroid drugs) in a patient with <strong>underlying uncontrolled hyperthyroidism</strong> -- most commonly <strong>Graves disease</strong>. Mortality remains <strong>10-30%</strong> despite modern therapy, making <strong>immediate recognition and aggressive treatment</strong> essential.</p>
<p>The pathophysiology is a <strong>massive catecholamine-mediated hypermetabolic crisis</strong> driven by high circulating thyroid hormones. Thyroid hormone upregulates <strong>beta-adrenergic receptors</strong> and amplifies catecholamine signaling, producing <strong>tachycardia, arrhythmia, hyperpyrexia, agitation, and high-output cardiac failure</strong>. The transition from uncomplicated thyrotoxicosis to thyroid storm is <strong>clinical</strong>, not biochemical -- there is no single T4/T3 threshold that defines storm, and thyroid hormone levels overlap significantly between storm and uncomplicated thyrotoxicosis. The <strong>Burch-Wartofsky Point Scale (BWPS)</strong> uses body temperature, central nervous system effects, gastrointestinal-hepatic dysfunction, tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, and precipitant history to score probability; <strong>&gt;=45</strong> is highly suggestive of thyroid storm.</p>
<p>The diagnosis is <strong>clinical</strong> and must be made rapidly -- waiting for thyroid function tests can be fatal. The classic tetrad is <strong>(1) hyperpyrexia (&gt;104 F/40 C, often disproportionate to infection), (2) tachycardia (&gt;140 bpm, often atrial fibrillation), (3) CNS dysfunction (agitation, delirium, seizures, coma), and (4) gastrointestinal-hepatic dysfunction (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, jaundice)</strong>. The differential includes <strong>sepsis, malignant hyperthermia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, heat stroke, and pheochromocytoma crisis</strong> -- but a known history of hyperthyroidism, goiter, exophthalmos (Graves), or a precipitating trigger should raise suspicion for storm.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Immediate priorities (ICU admission; do not wait for labs):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Recognize the diagnosis clinically</strong> (Burch-Wartofsky score &gt;=45; or strong suspicion)</li>
<li><strong>Search for and treat the precipitant</strong> -- blood/urine cultures, CXR; broad-spectrum antibiotics if infection suspected; cooling; correct electrolytes</li>
<li><strong>Supportive care</strong> -- IV fluids, cardiac monitoring, active cooling (cooling blankets, ice), <strong>acetaminophen</strong> (NOT aspirin -- aspirin displaces T4 from TBG raising free T4)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Specific therapy (in order of administration -- TIMING IS CRITICAL):</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>
<p><strong>Beta-blockade (FIRST)</strong> -- <strong>propranolol 60-80 mg PO q4-6h</strong> OR <strong>esmolol 50-100 mcg/kg/min IV</strong> (loading 250-500 mcg/kg) for unstable patients. Propranolol preferred because at high doses it also blocks <strong>T4-to-T3 conversion</strong>. <strong>Caution in heart failure</strong> -- use short-acting esmolol; some HF is high-output from tachycardia and improves with beta-blockade</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Antithyroid drugs (1-2 hours AFTER beta-blocker)</strong> -- blocks new hormone synthesis</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Propylthiouracil (PTU) 500-1000 mg loading, then 250 mg q4h PO/NG</strong> -- preferred in storm because it <strong>also blocks peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion</strong> (superior to methimazole for this)</li>
<li><strong>Methimazole 60-80 mg/day</strong> as alternative; easier dosing but no peripheral conversion block</li>
<li>PTU preferred in first trimester pregnancy and storm; methimazole preferred otherwise</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Iodine (AT LEAST 1 hour AFTER antithyroid drug)</strong> -- blocks release of preformed hormone from the gland (Wolff-Chaikoff effect) AND blocks new synthesis (Jod-Basedowski prevented by prior thionamide)</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Lugol's iodine</strong> 8-10 drops q6-8h PO, OR <strong>saturated solution of potassium iodide (SSKI)</strong> 5 drops q6h, OR <strong>sodium iodide</strong> 0.5-1 g IV q12h</li>
<li><strong>Critical timing</strong>: if given before antithyroid drug, iodine provides substrate for MORE hormone synthesis (Jod-Basedowski phenomenon) -- always give thionamide first</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Glucocorticoids</strong> -- <strong>hydrocortisone 100 mg IV q8h</strong> (or dexamethasone 2 mg q6h)</p>
<ul>
<li>Blocks <strong>T4-to-T3 conversion</strong> (additive to PTU)</li>
<li>Treats <strong>relative adrenal insufficiency</strong> (high cortisol demand in storm; common)</li>
<li>Reduces autoimmune drive in Graves</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Bile acid sequestrants (refractory cases)</strong> -- <strong>cholestyramine 4 g q6h</strong> blocks enterohepatic recirculation of T4; useful when other agents contraindicated</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Plasmapheresis / plasma exchange</strong> -- for <strong>refractory storm</strong> or contraindication to thionamides; rapidly lowers circulating T4</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Thyroidectomy</strong> -- definitive therapy after stabilization; indicated for refractory storm or when drugs contraindicated</p>
</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Ongoing management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Continue all four agents until clinical improvement (typically 24-72 hours)</li>
<li>Taper iodine and hydrocortisone first; continue beta-blocker and antithyroid drug long-term</li>
<li>Plan definitive therapy: radioactive iodine (after 4-6 weeks of drug preparation) or thyroidectomy</li>
<li><strong>Monitor</strong>: cardiac rhythm, temperature, mental status, liver function, electrolytes</li>
<li><strong>Avoid</strong> aspirin (displaces T4 from TBG), pseudoephedrine and sympathomimetics, amiodarone (iodine load)</li>
</ul>
