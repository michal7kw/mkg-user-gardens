<h1>Nephrotic Syndrome</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Nephrotic syndrome is a clinical constellation defined by the tetrad <strong>(1) proteinuria &gt;=3.5 g/day per 1.73 m2, (2) hypoalbuminemia (&lt;3.0 g/dL), (3) edema, and (4) hyperlipidemia</strong>. It reflects a breakdown of the glomerular filtration barrier -- the <strong>trilayer of fenestrated endothelium, glomerular basement membrane, and podocyte slit diaphragm</strong> -- that normally prevents albumin and larger proteins from crossing into the urine. When this barrier is damaged (podocyte injury, immune complex deposition, or basement membrane thickening), massive non-selective proteinuria follows, with systemic consequences that extend far beyond the kidney.</p>
<p>The clinical importance of nephrotic syndrome lies in its <strong>complications</strong>, which are driven by the urinary loss of regulatory proteins. <strong>Edema</strong> results from hypoalbuminemia (reduced plasma oncotic pressure) plus primary renal sodium retention. <strong>Hyperlipidemia</strong> (elevated cholesterol and triglycerides) reflects upregulated hepatic lipoprotein synthesis in response to hypoalbuminemia, and accelerates atherosclerosis. <strong>Hypercoagulability</strong> from urinary loss of antithrombin III, plus increased fibrinogen and platelet activation, produces a <strong>thrombotic tendency</strong> -- particularly <strong>renal vein thrombosis</strong> (flank pain, hematuria, worsening renal function) and pulmonary embolism; risk is highest when albumin is <strong>&lt;2.5 g/dL</strong>. <strong>Infection susceptibility</strong> results from urinary loss of IgG and alternative complement pathway components; <strong>encapsulated organisms</strong> (<em>Pneumococcus</em>) and <strong>peritonitis</strong> are classic concerns. Loss of <strong>vitamin D-binding protein, thyroxine-binding globulin, and transferrin</strong> causes vitamin D deficiency, euthyroid sick syndrome, and microcytic anemia.</p>
<p>The differential diagnosis is broad and age-dependent. In <strong>children</strong> (peak 2-6 years), <strong>minimal change disease</strong> accounts for ~75-90% and is highly steroid-responsive. In <strong>adults</strong>, common causes include <strong>membranous nephropathy</strong> (PLA2R antibody positive in 70%), <strong>focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)</strong> (rising in African Americans, associated with APOL1 variants), and <strong>membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis</strong>. <strong>Secondary causes</strong> include <strong>diabetic nephropathy</strong> (the leading cause overall in adults), <strong>lupus nephritis</strong> (class V), and <strong>amyloidosis</strong>. Renal biopsy is usually required in adults for precise diagnosis and treatment guidance.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Treat the underlying cause:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Minimal change disease</strong> -- prednisone 1 mg/kg/day (60 mg) for 4-16 weeks; &gt;90% response. Cyclophosphamide or calcineurin inhibitors for frequent relapsers</li>
<li><strong>Membranous nephropathy</strong> -- spontaneous remission in ~30%; immunosuppression (cyclophosphamide + steroids, or calcineurin inhibitors, or rituximab) for high-risk disease. Anti-PLA2R titers guide treatment</li>
<li><strong>FSGS</strong> -- steroids for 4-6 months if primary; treat secondary FSGS by addressing the cause (obesity, HIV, drug)</li>
<li><strong>Diabetic nephropathy</strong> -- RAS blockade, glycemic and BP control, SGLT2 inhibitor</li>
<li><strong>Lupus nephritis (class V)</strong> -- mycophenolate mofetil or calcineurin inhibitors + steroids</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Supportive care (all patients):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ACE inhibitor or ARB</strong> -- first-line for all proteinuric CKD; reduces intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria by 30-50%. Monitor K+ and creatinine</li>
<li><strong>Sodium restriction</strong> (&lt;2 g/day) and <strong>loop diuretics</strong> (furosemide 40-80 mg; combine with thiazide for refractory edema) for edema</li>
<li><strong>SGLT2 inhibitors</strong> (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) -- reduce proteinuria and slow CKD progression independent of glucose</li>
<li><strong>Statin</strong> (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin) for hyperlipidemia</li>
<li><strong>Anticoagulation</strong> -- consider prophylactic when albumin &lt;2.5 g/dL (especially membranous); mandatory for documented thrombosis</li>
<li><strong>Pneumococcal, influenza, hepatitis B vaccination</strong></li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> (cholecalciferol 1000-2000 IU/day) and calcium supplementation</li>
<li>Monitor for hypothyroidism and treat if symptomatic</li>
</ul>
