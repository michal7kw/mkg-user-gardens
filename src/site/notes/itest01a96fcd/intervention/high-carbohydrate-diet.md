<h1>High-Carbohydrate Diet</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>A high-carbohydrate diet is one in which carbohydrates contribute a high share of daily energy—conventionally more than 55-60% of total calories. Historically it described traditional plant-based eating patterns in many regions of the world, but in industrialized settings it often coincides with high intake of refined grains and added sugars. The health consequences of a high-carbohydrate diet are therefore highly dependent on <em>carbohydrate quality</em> rather than quantity alone.</p>
<p>When carbohydrates come predominantly from whole grains, legumes, vegetables, and fruit—rich in fiber and low in glycemic index—a high-carbohydrate diet can support stable glycemia, healthy lipids, and a favorable gut microbiome. When they come from refined flour, sugar-sweetened beverages, and ultra-processed foods, the same macronutrient share is associated with hyperinsulinemia, hypertriglyceridemia, weight gain, and increased cardiometabolic and mortality risk. The large international PURE cohort found that very high carbohydrate intake was associated with higher total mortality, while the relationship between carbohydrate intake and mortality is U-shaped, with lowest risk in the 50-55% range.</p>
<p>Evidence Level: 3/5. Large prospective cohorts characterize the dose-response and the dominant role of carbohydrate quality; mechanistic data on glycemic and lipid responses are well established.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Dietary carbohydrate is the primary determinant of postprandial blood glucose and insulin secretion. High intake of rapidly digested, high-glycemic-index carbohydrate produces large glucose and insulin excursions; chronic hyperinsulinemia promotes hepatic de novo lipogenesis, elevated triglycerides, reduced HDL cholesterol, and—in susceptible individuals—progressive insulin resistance. Conversely, high intake of fiber-rich, low-glycemic-index carbohydrate slows glucose absorption, lowers the glycemic load, feeds butyrate-producing gut bacteria, and improves insulin sensitivity and lipid profiles. Thus the metabolic signature of a high-carbohydrate diet is set largely by the glycemic quality of the carbohydrate consumed.</p>
<h2>Protocol Details</h2>
<ul>
<li>Total carbohydrate typically &gt;55-60% of energy</li>
<li>Quality matters most: favor whole grains, legumes, vegetables, intact fruit</li>
<li>Minimize refined grains, added sugars, and sugar-sweetened beverages</li>
<li>Target adequate fiber (25-38 g/day) to lower glycemic load</li>
<li>Pair carbohydrate with protein, fat, or fiber to blunt glycemic spikes</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<p>A high-carbohydrate diet built on refined carbohydrates may worsen glycemic control in people with type 2 diabetes, prediabetes, or insulin resistance, and may aggravate hypertriglyceridemia. In these populations, improving carbohydrate quality (or reducing total carbohydrate) is generally preferred. A fiber-rich, whole-food high-carbohydrate diet is appropriate for most healthy adults.</p>
