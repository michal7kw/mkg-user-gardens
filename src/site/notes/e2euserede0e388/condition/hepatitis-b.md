<h1>Hepatitis B</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Hepatitis B is a liver infection caused by the <strong>hepatitis B virus (HBV)</strong>, a partially double-stranded DNA virus of the Hepadnaviridae family. Infection produces a spectrum of outcomes ranging from a self-limited acute hepatitis with full recovery, to an asymptomatic carrier state, to progressive chronic infection. The likelihood of chronicity is strongly age-dependent: most infections acquired perinatally or in early childhood become chronic, whereas the majority acquired in adulthood resolve.</p>
<p>The virus is transmitted through blood and body fluids, with perinatal mother-to-child transmission being the dominant route in highly endemic regions and sexual or percutaneous (needle-sharing, needlestick) exposure more common elsewhere. After infection, HBV replicates in hepatocytes; the resulting immune response against infected liver cells, rather than direct viral cytotoxicity, is largely responsible for liver injury. The clinical course is monitored through a panel of serologic markers — HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and HBV DNA — that together define infection status, phase, and infectivity.</p>
<p>Chronic hepatitis B is a major global health problem, infecting hundreds of millions of people and serving as a leading cause of <strong>cirrhosis</strong> and <strong>hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)</strong>. Critically, hepatitis B is <strong>vaccine-preventable</strong>: universal infant vaccination, including a timely birth dose, has dramatically reduced incidence and the downstream burden of liver cancer in countries that have implemented it.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Acute hepatitis B is frequently subclinical. When symptomatic, it presents with a prodrome of fatigue, malaise, anorexia, nausea, low-grade fever, and sometimes arthralgia or a serum-sickness-like syndrome, followed by jaundice, dark urine, and pale stools. Chronic hepatitis B is usually <strong>asymptomatic</strong> for years or decades and is often discovered incidentally on screening or when complications arise. Decompensated cirrhosis presents with ascites, variceal bleeding, jaundice, and hepatic encephalopathy.</p>

<p>Persistence of <strong>HBsAg for &gt;=6 months</strong> defines chronic infection. Liver enzymes (ALT/AST), fibrosis assessment (elastography or biopsy), and HBV DNA levels determine the infection phase and the need for treatment.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The principal routes of acquisition are perinatal transmission (dominant in endemic Asia and sub-Saharan Africa), unprotected sexual contact, injection drug use with needle sharing, and percutaneous/occupational blood exposure. Living in or being born in a high-prevalence region, household or sexual contact with a chronic carrier, hemodialysis, and HIV co-infection all increase risk.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Acute hepatitis B in adults is usually self-limited and managed supportively. <strong>Chronic</strong> hepatitis B is treated with potent <strong>nucleos(t)ide analogues — tenofovir or entecavir</strong> — that durably suppress HBV DNA and reduce progression to cirrhosis and HCC; pegylated interferon alfa is an alternative for selected patients. Most chronic patients require long-term suppressive therapy because functional cure (HBsAg loss) is uncommon. Patients at risk should undergo periodic <strong>HCC surveillance</strong> with ultrasound. Prevention through vaccination, plus hepatitis B immunoglobulin and a birth-dose vaccine for infants of HBsAg-positive mothers, is the cornerstone of control.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most serious complications of chronic infection are progressive fibrosis leading to <strong>cirrhosis</strong> and <strong>hepatocellular carcinoma</strong>, the latter of which can develop even in the absence of cirrhosis. Acute infection can rarely cause fulminant hepatic failure. <strong>Hepatitis D (delta)</strong> superinfection accelerates liver disease, and extrahepatic immune-complex manifestations (polyarteritis nodosa, membranous glomerulonephritis) can occur.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Most immunocompetent adults with acute hepatitis B recover fully and develop lasting immunity. Prognosis in chronic infection depends on viral replication, the degree of liver inflammation/fibrosis, and treatment: effective viral suppression substantially lowers the risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer. Untreated chronic infection with active replication carries a meaningful lifetime risk of cirrhosis and HCC.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Hepatitis B is one of the most common chronic viral infections worldwide, with the highest prevalence in the Western Pacific and African regions. Universal infant immunization programs have markedly reduced new infections and childhood HCC where adopted, though a large reservoir of chronically infected individuals remains.</p>
