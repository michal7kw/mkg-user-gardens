<h1>Osteomyelitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Osteomyelitis is infection of the bone, most commonly caused by <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em> (including MRSA). Other pathogens include coagulase-negative staphylococci (hardware-associated), streptococci, gram-negative bacilli (Pseudomonas in IV drug users; Enterobacteriaceae in diabetics), and anaerobes (polymicrobial diabetic foot infections). In children, osteomyelitis is typically <strong>hematogenous</strong> (bloodstream spread to the metaphysis of long bones); in adults, it is more commonly <strong>contiguous</strong> (spread from adjacent soft tissue infection, surgical site, or open fracture).</p>
<p>The Cierny-Mader classification stratifies osteomyelitis by anatomical type (medullary, superficial, localized, diffuse) and host status (A: healthy; B: compromised; C: extreme compromise). <strong>Chronic osteomyelitis</strong> develops when acute infection is inadequately treated, leading to necrotic bone (sequestrum) surrounded by reactive new bone (involucrum). Chronic infection can persist for years with recurrent flares, sinus tracts, and risk of malignant transformation (Marjolin's ulcer).</p>
<p>Diabetic foot osteomyelitis is a particularly challenging form, occurring in up to 20% of diabetic foot infections. Diagnosis combines clinical examination (probe-to-bone test), imaging (MRI gold standard), and bone biopsy for culture.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p><strong>Acute osteomyelitis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Localized bone pain (most consistent symptom)</li>
<li>Fever, chills (may be absent)</li>
<li>Swelling, erythema, warmth over the affected area</li>
<li>Limited range of motion of adjacent joint</li>
<li>In children: refusal to bear weight or use affected limb</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Chronic osteomyelitis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Persistent or recurrent pain</li>
<li>Sinus tract with purulent drainage</li>
<li>Low-grade fever</li>
<li>Local swelling and induration</li>
</ul>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p><strong>Laboratory:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Elevated ESR (&gt;30 mm/hr; typically &gt;50) and CRP -- useful for monitoring treatment response</li>
<li>Leukocytosis (may be absent)</li>
<li>Blood cultures (positive in ~40% of hematogenous osteomyelitis)</li>
<li><strong>Bone biopsy with culture</strong> -- gold standard for pathogen identification</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Imaging:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>MRI</strong> -- gold standard; sensitivity ~90%, specificity ~80%; shows marrow edema, abscess, sinus tract</li>
<li><strong>Plain radiography</strong> -- insensitive early (changes visible after 2-3 weeks); shows periosteal reaction, lytic lesions, sequestra</li>
<li><strong>CT</strong> -- useful for cortical destruction and surgical planning</li>
<li><strong>Bone scan (99mTc)</strong> -- high sensitivity but low specificity; useful for screening multifocal disease</li>
<li><strong>18F-FDG PET</strong> -- increasingly used for chronic osteomyelitis</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Probe-to-bone test</strong> (diabetic foot): palpating bone with a sterile probe through an ulcer; sensitivity ~87%, specificity ~83%.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Acute osteomyelitis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pathogen-directed IV antibiotics</strong> for 4-6 weeks (after culture)</li>
<li>Empiric therapy: vancomycin + 3rd-gen cephalosporin (broad coverage)</li>
<li>Transition to oral antibiotics after IV course if responding</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Chronic osteomyelitis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Surgical debridement</strong> of all necrotic bone (sequestrectomy) -- essential</li>
<li>Removal of infected hardware (if possible)</li>
<li><strong>Prolonged antibiotics</strong> (6 weeks minimum; often 3-6 months)</li>
<li>Local antibiotic delivery (antibiotic-impregnated cement spacers, beads)</li>
<li><strong>Soft tissue coverage</strong> (muscle/skin flaps) after debridement</li>
<li>Amputation for refractory or limb-threatening cases</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Diabetic foot osteomyelitis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Combined medical-surgical approach</li>
<li>Wound care, offloading, revascularization if needed</li>
<li>Duration of antibiotics controversial (6 weeks vs shorter with complete debridement)</li>
</ul>
