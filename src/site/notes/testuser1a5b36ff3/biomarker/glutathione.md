<h1>Glutathione</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Glutathione (gamma-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine, GSH) is the most abundant intracellular non-protein thiol and the body's master antioxidant. It is synthesized in all human cells via two ATP-dependent enzymatic steps catalyzed by gamma-glutamylcysteine synthetase (GCL) and glutathione synthetase (GS), with cysteine availability being the rate-limiting factor. GSH functions through three principal mechanisms: (1) direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals, (2) serving as a cofactor for glutathione peroxidase (GPx) enzymes that detoxify hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxides, and (3) conjugating with xenobiotics via glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) for Phase II hepatic detoxification.</p>
<p>When GSH donates an electron, it becomes oxidized to glutathione disulfide (GSSG). Under normal conditions, the enzyme glutathione reductase (GR) regenerates GSH from GSSG using NADPH as a cofactor. The ratio of reduced GSH to oxidized GSSG (the GSH/GSSG ratio) is a sensitive indicator of cellular redox status — a healthy ratio exceeds 10:1, while ratios below 5:1 indicate significant oxidative stress. Total glutathione (GSH + GSSG) reflects overall antioxidant reserves, while the GSH/GSSG ratio captures the dynamic balance between oxidation and reduction.</p>
<p>Clinically, glutathione depletion is one of the earliest and most consistent biochemical abnormalities observed in aging, chronic disease, and toxin exposure. Whole blood glutathione levels decline approximately 30-50% between ages 20 and 80, and this decline correlates with increased morbidity and mortality. Measurement is most informative in the context of comprehensive functional testing panels such as the <a href="/garden/testuser1a5b36ff3/labtest/nutreval-fmv/" class="internal-link">nutreval_fmv</a> and <a href="/garden/testuser1a5b36ff3/labtest/organic-acids-test/" class="internal-link">organic_acids_test</a>.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference ranges vary considerably by assay method (enzymatic recycling, HPLC, mass spectrometry) and laboratory</li>
<li>Whole blood is preferred for total GSH as erythrocytes contain high concentrations; plasma is preferred for the GSH/GSSG ratio</li>
<li>No universally standardized reference range exists; results should be interpreted in clinical context</li>
<li>GSH levels are influenced by recent diet, exercise, illness, and supplement intake</li>
<li>Sample handling is critical — GSH oxidizes rapidly ex vivo; samples must be immediately acidified or frozen</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<h3>Increase Glutathione</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>NAC (N-acetylcysteine) supplementation</strong> (provides cysteine, the rate-limiting precursor for GSH synthesis)</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C</strong> (recycles oxidized glutathione back to reduced form; spares GSH)</li>
<li><strong>Selenium</strong> (essential cofactor for glutathione peroxidase activity)</li>
<li><strong>Alpha lipoic acid</strong> (regenerates GSH from GSSG; universal antioxidant)</li>
<li><strong>Cruciferous vegetable intake</strong> (sulforaphane from broccoli activates Nrf2 pathway, upregulating GSH synthesis enzymes)</li>
<li><strong>Regular moderate exercise</strong> (hormetic stress upregulates endogenous antioxidant systems including GSH)</li>
<li><strong>Adequate protein intake</strong> (provides cysteine, glycine, and glutamate precursors)</li>
<li><strong>Whey protein</strong> (rich in cysteine-containing cystine residues)</li>
</ul>
<h3>Decrease Glutathione</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Aging</strong> (30-50% decline in GSH between ages 20-80)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic oxidative stress</strong> (depletes GSH faster than it can be regenerated)</li>
<li><strong>Alcohol consumption</strong> (hepatic GSH depletion through acetaldehyde metabolism)</li>
<li><strong>Acetaminophen (paracetamol) overdose</strong> (depletes hepatic GSH via NAPQI conjugation)</li>
<li><strong>Heavy metal exposure</strong> (mercury, lead, cadmium bind GSH and deplete reserves)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic illness</strong> (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, HIV all associated with GSH depletion)</li>
<li><strong>Malnutrition or protein deficiency</strong> (insufficient amino acid precursors)</li>
<li><strong>Intense prolonged exercise without recovery</strong> (excessive ROS production)</li>
<li><strong>Radiation and chemotherapy</strong> (massive oxidative burden depletes GSH)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<h3>Reduced levels are associated with:</h3>
<ul>
<li>
<p><strong>Liver Disease</strong>: The liver is the primary site of glutathione synthesis and utilization. <a href="/garden/testuser1a5b36ff3/condition/liver-disease/" class="internal-link">Liver Disease</a> of any etiology (viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, NAFLD, drug-induced liver injury) is associated with depleted hepatic GSH. In acetaminophen toxicity, GSH depletion below 30% of normal leads to unchecked oxidative damage and hepatic necrosis — NAC is administered as an antidote to replenish GSH.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Oxidative Stress Overload</strong>: GSH depletion is both a cause and consequence of <a href="/garden/testuser1a5b36ff3/condition/oxidative-stress/" class="internal-link">Oxidative Stress</a>. When GSH reserves are insufficient to neutralize ROS, a vicious cycle of oxidative damage occurs, damaging lipids (lipid peroxidation), proteins (carbonylation), and DNA (8-OHdG formation). This drives aging and chronic disease pathology.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Autoimmune Disease</strong>: Low GSH levels are consistently observed in <a href="/garden/testuser1a5b36ff3/condition/autoimmune-disease/" class="internal-link">Autoimmune Disease</a> conditions including rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and multiple sclerosis. GSH deficiency in immune cells impairs regulatory T-cell function and promotes pro-inflammatory T-helper 17 cell differentiation, sustaining autoimmune inflammation.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Neurodegenerative Diseases</strong>: The brain is particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage due to high metabolic rate and lipid content. GSH depletion in the substantia nigra is an early event in Parkinson's disease, and low brain GSH correlates with disease severity in Alzheimer's disease.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Cancer</strong>: Paradoxically, many cancers upregulate GSH to resist chemotherapy, while systemic GSH depletion in cancer patients reflects disease burden and treatment toxicity. The relationship is complex — low systemic GSH indicates poor antioxidant capacity, while high tumor GSH indicates treatment resistance.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Aging and Longevity</strong>: Age-related GSH decline is one of the most reproducible biomarkers of biological aging. Centenarians maintain higher GSH levels than age-matched controls, suggesting that preserved glutathione homeostasis contributes to exceptional longevity.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<h3>Monitoring use cases:</h3>
<ul>
<li>Assessing oxidative stress burden in chronic illness</li>
<li>Monitoring antioxidant adequacy during detoxification protocols</li>
<li>Evaluating response to NAC or glutathione supplementation</li>
<li>Functional medicine assessment of mitochondrial function</li>
<li>Monitoring hepatic reserve in liver disease</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>NAC (N-acetylcysteine) supplementation</strong> — 600-1800 mg/day; provides cysteine, the rate-limiting GSH precursor. The GlyNAC trial (glycine + NAC) demonstrated improved GSH, oxidative stress, inflammation, and physical function in older adults</li>
<li><strong>Increase dietary cysteine and glycine</strong> — whey protein (20-30g/day), cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, Brussels sprouts), allium vegetables (garlic, onions)</li>
<li><strong>Sulforaphane-rich foods</strong> — broccoli sprouts contain 20-50x more sulforaphane precursor than mature broccoli; activates Nrf2 pathway to upregulate GSH synthesis enzymes</li>
<li><strong>Selenium supplementation</strong> (if deficient) — 100-200 mcg/day; essential cofactor for glutathione peroxidase activity</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C</strong> — 500-1000 mg/day; spares GSH by directly scavenging ROS and recycling oxidized GSH</li>
<li><strong>Alpha lipoic acid</strong> — 300-600 mg/day; regenerates GSH and other antioxidants (vitamin C, vitamin E, CoQ10) in a redox cycling network</li>
<li><strong>Regular moderate exercise</strong> — 150 minutes/week; hormetic upregulation of endogenous antioxidant systems</li>
<li><strong>Minimize toxin exposure</strong> — reduce alcohol, avoid unnecessary acetaminophen, filter water, choose organic produce when possible</li>
</ol>
