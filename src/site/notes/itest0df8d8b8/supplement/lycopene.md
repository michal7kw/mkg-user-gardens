<h1>Lycopene</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Lycopene is a bright-red, fat-soluble carotenoid responsible for the color of tomatoes, watermelon, pink grapefruit, and guava. Unlike beta-carotene it is not a vitamin A precursor; its principal biological relevance is as one of the most efficient singlet-oxygen-quenching antioxidants among dietary carotenoids. Tomato products are the dominant dietary source, and heat processing with oil substantially increases its bioavailability by converting the all-trans form to more absorbable cis-isomers.</p>
<p>Clinically, lycopene is best known in the context of prostate health. Observational and epidemiological studies have repeatedly associated higher tomato/lycopene intake with reduced prostate cancer incidence, which generated strong interest in supplementation. However, randomized controlled trials of isolated lycopene have produced modest and inconsistent effects on serum prostate-specific antigen and on disease progression, so it is best regarded as a supportive dietary antioxidant rather than a proven therapeutic agent.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level:</strong> 2/5</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Lycopene's actions stem mainly from its conjugated polyene structure and antioxidant chemistry:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Singlet-oxygen quenching and radical scavenging:</strong> Its eleven conjugated double bonds make it exceptionally effective at neutralizing singlet oxygen and peroxyl radicals, limiting oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA in tissues such as the prostate.</li>
<li><strong>Redox and detoxification signaling:</strong> Lycopene activates the Nrf2/ARE pathway, upregulating endogenous antioxidant and phase II detoxification enzymes, and dampens NF-kB-driven inflammatory signaling.</li>
<li><strong>Anti-proliferative effects:</strong> In preclinical prostate models it downregulates insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling and androgen-responsive proliferative pathways and can induce cell-cycle arrest, providing a plausible mechanism behind the epidemiological associations.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dosage</h2>
<p>Typical supplemental doses are 10-30 mg/day, taken with a meal containing fat to enhance absorption. Dietary lycopene from cooked tomato products (sauce, paste, juice) is often preferred because processing increases the proportion of bioavailable cis-lycopene. Effects, when present, develop over months of consistent intake rather than acutely.</p>
