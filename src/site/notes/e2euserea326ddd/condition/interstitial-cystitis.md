<h1>Interstitial Cystitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Interstitial cystitis (IC), also called <strong>bladder pain syndrome (BPS)</strong> or <strong>painful bladder syndrome</strong>, is a <strong>chronic, non-infectious</strong> condition characterized by <strong>pelvic pain, urinary urgency and frequency, and negative urine cultures</strong>. It predominantly affects women (9:1 ratio) with typical onset in the 30s-50s, affecting an estimated <strong>3-6% of adult women</strong> (previously thought rare; modern epidemiology suggests much higher prevalence). The diagnosis is one of <strong>exclusion</strong> -- after ruling out urinary tract infection, bladder cancer, and other definable causes.</p>
<p>The etiology is unknown, but the prevailing hypothesis involves a <strong>breakdown of the protective glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer</strong> of the bladder urothelium, allowing <strong>potassium and other urinary solutes</strong> to penetrate into the bladder wall and trigger <strong>neurogenic inflammation</strong>, <strong>mast cell activation</strong>, and <strong>sensory nerve upregulation</strong>. This produces the characteristic <strong>allodynia</strong> (pain from normally non-painful stimuli like bladder filling) and <strong>hyperalgesia</strong>. A subset (~10%) of patients have <strong>Hunner lesions</strong> -- distinct ulcerations of the bladder wall visible on cystoscopy -- and represent a more severe phenotype with worse prognosis and distinct management. <strong>Central sensitization</strong> (amplification of pain signaling in the central nervous system) explains the high comorbidity with other chronic pain syndromes (<strong>fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, chronic fatigue syndrome, vulvodynia, temporomandibular disorder</strong>) -- collectively the <strong>chronic overlapping pain conditions (COPCs)</strong>.</p>
<p>The clinical hallmark is <strong>bladder pain that worsens with filling and is relieved by voiding</strong>, accompanied by <strong>urgency and frequency</strong> (up to 40-60 times/day in severe cases) and <strong>nocturia</strong>. Symptoms typically flare in response to dietary triggers (<strong>caffeine, alcohol, citrus, tomatoes, spicy foods, artificial sweeteners</strong>), stress, and hormonal changes (menstrual cycle). The condition is not life-threatening but <strong>severely impairs quality of life</strong> -- comparable to patients on renal dialysis -- and is associated with <strong>depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and sexual dysfunction</strong>.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>AUA (American Urological Association) guidelines recommend a <strong>stepwise, multimodal approach</strong> based on symptom severity, starting with conservative measures and escalating only if needed. <strong>The treatment must be individualized</strong> -- no single therapy works for all patients, and combinations are usually required.</p>
<p><strong>Tier 1 (first-line, all patients):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Patient education</strong> -- chronic nature, flare patterns, expected outcomes</li>
<li><strong>Dietary modification</strong> -- <strong>elimination diet</strong> to identify triggers; common offenders: caffeine, alcohol, citrus, tomatoes, vinegar, spicy foods, artificial sweeteners, carbonated beverages</li>
<li><strong>Stress management</strong> -- relaxation, mindfulness, cognitive behavioral therapy</li>
<li><strong>Pelvic floor physical therapy</strong> -- myofascial release, trigger point therapy, biofeedback; effective for the ~85% of IC patients with coexisting pelvic floor dysfunction</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tier 2 (if Tier 1 inadequate):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Amitriptyline</strong> (10-75 mg at bedtime) -- tricyclic with analgesic, anticholinergic, and antihistaminic effects; first-line oral therapy for pain</li>
<li><strong>Hydroxyzine</strong> (25-50 mg at bedtime) -- H1 antagonist; reduces mast cell activation</li>
<li><strong>Pentosan polysulfate sodium (Elmiron)</strong> (100 mg TID) -- only FDA-approved oral therapy for IC; aims to repair the GAG layer; modest benefit, slow onset (3-6 months); recent concern for pigmentary maculopathy with long-term use</li>
<li><strong>Cimetidine</strong> (300 mg BID) -- H2 antagonist; off-label</li>
<li><strong>Bladder training</strong> and fluid management</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tier 3 (refractory):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Intravesical therapy</strong> -- <strong>DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide)</strong> instillation (weekly x6 weeks; the only FDA-approved intravesical agent), heparin, lidocaine +/- sodium bicarbonate, pentosan, botulinum toxin A (100-200 U)</li>
<li><strong>Cystoscopy with hydrodistention</strong> under anesthesia (diagnostic and therapeutic; stretches the bladder wall)</li>
<li><strong>Hunner lesion fulguration</strong> (laser or electrocautery) -- for the ulcerative phenotype; can provide months of relief</li>
<li><strong>Neuromodulation</strong> (sacral, pudendal nerve stimulation; InterStim)</li>
<li><strong>Cyclosporine A</strong> -- immunosuppressant for refractory disease; risk of nephrotoxicity and hypertension</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tier 4 (last resort, severe refractory):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Major surgery</strong> -- urinary diversion +/- cystectomy (cystourethrectomy); reserved for the most severe, refractory cases; significant morbidity</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Comorbidity management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Treat depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance (SSRIs, SNRIs, CBT-I)</li>
<li>Manage overlapping pain syndromes (fibromyalgia, IBS, vulvodynia) -- often requires multidisciplinary pain management</li>
<li>Avoid long-term opioids if possible (chronic pain, dependence, hyperalgesia)</li>
<li>Avoid unnecessary antibiotics for &quot;UTI&quot; symptoms when cultures are negative</li>
</ul>
