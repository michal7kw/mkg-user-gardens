<h1>Mitophagy</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Mitophagy is the selective degradation of damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria through the autophagy–lysosome system. It is the principal mechanism by which cells perform mitochondrial quality control: rather than allowing a depolarized, ROS-leaking, or fragmented mitochondrion to persist, the cell tags it for engulfment by an autophagosome and ultimately for digestion within the lysosome. By continuously pruning compromised organelles and balancing their elimination against mitochondrial biogenesis, mitophagy maintains a healthy mitochondrial network and protects against bioenergetic decline.</p>
<p>The health relevance of mitophagy is broad. Failing mitochondria are a major source of reactive oxygen species and can release cytochrome c and other factors that initiate apoptosis or activate the inflammasome. Efficient mitophagy therefore limits oxidative damage, prevents inappropriate cell death, and dampens sterile inflammation. Mitophagic flux is highest in metabolically demanding, post-mitotic tissues such as neurons and cardiac and skeletal muscle, where the inability to dilute damaged organelles by cell division makes recycling essential.</p>
<p>Mitophagy declines with age, and this decline is mechanistically linked to neurodegenerative disease (notably Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease), cardiometabolic dysfunction, and the loss of muscle mass and strength seen in sarcopenia. Because the pathway is responsive to lifestyle inputs and to several nutraceuticals, restoring mitophagic capacity has become an active target for healthy-aging and neuroprotective interventions.</p>
<h2>Mechanism</h2>
<p>Two broad routes drive mitophagy. In the <strong>ubiquitin-dependent (PINK1/Parkin) pathway</strong>, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential stabilizes the kinase PINK1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane. PINK1 phosphorylates ubiquitin and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin (PRKN), recruiting and activating Parkin to build poly-ubiquitin chains on outer-membrane proteins. These chains are read by autophagy receptors such as OPTN and p62/SQSTM1, which bridge the tagged mitochondrion to LC3 on the forming autophagosomal membrane. In the <strong>receptor-mediated pathway</strong>, outer-membrane proteins including BNIP3, NIX (BNIP3L), and FUNDC1 bind LC3 directly through LIR motifs, a route especially important during hypoxia and developmental mitochondrial clearance.</p>
<p>Mitophagy is integrated with broader nutrient- and energy-sensing networks. AMPK activation (from low energy charge, exercise, or caloric restriction) promotes both autophagy initiation and mitochondrial turnover, and sirtuin/NAD+-dependent signaling couples mitophagy to mitochondrial biogenesis so that removal of damaged organelles is matched by synthesis of new, functional ones.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Reduced mitophagy contributes to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria that characterizes aging tissues. Loss-of-function mutations in <em>PINK1</em> and <em>PRKN</em> cause autosomal-recessive early-onset Parkinson's disease, providing the clearest human evidence that defective mitophagy is pathogenic in neurodegeneration. Impaired clearance also features in Alzheimer's disease, heart failure, metabolic syndrome, and age-related muscle wasting. Conversely, enhancing mitophagy is protective in numerous preclinical models, and compounds that boost mitophagic flux—such as urolithin A, spermidine, and NAD+ precursors—are being evaluated for benefits on muscle function, cardiometabolic health, and cognition.</p>
<h2>Modulation and Interventions</h2>
<p>Lifestyle factors are among the most reliable activators of mitophagy: aerobic and resistance exercise, caloric restriction, and intermittent fasting all increase mitophagic flux, largely via AMPK and reduced mTOR signaling. Several supplements and dietary metabolites enhance the pathway—urolithin A (a gut-microbial metabolite of ellagitannins), spermidine, NAD+ precursors such as NMN, resveratrol, sulforaphane, and coenzyme Q10. Pathway activity can be monitored indirectly through oxidative-stress biomarkers, since effective mitophagy lowers ROS-driven markers such as 8-OHdG and F2-isoprostanes while supporting glutathione status.</p>
