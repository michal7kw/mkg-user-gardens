<h1>Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is defined as nausea, retching, or vomiting occurring within the first 24-48 hours after surgery. It affects approximately <strong>25-30% of surgical patients</strong> overall, and up to <strong>70-80% of high-risk patients</strong> without prophylaxis. PONV is one of the most common complications of anesthesia and surgery, and is consistently rated by patients as the most unpleasant aspect of their surgical experience -- sometimes more distressing than postoperative pain itself.</p>
<p>The pathophysiology is <strong>multifactorial</strong>, involving activation of several receptor pathways:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>5-HT3 receptors</strong> (vagal afferents and chemoreceptor trigger zone; activated by anesthetics and opioids)</li>
<li><strong>Dopamine D2 receptors</strong> (chemoreceptor trigger zone)</li>
<li><strong>NK1 receptors</strong> (vomiting center; substance P)</li>
<li><strong>Histamine H1 and muscarinic M1 receptors</strong> (vestibular pathway)</li>
<li><strong>GABA</strong> (inhibitory modulation)</li>
</ul>
<p>The <strong>Apfel simplified risk score</strong> identifies four independent risk factors (1 point each): female sex, non-smoking status, history of PONV/motion sickness, and postoperative opioid use. Score 0-1: risk ~10-20%; score 2: ~40%; score 3: ~60%; score 4: ~80%. Patients at moderate-high risk should receive prophylactic antiemetics.</p>
<p><strong>Multimodal prophylaxis</strong> (combining antiemetics from different receptor classes) is more effective than monotherapy. PONV prevention improves patient satisfaction, reduces unplanned admissions, and shortens hospital stay.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Prophylaxis</strong> (risk-stratified):</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low risk (0-1):</strong> no routine prophylaxis; rescue antiemetic if needed</li>
<li><strong>Moderate risk (2):</strong> 1-2 prophylactic agents (e.g., ondansetron + dexamethasone)</li>
<li><strong>High risk (3-4):</strong> 2-3 prophylactic agents + opioid-sparing analgesia + propofol-based TIVA</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Common prophylactic agents:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>5-HT3 antagonist:</strong> ondansetron 4 mg IV (at end of surgery)</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroid:</strong> dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV (at induction)</li>
<li><strong>Butyrophenone:</strong> droperidol 0.625-1.25 mg IV (at end of surgery)</li>
<li><strong>NK1 antagonist:</strong> aprepitant 40 mg PO (preoperative)</li>
<li><strong>Anticholinergic:</strong> transdermal scopolamine (apply night before or 2 hours pre-op)</li>
<li><strong>Phenothiazine:</strong> promethazine 6.25-12.5 mg IV</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Rescue treatment</strong> (if PONV despite prophylaxis): use an agent from a different class than was used for prophylaxis</p>
<p><strong>Non-pharmacologic:</strong> acupuncture (P6), ginger, aromatherapy (isopropyl alcohol)</p>
