<h1>Acute Myocardial Infarction</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Acute myocardial infarction (AMI), commonly called a heart attack, is the irreversible necrosis of cardiac muscle that results when myocardial oxygen demand outstrips a critically reduced supply. The most common mechanism (type 1 MI) is rupture or erosion of an atherosclerotic plaque within a coronary artery, triggering platelet aggregation and thrombus formation that abruptly occludes blood flow. When the occlusion is complete and persistent, the overlying myocardium begins to die within roughly 20-30 minutes, and the infarct expands outward from the subendocardium over the following hours — making time-to-reperfusion the single most important determinant of outcome (&quot;time is muscle&quot;).</p>
<p>The Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction frames the diagnosis around evidence of myocardial injury (a rise and/or fall of cardiac troponin above the 99th percentile) combined with a clinical context of ischemia. It distinguishes spontaneous plaque-related infarction (type 1) from infarction due to a supply-demand mismatch without acute plaque rupture (type 2, e.g., severe anemia, tachyarrhythmia, or hypotension), as well as procedure-related and fatal subtypes. Clinically and electrocardiographically, AMI is operationally divided into ST-elevation MI (STEMI), which signifies acute transmural ischemia from total occlusion and mandates emergency reperfusion, and non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI), which reflects subtotal occlusion and is managed with an early invasive or ischemia-guided strategy.</p>
<p>AMI remains a leading global cause of mortality and a major driver of chronic heart failure among survivors. Because most events arise on a background of modifiable risk, the disease is highly preventable through smoking cessation, blood-pressure and lipid control, glycemic management, and physical activity. For those who survive the acute event, guideline-directed medical therapy and cardiac rehabilitation substantially reduce recurrence and improve long-term survival.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The classic presentation is acute, persistent retrosternal chest pain or pressure, frequently radiating to the left arm, jaw, neck, or back, accompanied by dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, and a feeling of impending doom. Symptoms typically last longer than 20 minutes and are not relieved by rest. Atypical or &quot;silent&quot; presentations — fatigue, indigestion-like discomfort, or isolated dyspnea — are disproportionately common in women, older adults, and people with diabetes, contributing to delayed presentation and worse outcomes.</p>

<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant risk factors are the traditional drivers of atherosclerosis: smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, and an atherogenic diet, layered on non-modifiable factors of older age, male sex, and family history of premature coronary disease. Prior coronary artery disease or a previous infarction markedly raises recurrence risk.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>STEMI is a time-critical emergency: primary PCI is the preferred reperfusion strategy when achievable within recommended timeframes, with fibrinolysis used where timely PCI is unavailable. NSTEMI is managed with antithrombotic therapy and risk-stratified invasive coronary evaluation. Across all types, the medical backbone includes dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor such as clopidogrel), high-intensity statins, beta-blockers, and ACE inhibitors or ARBs, followed by structured secondary prevention and cardiac rehabilitation.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Early complications include acute heart failure, cardiogenic shock, and life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, while mechanical complications such as papillary muscle or ventricular septal rupture, though less common in the reperfusion era, carry high mortality. Later sequelae include left ventricular remodeling with chronic heart failure, left ventricular thrombus formation, and recurrent ischemic events.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis depends heavily on the speed and completeness of reperfusion, infarct size, residual left ventricular function, and adherence to secondary prevention. Prompt reperfusion and modern guideline-directed therapy have dramatically reduced in-hospital mortality, but large infarcts and post-infarction heart failure continue to predict poorer long-term survival.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Ischemic heart disease, of which AMI is the acute manifestation, is among the most common causes of death globally. Incidence rises sharply with age and is strongly patterned by the prevalence of smoking, hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, with a growing burden in low- and middle-income countries.</p>
