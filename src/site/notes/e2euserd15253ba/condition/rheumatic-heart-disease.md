<h1>Rheumatic Heart Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) is the chronic valvular damage caused by <strong>acute rheumatic fever (ARF)</strong> -- an autoimmune inflammatory response to Group A Streptococcus (GAS) pharyngitis. The molecular mimicry between GAS M-protein and human cardiac proteins (myosin, tropomyosin, valvular proteins) triggers cross-reactive antibodies and T-cells that damage cardiac tissue, particularly the <strong>heart valves</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Valvular involvement in RHD:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mitral valve</strong> (most common; ~70%): <strong>mitral stenosis</strong> (fusion of commissures; &quot;fish-mouth&quot; valve), mitral regurgitation (acute phase)</li>
<li><strong>Aortic valve</strong> (~25%): aortic regurgitation, aortic stenosis (late)</li>
<li><strong>Tricuspid valve</strong> (~10%): usually secondary to right heart dilation</li>
<li><strong>Pulmonary valve</strong> (&lt;5%): rare</li>
</ul>
<p>RHD remains the <strong>leading cause of acquired valvular heart disease in developing countries</strong> and is responsible for an estimated <strong>305,000 deaths annually</strong> worldwide. It is <strong>entirely preventable</strong> through prompt antibiotic treatment of GAS pharyngitis (primary prevention) and through secondary prophylaxis after an episode of ARF (benzathine penicillin G injections every 3-4 weeks for years).</p>
<p>The <strong>2022 WHF (World Heart Federation) Guidelines</strong> provide comprehensive recommendations for RHD diagnosis, management, and prevention in resource-limited settings. Echocardiographic screening has revealed a much higher prevalence of subclinical RHD than previously recognized.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Secondary prevention (penicillin prophylaxis):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Benzathine penicillin G</strong> 1.2 million units IM every 3-4 weeks (gold standard)</li>
<li><strong>Penicillin V</strong> 250 mg PO BID (alternative; less reliable adherence)</li>
<li><strong>Duration:</strong> per WHO/WHF guidelines:
<ul>
<li>After ARF without carditis: 5 years or until age 21 (whichever longer)</li>
<li>After ARF with mild carditis: 10 years or until age 21</li>
<li>After ARF with severe carditis or valve surgery: lifelong or until age 40</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Heart failure management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Diuretics (furosemide) for congestion</li>
<li>ACE inhibitors/ARBs for afterload reduction</li>
<li>Beta-blockers (if tolerable)</li>
<li>Digoxin for rate control in AF</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Valvular intervention:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Balloon mitral valvotomy (BMV):</strong> percutaneous; for severe mitral stenosis with favorable valve morphology</li>
<li><strong>Surgical valve repair or replacement:</strong> for severe mitral or aortic regurgitation, failed BMV, or combined lesions
<ul>
<li>Mechanical valves require lifelong anticoagulation (warfarin; target INR 2.5-3.5)</li>
<li>Bioprosthetic valves have shorter lifespan but no anticoagulation needed unless AF</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Endocarditis prophylaxis:</strong> amoxicillin 2 g (or ampicillin 2 g IV) before dental and selected surgical procedures</p>
<p><strong>Anticoagulation:</strong> for atrial fibrillation (CHA2DS2-VASc typically high in RHD)</p>
