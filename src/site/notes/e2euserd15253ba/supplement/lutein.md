<h1>Lutein</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Lutein is a xanthophyll carotenoid — an oxygenated member of the carotenoid family — that the human body cannot synthesize and must obtain from the diet. Along with its stereoisomer zeaxanthin, lutein selectively accumulates in the macula lutea of the retina and in the crystalline lens, where the two pigments together constitute the macular pigment. The yellow macular pigment filters short-wavelength, high-energy blue light before it reaches the photoreceptors and acts as a chain-breaking lipid antioxidant, quenching singlet oxygen and reactive oxygen species generated by the intense light flux and high oxygen tension of the outer retina.</p>
<p>Dietary sources are concentrated in dark green leafy vegetables (spinach, kale, collard greens), as well as egg yolk, corn, and orange/yellow vegetables. Egg-yolk lutein is highly bioavailable because it is delivered in a lipid matrix. As a fat-soluble compound, lutein absorption is enhanced when consumed with dietary fat.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Blue-light filtration</strong>: Absorbs high-energy visible light, reducing photo-oxidative stress on the macula</li>
<li><strong>Antioxidant defense</strong>: Quenches singlet oxygen and scavenges peroxyl radicals in retinal and lens lipid membranes</li>
<li><strong>Macular pigment optical density</strong>: Supplementation raises macular pigment density, a measurable correlate of foveal protection</li>
</ul>
<h2>Evidence</h2>
<p>The AREDS2 randomized controlled trial supported the addition of lutein and zeaxanthin to the age-related eye disease formulation for reducing progression to advanced age-related macular degeneration, and lutein/zeaxanthin replaced beta-carotene in the recommended formula owing to the lung-cancer risk of beta-carotene in smokers. Evidence for benefits in cognition and general antioxidant status is preliminary.</p>
