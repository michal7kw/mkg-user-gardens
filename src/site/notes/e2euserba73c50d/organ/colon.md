<h1>Colon</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The colon, or large intestine, is the terminal portion of the gastrointestinal tract extending from the ileocecal valve to the anus. It is subdivided into the cecum (with the appendix), the ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid colon, and the rectum. Its principal physiological roles are the reabsorption of water and electrolytes from the ~1.5 L of fluid delivered from the small intestine each day, and the storage and controlled elimination of stool.</p>
<p>The colon harbors the densest and most diverse microbial community in the human body. These commensal bacteria ferment otherwise indigestible dietary fiber into short-chain fatty acids — acetate, propionate, and especially butyrate — which serve as the primary energy source for colonocytes, reinforce the epithelial barrier, and exert anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. The colonic mucosa also participates in immune surveillance through gut-associated lymphoid tissue.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>The colon is the site of several major diseases. Colorectal cancer, frequently arising from adenomatous polyps over years, is among the most common and most preventable cancers, with colonoscopy serving as both a screening and a therapeutic (polypectomy) tool. The inflammatory bowel diseases — ulcerative colitis (confined to the colon) and Crohn's disease (which can affect any segment) — cause chronic mucosal inflammation. Irritable bowel syndrome is a common functional disorder of the colon. Adequate dietary fiber intake supports colonic transit, microbial diversity, and butyrate production.</p>
