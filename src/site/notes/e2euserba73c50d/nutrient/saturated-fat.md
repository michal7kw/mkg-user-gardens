<h1>Saturated Fat</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Saturated fatty acids (SFAs) are lipids with no carbon-carbon double bonds in their hydrocarbon chain, resulting in a straight molecular structure that allows tight packing and solid state at room temperature. They are found predominantly in animal fats (butter, lard, tallow) and tropical oils (coconut, palm). Common saturated fatty acids include lauric acid (C12), myristic acid (C14), palmitic acid (C16), and stearic acid (C18), each with distinct metabolic effects.</p>
<p>The relationship between saturated fat and cardiovascular disease has been debated for decades. The traditional lipid hypothesis holds that SFA intake raises LDL cholesterol, promoting atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events. However, recent evidence has nuanced this view: saturated fat predominantly raises large, buoyant LDL particles (less atherogenic) rather than small, dense LDL. Stearic acid (found in cocoa butter and beef) has a neutral effect on cholesterol, while lauric acid raises both LDL and HDL. The metabolic context -- what saturated fat replaces or is replaced by -- matters significantly.</p>
<p>Current guidelines from the American Heart Association recommend limiting saturated fat to less than 6% of total calories for cardiovascular risk reduction. Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat (particularly omega-3) reduces cardiovascular risk, while replacing with refined carbohydrates does not improve outcomes. The body can synthesize all necessary saturated fatty acids endogenously, so there is no dietary requirement. Saturated fat does play important physiological roles in cell membrane structure, surfactant production in lungs, and immune signaling.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Provides structural rigidity to cell membranes alongside unsaturated fats</li>
<li>Serves as a substrate for lung surfactant production (dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine)</li>
<li>Contributes to immune signaling through lipid raft formation</li>
<li>Provides concentrated energy for metabolic processes</li>
<li>Supports absorption of fat-soluble vitamins when consumed with meals</li>
<li>Serves as a precursor for ceramide and sphingolipid synthesis</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Butter and ghee</li>
<li>Red meat (beef, pork, lamb) -- especially fatty cuts</li>
<li>Full-fat cheese and cream</li>
<li>Coconut oil and coconut cream</li>
<li>Palm oil and palm kernel oil</li>
<li>Lard and tallow</li>
<li>Full-fat dairy (whole milk, ice cream)</li>
<li>Processed meats (bacon, sausage)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Saturated fat deficiency per se does not occur because the body readily synthesizes saturated fatty acids from carbohydrates via de novo lipogenesis. However, severely fat-restricted diets that eliminate all fat sources (including saturated) may impair fat-soluble vitamin absorption and hormone production.</p>
<p>Excess saturated fat intake is associated with elevated LDL cholesterol, increased cardiovascular risk (when replacing polyunsaturated fat), insulin resistance, systemic inflammation (via TLR4 activation), and increased risk of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. High saturated fat diets may also adversely affect gut microbiome composition, promoting pro-inflammatory bacterial species. The magnitude of cardiovascular risk depends heavily on the overall dietary pattern and what replaces saturated fat in the diet.</p>
