<h1>Sodium</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Sodium is the principal extracellular cation and an essential electrolyte that maintains fluid balance, blood volume, and osmotic pressure throughout the body. Together with potassium, sodium drives the Na+/K+-ATPase pump that generates the electrochemical gradient essential for nerve impulse transmission, muscle contraction, and nutrient absorption across cell membranes. Sodium also plays a critical role in acid-base balance and the co-transport of glucose and amino acids in the intestine.</p>
<p>While sodium is physiologically essential, the overwhelming public health concern is excess intake. Average sodium consumption in Western diets is 3,400-4,000 mg/day, far exceeding the AHA recommendation of less than 2,300 mg/day (ideally less than 1,500 mg/day for those with hypertension). Approximately 75% of dietary sodium comes from processed and restaurant foods rather than added table salt. Sodium sensitivity -- the degree to which blood pressure rises with sodium intake -- varies among individuals due to genetic polymorphisms in renal sodium handling (ACE, AGT, ADD1 genes), age, race, and kidney function.</p>
<p>The DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet, which emphasizes potassium-rich foods while limiting sodium, is one of the most effective non-pharmacological interventions for hypertension. Sodium restriction to 1,500 mg/day reduces systolic blood pressure by 5-6 mmHg in hypertensive individuals. However, some evidence suggests that very low sodium intake (&lt;1,500 mg/day) may paradoxically increase cardiovascular risk in certain populations, a phenomenon described as the J-shaped curve.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Maintains extracellular fluid volume and osmotic balance</li>
<li>Drives the Na+/K+-ATPase pump for cellular electrochemical gradients</li>
<li>Enables nerve impulse conduction and action potential generation</li>
<li>Facilitates intestinal absorption of glucose and amino acids via co-transport</li>
<li>Supports acid-base balance through renal bicarbonate reabsorption</li>
<li>Contributes to muscle contraction signaling</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Table salt (sodium chloride) -- 40% sodium by weight</li>
<li>Processed and packaged foods (major source)</li>
<li>Bread and baked goods</li>
<li>Canned soups and vegetables</li>
<li>Cheese and dairy products</li>
<li>Deli meats and cured meats</li>
<li>Soy sauce and condiments</li>
<li>Fast food and restaurant meals</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Hyponatremia (serum sodium &lt;135 mEq/L) causes headache, nausea, confusion, fatigue, and muscle cramps. Severe hyponatremia (&lt;120 mEq/L) can lead to cerebral edema, seizures, coma, and death. It occurs most commonly from excessive water intake (exercise-associated hyponatremia), SIADH, diuretic use, and severe vomiting or diarrhea. Chronic mild hyponatremia is associated with gait instability, falls, and osteoporosis in the elderly.</p>
<p>Chronic excess sodium intake is one of the most significant modifiable risk factors for hypertension, affecting approximately 1.3 billion people worldwide. Excess sodium promotes fluid retention, increases blood volume and cardiac output, and causes vascular stiffness. It is associated with increased cardiovascular risk, stroke, kidney disease progression, gastric cancer, calcium excretion (contributing to osteoporosis), and kidney stone formation. Reducing sodium intake is a cornerstone of cardiovascular disease prevention.</p>
