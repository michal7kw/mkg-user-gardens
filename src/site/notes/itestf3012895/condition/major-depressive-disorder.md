<h1>Major Depressive Disorder</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common mood disorder defined by a period of at least two weeks during which a person experiences <strong>persistent depressed mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia)</strong> together with a cluster of neurovegetative, cognitive, and somatic symptoms - changes in sleep and appetite, fatigue, psychomotor changes, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, difficulty concentrating, and recurrent thoughts of death. These symptoms represent a clear change from baseline functioning and cause meaningful distress or impairment.</p>
<p>MDD is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide and tends to be <strong>recurrent</strong>: many individuals who experience one episode go on to have further episodes over their lifetime. Its pathophysiology is multifactorial, involving genetic vulnerability, dysregulation of monoaminergic neurotransmission (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine), hyperactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal stress axis with elevated cortisol, neuroinflammation reflected in markers such as hs-CRP, and impaired neuroplasticity in brain regions including the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. Environmental contributors - childhood adversity, chronic stress, medical illness - interact with this biological substrate.</p>
<p>Clinically, MDD matters because of its high prevalence, its association with <strong>suicide</strong>, and its profound impact on quality of life and physical health. It is highly comorbid with anxiety disorders such as generalized anxiety disorder. Screening with the <strong>PHQ-9</strong> is widely used in primary care, and effective treatments exist; however, more than half of patients do not achieve remission with the first antidepressant trial, and a subset develop treatment-resistant depression requiring augmentation or neurostimulation.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The core features are pervasive low mood and anhedonia. Patients commonly report disrupted sleep (insomnia or hypersomnia), appetite and weight change, low energy, and difficulty concentrating or making decisions. Psychomotor slowing or, less often, agitation may be observed. Cognitive symptoms include guilt, hopelessness, and a sense of worthlessness, and <strong>suicidal ideation must be actively assessed</strong>. Somatic complaints such as pain and fatigue are frequent and may dominate the presentation, particularly in primary care.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis follows DSM-5 criteria: at least five of nine symptoms present during the same two-week period, with at least one being depressed mood or anhedonia, causing significant distress or impairment, not attributable to a substance or medical condition, and without a history of mania or hypomania (which would indicate bipolar disorder). The <strong>PHQ-9</strong> quantifies severity and monitors response. Thyroid dysfunction, anemia, and medication or substance effects should be excluded as contributors.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>MDD reflects an interplay of genetic and environmental factors. Risk is increased by a family history of mood disorders, female sex, childhood maltreatment, adverse life events, chronic medical illness and chronic pain, comorbid anxiety or substance use, and the postpartum period.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Mild episodes may respond to psychotherapy alone - <strong>cognitive behavioral therapy</strong> and interpersonal therapy have the strongest evidence - while moderate to severe episodes are best treated with a combination of psychotherapy and an antidepressant. First-line medications are SSRIs (escitalopram, sertraline, fluoxetine); SNRIs, bupropion, and mirtazapine are alternatives, with augmentation strategies for partial responders. <strong>Treatment-resistant depression</strong> may warrant ECT, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, or esketamine. Regular exercise, behavioral activation, and sleep regularization support recovery.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most serious complication is <strong>suicide</strong>. Others include <strong>treatment-resistant depression</strong>, chronic insomnia and fatigue, substance use disorders, and worsened outcomes of comorbid medical conditions such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Most depressive episodes are time-limited and treatable, but MDD is frequently recurrent, and each episode raises the likelihood of subsequent episodes. Prognosis is improved by early treatment, achieving full remission rather than partial response, continuation therapy to prevent relapse, and addressing comorbid anxiety and substance use; residual symptoms predict relapse.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>MDD has a lifetime prevalence on the order of 10-20% and is roughly twice as common in women as in men. It is among the leading global contributors to years lived with disability, typically first emerging in adolescence or young adulthood and following a recurrent course in a large proportion of those affected.</p>
