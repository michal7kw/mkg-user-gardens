<h1>Toxoplasmosis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Toxoplasmosis is an infection caused by <strong>Toxoplasma gondii</strong>, an obligate intracellular protozoan parasite with a worldwide distribution and one of the most prevalent chronic infections in humans. The definitive hosts are members of the cat family, in which the parasite completes its sexual cycle and sheds environmentally resistant oocysts in feces. Humans and other warm-blooded animals are intermediate hosts, acquiring infection by ingesting oocysts from contaminated soil, food, or water, or by consuming tissue cysts in undercooked meat. Vertical transmission from a newly infected mother to the fetus is a third important route.</p>
<p>In immunocompetent individuals, primary infection is most often <strong>asymptomatic or produces a mild, self-limiting illness</strong>, frequently manifesting only as painless lymphadenopathy. The immune system contains the infection by driving the rapidly dividing tachyzoites into dormant tissue cysts (bradyzoites), establishing lifelong latent infection. The clinical significance of toxoplasmosis lies disproportionately in two vulnerable groups: the <strong>immunocompromised</strong>, in whom reactivation can cause life-threatening encephalitis, and the <strong>developing fetus</strong>, in whom congenital infection can produce chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications, and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment.</p>
<p>Diagnosis hinges largely on serology—detecting Toxoplasma-specific IgM and IgG antibodies, with IgG avidity testing helping to date the infection and assess fetal risk in pregnancy—supplemented by molecular (PCR) testing of body fluids and, in immunocompromised patients, characteristic neuroimaging. Because most disease is preventable through food-safety and hygiene measures, public-health emphasis falls heavily on prevention, particularly during pregnancy.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The presentation varies dramatically with host immune status and the timing of infection:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Immunocompetent acute infection</strong>: usually subclinical; when symptomatic, presents as a flu-like illness with fever, malaise, and most characteristically painless, non-suppurative cervical lymphadenopathy</li>
<li><strong>Ocular toxoplasmosis</strong>: chorioretinitis causing blurred vision, floaters, photophobia, and eye pain, which may be congenital or acquired and tends to recur</li>
<li><strong>Cerebral toxoplasmosis (immunocompromised)</strong>: headache, fever, confusion, seizures, and focal neurologic deficits, typically from reactivation in advanced HIV/AIDS</li>
<li><strong>Congenital toxoplasmosis</strong>: the classic triad of chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, and intracranial calcifications, with severity inversely related to gestational age at maternal infection</li>
</ul>

<p>Serologic interpretation can be challenging because IgM may persist for months; reference-laboratory confirmation and avidity testing are recommended when timing matters, especially in pregnancy.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Principal risk factors are dietary and environmental: eating raw or undercooked meat, ingesting oocyst-contaminated produce or water, and contact with cat litter or contaminated soil. Host susceptibility modifies severity: <strong>immunosuppression</strong> (HIV/AIDS, transplantation, chemotherapy) predisposes to reactivation disease, and <strong>primary maternal infection during pregnancy</strong> carries the risk of vertical transmission and congenital disease.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Most immunocompetent patients with mild acute infection require no specific treatment. Active or severe disease is treated with <strong>pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine and leucovorin (folinic acid)</strong> to prevent bone-marrow suppression; this combination is the mainstay for ocular and cerebral toxoplasmosis. In pregnancy, <strong>spiramycin</strong> is used to reduce maternal-fetal transmission before fetal infection is confirmed, with pyrimethamine-sulfadiazine reserved for documented fetal infection after the first trimester. In HIV/AIDS, effective antiretroviral therapy plus primary or secondary prophylaxis is central to prevention and control.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most serious complications occur in vulnerable hosts: <strong>toxoplasmic encephalitis</strong> in advanced immunosuppression, recurrent <strong>ocular toxoplasmosis</strong> with progressive vision loss, and <strong>congenital toxoplasmosis</strong> with lasting neurologic and ophthalmologic sequelae. Disseminated multi-organ disease can develop in severely immunocompromised patients.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Acute infection in immunocompetent individuals resolves spontaneously with an excellent prognosis, though latent cysts persist for life. Prognosis is far more guarded in immunocompromised patients, where untreated cerebral disease can be fatal, and in congenital infection, where outcomes depend on the timing of maternal infection and the promptness of treatment.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Toxoplasma gondii infects roughly a third of the world's human population, with seroprevalence varying widely by region, dietary habits, and climate. Higher rates are seen where consumption of undercooked meat is common and in warm, humid environments favorable to oocyst survival. The burden of clinically significant disease is concentrated among immunocompromised individuals and congenitally infected infants.</p>
