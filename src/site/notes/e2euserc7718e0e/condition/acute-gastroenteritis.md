<h1>Acute Gastroenteritis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Acute gastroenteritis (AGE) is an acute inflammatory condition of the stomach and intestines characterized by the rapid onset of diarrhea, frequently accompanied by nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and fever. By convention the illness lasts less than 14 days; symptoms persisting longer are classified as persistent or chronic diarrhea and prompt a different evaluation. AGE is one of the most common acute illnesses worldwide and a major reason for outpatient visits, emergency presentations, and, in low-resource settings, childhood death.</p>
<p>Most cases are infectious and self-limited. In adults and children alike, viruses are the leading cause — norovirus dominates among older children and adults, while rotavirus historically dominated young children before widespread vaccination. Bacterial pathogens (<em>Salmonella</em>, <em>Campylobacter</em>, <em>Shigella</em>, <em>E. coli</em>) and protozoa account for a smaller but clinically important fraction, particularly when diarrhea is bloody or febrile. Transmission is overwhelmingly fecal-oral via contaminated food, water, or person-to-person contact, which is why outbreaks cluster in households, daycares, cruise ships, and long-term-care facilities.</p>
<p>The clinical danger of AGE lies less in the gastrointestinal symptoms themselves than in their consequence: fluid and electrolyte loss leading to dehydration. Because the illness is usually self-resolving, management centers on restoring and maintaining hydration — oral rehydration solution is the single most important and most cost-effective intervention. Antimicrobial and antimotility therapy are reserved for specific situations, and overuse is actively discouraged.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Patients present with the abrupt onset of loose or watery stools, often with nausea and vomiting that may precede the diarrhea, plus crampy abdominal pain, anorexia, and malaise. Fever is variable. The most important bedside assessment is hydration status — tachycardia, reduced urine output, dry mucous membranes, and orthostatic changes indicate significant volume loss. Red-flag features that suggest invasive bacterial disease or a non-infectious surgical cause include bloody stools, high fever, severe or localized abdominal pain, and toxicity.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>AGE is a clinical diagnosis in the otherwise healthy patient and does not require laboratory testing. Stool studies — culture, multiplex gastrointestinal PCR panels, and microscopy for ova and parasites — are reserved for severe, dysenteric, persistent, or immunocompromised presentations, or for public-health investigation of outbreaks. Basic labs (electrolytes, renal function) are useful when dehydration is significant.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Exposure to contaminated food or water and close-contact settings are the principal drivers of infection. Host vulnerability — extremes of age, immunocompromise, and reduced gastric acidity — raises both the likelihood of infection and the risk of severe dehydration. Poor sanitation and hand hygiene amplify transmission.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Oral rehydration solution is the foundation of care for all but the most severe cases and is effective across pathogens. Intravenous fluids are used for severe dehydration, shock, or intractable vomiting. Antiemetics such as ondansetron reduce vomiting and facilitate oral rehydration, particularly in children. Antibiotics are indicated only for specific bacterial or protozoal infections and should be avoided empirically; antimotility agents are contraindicated in bloody or febrile diarrhea. Early refeeding with an age-appropriate diet is encouraged once vomiting subsides.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Dehydration and hypovolemia are the leading complications, which in turn can produce electrolyte imbalance, metabolic acidosis, and acute kidney injury. Specific pathogens carry distinct risks — Shiga toxin-producing <em>E. coli</em> can cause hemolytic uremic syndrome — and a subset of patients develop post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>The prognosis is excellent for immunocompetent patients with timely rehydration; most recover within a few days to a week. Morbidity and mortality concentrate in young children, the elderly, and the immunocompromised, almost always driven by dehydration.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>AGE accounts for billions of episodes globally each year and remains a leading cause of death among young children in low-income regions, predominantly from dehydration. Norovirus is the most common cause of sporadic disease and outbreaks across all ages in high-income countries; rotavirus burden has fallen substantially where vaccination is widespread.</p>
