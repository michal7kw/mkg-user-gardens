<h1>Kidney Cancer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Kidney cancer encompasses malignancies originating in the kidney, with renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounting for roughly 90% of adult cases; clear-cell RCC is the dominant histological subtype, followed by papillary and chromophobe types. RCC arises from the epithelium of the renal tubules, and clear-cell tumors are characteristically driven by inactivation of the VHL tumor-suppressor gene, which leads to constitutive activation of hypoxia-inducible factor and downstream pro-angiogenic signaling — the biological rationale for therapies targeting the VEGF pathway. A separate, rarer cancer of the renal collecting system (urothelial carcinoma of the renal pelvis) is distinct from RCC and behaves more like bladder cancer.</p>
<p>A striking feature of modern kidney cancer is that a large and growing proportion of tumors are discovered incidentally, when imaging performed for unrelated reasons reveals an enhancing renal mass. As a result, the classic triad of flank pain, hematuria, and a palpable mass now represents a minority of advanced presentations. This incidental detection has shifted the stage distribution toward smaller, localized tumors that are often curable with surgery, and has expanded the role of nephron-sparing approaches and active surveillance for very small masses in older or comorbid patients.</p>
<p>The clinical landscape of advanced kidney cancer has been transformed by systemic therapy. Cytotoxic chemotherapy is largely ineffective in RCC, but the disease is uniquely responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitors and to targeted agents directed at angiogenesis (VEGF tyrosine kinase inhibitors) and the mTOR pathway. Combinations of immunotherapy with targeted therapy are now standard for many patients with metastatic disease, substantially improving survival compared with earlier eras.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Most localized tumors are asymptomatic and found incidentally. When symptoms occur, they include hematuria, flank pain, and a palpable mass (the now-uncommon classic triad), along with constitutional features such as weight loss, fatigue, and fever. RCC is notable for paraneoplastic syndromes, including hypertension, hypercalcemia, polycythemia, and anemia.</p>

<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Established risk factors include cigarette smoking, obesity, hypertension, chronic kidney disease and long-term dialysis (with acquired cystic disease), certain occupational exposures, and a family history or hereditary syndromes such as von Hippel-Lindau disease.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Localized disease is managed surgically, with partial (nephron-sparing) nephrectomy preferred for small renal masses to preserve kidney function, and radical nephrectomy reserved for larger or central tumors. Active surveillance and thermal ablation are options for selected small, low-risk masses. Advanced or metastatic RCC is treated with systemic therapy, principally immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with VEGF-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors, with mTOR inhibitors as additional options.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Complications include local invasion with venous tumor thrombus extending into the renal vein or inferior vena cava, distant metastasis to lung, bone, liver, and brain, paraneoplastic syndromes, and loss of renal function following nephrectomy, particularly in patients with pre-existing chronic kidney disease.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis depends strongly on stage, tumor grade, and histological subtype. Localized RCC has high cure rates after surgery, whereas metastatic disease historically carried a poor outlook — though survival has improved markedly with modern immunotherapy and targeted combinations. Clear-cell histology, higher grade, and sarcomatoid features predict worse outcomes.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Kidney cancer is among the more common cancers in adults, with incidence rising in part due to increased incidental detection by imaging. It is more frequent in men and in older adults, and its incidence parallels the prevalence of smoking, obesity, and hypertension.</p>
