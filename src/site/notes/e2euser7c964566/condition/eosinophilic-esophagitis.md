<h1>Eosinophilic Esophagitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a chronic, immune- and antigen-mediated inflammatory disease of the esophagus. It is defined by symptoms of esophageal dysfunction together with a dense, eosinophil-predominant inflammatory infiltrate confined to the esophageal mucosa. First clearly delineated as a distinct entity in the 1990s, EoE has since been recognized as the most common cause of dysphagia and food impaction in children and young adults, and its incidence has risen sharply over recent decades.</p>
<p>The disease is driven largely by an allergic, T-helper-2 (Th2) immune response to ingested food antigens, and to a lesser extent aeroallergens. Exposure triggers cytokine signaling (IL-5, IL-13) that recruits eosinophils to the esophageal epithelium, where their products promote inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and, over time, subepithelial fibrosis. This explains both the strong association of EoE with other atopic diseases — food allergy, asthma, atopic dermatitis, and allergic rhinitis — and the value of dietary and anti-inflammatory therapies.</p>
<p>Clinically, EoE matters because untreated inflammation progresses from an inflammatory phenotype to a fibrostenotic one, producing rings, strictures, and a narrowed, rigid esophagus that causes dysphagia and food impaction. Because the condition is chronic and relapsing, the goals of care are to control symptoms, normalize histology, and prevent the long-term remodeling that leads to strictures.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Presentation is age-dependent. Adolescents and adults most often present with dysphagia for solid food and episodes of food impaction, sometimes with chest pain and heartburn that does not respond to acid suppression. Many patients have developed subtle compensatory behaviors — eating slowly, chewing excessively, avoiding hard textures, and washing food down with liquids — that can mask the severity of their dysphagia. In infants and young children, the disease more commonly presents with feeding difficulty, vomiting, abdominal pain, and failure to thrive.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis requires both clinical and histologic criteria: symptoms of esophageal dysfunction plus an esophageal biopsy demonstrating at least 15 eosinophils per high-power field, after exclusion of other causes of esophageal eosinophilia. Upper endoscopy (EGD) with multiple biopsies along the esophagus is essential because the disease can be patchy. Characteristic endoscopic findings, graded by the EREFS classification, include fixed rings (&quot;trachealization&quot;), longitudinal furrows, white exudates, edema, and strictures. Allergy testing may help identify dietary triggers but does not establish the diagnosis.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>EoE is strongly associated with personal and family atopy, including food allergy, asthma, atopic dermatitis, and allergic rhinitis. It shows a male predominance and is more frequently reported in Caucasian populations. Sensitization to common food antigens — particularly milk, wheat, egg, and soy — is central, and genetic predisposition (such as variants influencing eotaxin-3/CCL26 expression) contributes to susceptibility.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management uses three complementary strategies, often summarized as &quot;diet, drugs, and dilation.&quot; Proton pump inhibitors are a recognized first-line anti-inflammatory therapy and induce histologic remission in a substantial subset. Topical corticosteroids, swallowed rather than inhaled (e.g., budesonide or fluticasone formulations), directly suppress esophageal inflammation. Dietary elimination — either empiric removal of common trigger foods or allergy-test-directed elimination — can achieve remission without medication. For patients with established fibrostenotic strictures, endoscopic dilation relieves dysphagia. Refractory disease may be treated with biologic agents targeting the Th2 axis, such as dupilumab.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The principal complications stem from chronic inflammation and fibrosis: esophageal strictures and luminal narrowing, recurrent food impaction requiring endoscopic removal, and progressive loss of esophageal compliance. Esophageal perforation is a rare but serious event, particularly during impaction or instrumentation. In children, persistent disease can impair nutrition and growth.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>EoE is a chronic, relapsing condition; symptoms and inflammation typically return when therapy is stopped, so long-term maintenance treatment is usually required. With appropriate anti-inflammatory or dietary therapy, most patients achieve good symptom control and histologic remission, and early, sustained treatment reduces progression to the fibrostenotic phenotype.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Once considered rare, EoE is now a well-recognized disease with rising incidence and prevalence across high-income countries, attributed both to greater awareness and a genuine increase in cases. It affects all ages but is most often diagnosed in children and young to middle-aged adults, with a marked male predominance.</p>
