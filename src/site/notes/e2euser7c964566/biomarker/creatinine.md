<h1>Creatinine</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Creatinine is a low-molecular-weight nitrogenous waste product (113 Da) formed by the spontaneous, non-enzymatic dehydration of creatine and creatine phosphate in skeletal muscle. Because roughly 1-2% of the body's creatine pool is converted to creatinine each day at a remarkably stable rate, the amount produced is largely a function of total muscle mass. This steady production, combined with predictable renal handling, makes serum creatinine the single most widely used routine indicator of kidney function in clinical practice.</p>
<p>Creatinine is freely filtered at the glomerulus and is neither reabsorbed nor significantly metabolized by the tubules, with only a small fraction (10-20%) actively secreted by the proximal tubule. As a result, serum concentration rises when glomerular filtration falls. The relationship between serum creatinine and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is inverse and non-linear: creatinine may remain within the reference interval until a substantial portion of renal function is already lost, so it is an insensitive marker of early or mild kidney impairment. To compensate, laboratories report a creatinine-based estimated GFR (eGFR) calculated from equations (such as the 2021 race-free CKD-EPI creatinine equation) that incorporate age and sex as surrogates for muscle mass.</p>
<p>Clinically, creatinine is measured to screen for and stage chronic kidney disease (CKD), detect acute kidney injury (AKI), monitor nephrotoxic drug therapy, adjust renally cleared medication dosing, and assess hydration and perfusion. It is interpreted together with blood urea nitrogen (BUN), cystatin C, electrolytes, and urinalysis. Because production depends on muscle, the same absolute value can mean very different things in a frail older woman versus a muscular young athlete; for this reason creatinine should rarely be interpreted in isolation.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals vary by laboratory and assay. The Jaffe (colorimetric) method is prone to interference from chromogens (e.g., ketones, certain drugs), while enzymatic assays are more specific; values are now standardized to IDMS-traceable calibration.</li>
<li>Serum creatinine alone is insensitive to early disease — always interpret alongside eGFR and, where available, cystatin C.</li>
<li>Levels are higher in individuals with greater muscle mass and lower in those with reduced muscle mass (older adults, amputees, malnutrition), independent of true kidney function.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Creatinine:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reduced glomerular filtration (acute or chronic kidney disease)</li>
<li>Dehydration and reduced renal perfusion (pre-renal azotemia)</li>
<li>Urinary tract obstruction (post-renal cause)</li>
<li>Rhabdomyolysis and severe muscle injury (release of muscle creatine)</li>
<li>High dietary protein / large cooked-meat intake (preformed creatinine)</li>
<li>High muscle mass (athletes, bodybuilders)</li>
<li>Creatine supplementation (increased substrate for creatinine formation)</li>
<li>Nephrotoxic or filtration-altering drugs (e.g., NSAIDs, aminoglycosides; trimethoprim and cimetidine block tubular secretion, raising measured creatinine without true GFR change)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Creatinine:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Low muscle mass / sarcopenia / cachexia</li>
<li>Advanced liver disease (reduced hepatic creatine synthesis)</li>
<li>Pregnancy (increased GFR and plasma volume)</li>
<li>Malnutrition and low protein intake</li>
<li>Overhydration / volume expansion</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Chronic Kidney Disease</strong> — sustained elevation with reduced eGFR over ≥3 months defines and stages CKD; creatinine is the backbone of CKD classification and monitoring.</li>
<li><strong>Acute Kidney Injury</strong> — a rapid rise in serum creatinine (e.g., ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours or ≥1.5× baseline) is a defining criterion of AKI under KDIGO/RIFLE staging.</li>
<li><strong>Urinary Tract Obstruction</strong> — post-renal back-pressure impairs filtration and raises creatinine, often reversible if relieved promptly.</li>
<li><strong>Rhabdomyolysis</strong> — massive muscle breakdown releases creatine and causes pigment nephropathy, elevating creatinine disproportionately.</li>
<li><strong>Diabetic Kidney Disease</strong> — progressive nephropathy in diabetes is tracked by rising creatinine and falling eGFR alongside albuminuria.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low muscle mass / sarcopenia</strong> — may mask true renal impairment because production is diminished; cystatin C is a useful complement in such patients.</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease and malnutrition</strong> — reduced creatine synthesis lowers baseline creatinine.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Staging and tracking progression of CKD (paired with eGFR and albuminuria).</li>
<li>Detecting AKI in hospitalized, critically ill, or post-surgical patients.</li>
<li>Renal dose-adjustment and safety monitoring of nephrotoxic or renally cleared drugs.</li>
<li>Assessing volume status and pre-renal causes via the BUN:creatinine ratio (a ratio &gt;20:1 suggests pre-renal azotemia/dehydration).</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>These strategies aim to preserve or improve kidney function so that creatinine reflects healthy filtration; they are not about lowering the number cosmetically.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Maintain adequate hydration</strong> — correcting dehydration restores renal perfusion and lowers pre-renal creatinine elevations.</li>
<li><strong>Optimize blood pressure and glycemic control</strong> — managing hypertension and diabetes is the most important measure to slow nephron loss; RAAS-blocking agents are protective in proteinuric CKD (under medical supervision).</li>
<li><strong>Moderate dietary protein</strong> — excessive protein and large cooked-meat meals transiently raise creatinine; a balanced, not excessive, protein intake reduces filtration burden in CKD.</li>
<li><strong>Avoid unnecessary nephrotoxins</strong> — limit chronic NSAID use, ensure appropriate dosing of aminoglycosides and contrast agents, and review medications periodically.</li>
<li><strong>Interpret creatine supplementation in context</strong> — creatine modestly raises serum creatinine through increased substrate, not kidney harm; meta-analyses find no adverse effect on kidney function in healthy individuals, but supplement use should be disclosed before kidney testing.</li>
<li><strong>Use complementary markers</strong> — pairing creatinine with cystatin C and eGFR avoids over- or under-estimating function in people with atypical muscle mass.</li>
</ol>
