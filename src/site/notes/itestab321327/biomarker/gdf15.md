<h1>GDF15</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15; also called MIC-1, macrophage inhibitory cytokine-1) is a distant, divergent member of the TGF-beta superfamily that behaves as a cell-stress cytokine. Unlike most metabolic hormones, GDF15 has a single known central receptor -- <strong>GFRAL</strong>, restricted to the hindbrain (area postrema and nucleus tractus solitarius) -- through which it <strong>suppresses appetite</strong> and conveys a signal of &quot;somatic distress&quot; to the brain. Its expression is induced by mitochondrial dysfunction, the integrated stress response, inflammation, tissue injury, and metabolic stress.</p>
<p>Clinically, GDF15 is notable on two fronts: it is among the <strong>strongest single-protein predictors of all-cause mortality and biological aging</strong> (alongside IL-6, cystatin C, and N-terminal pro-BNP), and it is the <strong>mediator of nausea/appetite-loss</strong> in several contexts -- pregnancy (especially hyperemesis gravidarum), mitochondrial disease, bariatric surgery, and metformin therapy. This dual identity -- robust aging/stress biomarker and actionable appetite axis -- makes it a focus of both longevity research and anti-obesity drug development (GDF15 analogs and GFRAL agonists in clinical trials).</p>
<p>Mechanistically, GDF15 expression is induced by the <strong>integrated stress response (ISR)</strong>, particularly via the eIF2alpha-ATF4-CHOP pathway. Mitochondrial dysfunction (including that caused by metformin's complex I inhibition) is a major driver. The receptor GFRAL is exclusively expressed in the area postrema and nucleus tractus solitarius of the hindbrain; GDF15-GFRAL signaling suppresses appetite via brainstem and hypothalamic circuits. GDF15 also has peripheral effects on inflammation, lipolysis, and thermogenesis.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (well-validated as a stress / aging biomarker; therapeutic applications emerging).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Strongly age-dependent -- always interpret against age norms</li>
<li>Assay-dependent; no universally standardized reference range</li>
<li>Transiently elevated by exercise (within 1-2 h) and metformin (chronic, ~50-100% elevation)</li>
<li>Sex differences: women slightly higher than men before menopause</li>
<li>Pregnancy: markedly elevated (especially in hyperemesis gravidarum)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase GDF15:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Aging</strong> (the principal non-modifiable determinant; ~2-3% per year)</li>
<li><strong>Mitochondrial dysfunction / integrated stress response</strong> (the mechanistic basis)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic illness</strong> (heart failure, CKD, COPD, cancer, liver disease)</li>
<li><strong>Cancer</strong> (especially cachexia; GDF15 is a driver of cancer anorexia-cachexia syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular disease</strong> (heart failure, acute coronary syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Diabetes / metabolic syndrome</strong></li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (especially hyperemesis gravidarum -- GDF15 is a major driver of nausea/vomiting)</li>
<li><strong>Mitochondrial disease</strong> (PMID: 28219989; muscle weakness, exercise intolerance)</li>
<li><strong>Acute illness, sepsis, surgery</strong></li>
<li><strong>Exercise</strong> (transient; ~2x within 1-2 h; adaptive stress signaling)</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction / fasting</strong> (mild adaptive increase)</li>
<li><strong>Metformin</strong> (chronic elevation ~50-100%; contributes to GI side effects)</li>
<li><strong>Bariatric surgery</strong> (substantial increase; may contribute to appetite suppression and weight loss)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease GDF15:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Younger age</strong></li>
<li><strong>Healthy lifestyle</strong> (regular exercise; chronic effect to lower baseline)</li>
<li><strong>Improved metabolic health</strong> (weight loss, glycemic control, lipid optimization)</li>
<li><strong>Recovery from acute illness</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated GDF15 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>All-cause mortality:</strong> among the strongest single-protein predictors (HR ~1.5-2.5 per SD)</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular disease and heart failure:</strong> independent predictor of incident HF, MACE, and mortality (after adjustment for traditional risk factors)</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome:</strong> GDF15 tracks with insulin resistance</li>
<li><strong>Cancer cachexia:</strong> GDF15 is a major driver of cancer anorexia-cachexia syndrome; anti-GDF15 therapies in development</li>
<li><strong>Frailty and sarcopenia in elderly</strong></li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy complications:</strong> hyperemesis gravidarum (markedly elevated GDF15); marker of pregnancy morbidity</li>
<li><strong>Mitochondrial disease:</strong> GDF15 is a sensitive biomarker of mitochondrial dysfunction (PMID: 28219989)</li>
<li><strong>CKD progression:</strong> rises with eGFR decline</li>
<li><strong>Acute illness, sepsis:</strong> GDF15 rises with severity</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Not in clinical guidelines</strong> -- research and longevity-medicine biomarker only as of 2025</li>
<li><strong>Multiple physiological roles</strong> make interpretation context-dependent</li>
<li><strong>Strong age-dependence</strong> requires age-stratified reference ranges</li>
<li><strong>No interventional trials</strong> in humans showing that lowering GDF15 improves hard clinical outcomes (the GDF15-GFRAL axis is the therapeutic target, not GDF15 itself)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Research: aging / longevity biomarker (alongside Klotho, p16INK4a, DNA methylation clocks)</li>
<li>Functional medicine: metabolic stress panel</li>
<li>Mitochondrial disease evaluation (with lactate, pyruvate, FGF21)</li>
<li>Cancer cachexia assessment (with weight loss trajectory)</li>
<li>Heart failure prognostication (with NT-proBNP)</li>
<li>Research: response to metformin, GLP-1 agonist, or bariatric surgery</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>GDF15 reflects underlying cellular stress; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat underlying chronic disease:</strong> heart failure (GDMT), CKD (SGLT2i, ACEi/ARB), diabetes (weight loss, GLP-1, metformin), cancer (oncology referral)</li>
<li><strong>Regular aerobic exercise</strong> -- paradoxically lowers baseline GDF15 despite acute transient rises (chronic adaptation to training reduces baseline stress signaling)</li>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> in obesity -- reduces chronic metabolic stress and inflammation</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong> rich in anti-inflammatory foods</li>
<li><strong>Sleep optimization</strong> -- sleep deprivation elevates GDF15</li>
<li><strong>Stress management</strong> -- chronic cortisol elevation drives GDF15</li>
<li><strong>Avoid excessive caloric restriction</strong> -- chronic starvation raises GDF15 via integrated stress response</li>
<li><strong>Treat mitochondrial dysfunction:</strong> CoQ10, L-carnitine, alpha-lipoic acid, NAD+ precursors (research-stage)</li>
<li><strong>Note on metformin:</strong> metformin raises GDF15 substantially (~50-100%); this is thought to be an ADAPTIVE beneficial response (mediates metformin's anti-diabetes and possible anti-aging effects via appetite suppression) -- do NOT discontinue metformin for elevated GDF15</li>
<li><strong>Pharmacological GDF15-GFRAL axis modulation</strong> (in clinical trials): GDF15 analogs and GFRAL agonists for obesity and cancer cachexia</li>
</ol>
