<h1>Neurogenic Bladder</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Neurogenic bladder (NGB) is bladder dysfunction resulting from <strong>neurological disease or injury</strong> affecting the complex neural pathways controlling bladder storage and micturition. The bladder requires coordinated function of the <strong>detrusor muscle</strong> (smooth muscle of the bladder wall), the <strong>internal urethral sphincter</strong> (smooth muscle; sympathetic control), and the <strong>external urethral sphincter</strong> (skeletal muscle; somatic control via pudendal nerve).</p>
<p><strong>Classification by neurological lesion level:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Suprapontine lesions (stroke, brain tumor, PD):</strong> loss of cortical inhibition -&gt; <strong>detrusor overactivity</strong> (urge incontinence; stroke) or <strong>detrusor underactivity</strong> (PD)</li>
<li><strong>Suprasacral spinal cord lesions (SCI above S2-S4; MS):</strong> preserved reflex arc -&gt; <strong>detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD)</strong> (detrusor contracts against closed sphincter; high-pressure bladder; risk of hydronephrosis); initially flaccid during spinal shock, then spastic</li>
<li><strong>Sacral/peripheral lesions (cauda equina, pelvic surgery, diabetes):</strong> destruction of reflex arc -&gt; <strong>detrusor areflexia</strong> (urinary retention; overflow incontinence; palpable distended bladder)</li>
</ul>
<p>The principal goals of management are: (1) <strong>protect the upper urinary tract</strong> (kidneys) from high-pressure damage, (2) <strong>prevent UTIs</strong>, (3) <strong>achieve social continence</strong>, and (4) <strong>improve quality of life</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC)</strong> is the gold standard for bladder emptying in most NGB patients with significant retention. It is self-administered 4-6 times daily and dramatically reduces UTI rates compared to indwelling catheters.</p>
<p><strong>Autonomic dysreflexia</strong> is a life-threatening complication of NGB in patients with spinal cord injury above T6. Bladder distension (or other noxious stimuli below the lesion) triggers massive sympathetic discharge -&gt; severe hypertension, bradycardia, headache, sweating. Treatment: sit upright, identify and remove the trigger (catheterize the bladder), and administer antihypertensives (nifedipine, nitrates).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>For detrusor overactivity (spastic bladder):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Anticholinergics:</strong> oxybutynin 5-10 mg BID, tolterodine 2 mg BID, solifenacin 5-10 mg daily</li>
<li><strong>Beta-3 agonist:</strong> mirabegron 25-50 mg daily (fewer cognitive side effects)</li>
<li><strong>Intravesical botulinum toxin:</strong> 200-300 units injected into detrusor (q6-12 months; highly effective for refractory cases)</li>
<li><strong>Sacral neuromodulation (InterStim):</strong> for refractory urge incontinence</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>For detrusor areflexia (flaccid bladder):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC):</strong> gold standard; self-catheterize 4-6x/day</li>
<li><strong>Alpha-blockers</strong> (tamsulosin 0.4 mg): relax bladder neck to facilitate emptying</li>
<li><strong>Crede maneuver</strong> (manual suprapubic pressure) -- less effective but may help</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>For DSD (suprasacral SCI):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Combine anticholinergic (for storage) + CIC (for emptying)</li>
<li><strong>Alpha-blockers</strong> (tamsulosin) for sphincter relaxation</li>
<li>Botulinum toxin to external sphincter (select cases)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>For all patients:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Annual renal ultrasound (monitor for hydronephrosis, stones)</li>
<li>Annual creatinine (monitor renal function)</li>
<li>Pneumococcal, influenza, and COVID vaccination</li>
<li>Prompt antibiotic treatment for symptomatic UTIs (asymptomatic bacteriuria NOT treated unless pregnant or undergoing urologic procedures)</li>
</ul>
