<h1>Gastrointestinal Bleeding</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding is <strong>any hemorrhage from the gastrointestinal tract</strong>, ranging from the <strong>mouth to the anus</strong>, and is one of the most common medical emergencies. Classification is anatomical: <strong>upper GI bleeding</strong> (proximal to the ligament of Treitz) presents with <strong>hematemesis</strong> (vomiting blood; &quot;coffee-ground&quot; if altered or frank red if brisk) and/or <strong>melena</strong> (black, tarry, foul-smelling stools from digested blood). <strong>Lower GI bleeding</strong> (distal to the ligament of Treitz) typically presents with <strong>hematochezia</strong> (bright red or maroon blood per rectum), though very brisk upper GI bleeding can also cause hematochezia. <strong>Obscure GI bleeding</strong> is persistent or recurrent bleeding without an identified source on standard endoscopy.</p>
<p>The causes vary by site and age. <strong>Upper GI bleeding</strong> is most often from <strong>peptic ulcer disease</strong> (~50% of cases; duodenal &gt; gastric), followed by <strong>erosive esophagitis</strong> (GERD), <strong>Mallory-Weiss tear</strong> (from retching), <strong>esophagogastric varices</strong> (from portal hypertension in cirrhosis; highest mortality), <strong>gastritis</strong> (NSAID- or alcohol-induced), and <strong>upper GI malignancy</strong> (gastric cancer). <strong>Lower GI bleeding</strong> in older adults is most often from <strong>diverticulosis</strong> (~40% of cases; right-sided diverticula bleed more), <strong>angiodysplasia</strong> (vascular ectasias; associated with aortic stenosis -- Heyde syndrome), <strong>colorectal cancer or polyps</strong>, <strong>ischemic colitis</strong>, <strong>inflammatory bowel disease</strong>, and <strong>hemorrhoids</strong>. In children, common causes are <strong>intussusception, Meckel diverticulum, and juvenile polyps</strong>.</p>
<p>The clinical priorities are <strong>resuscitation, source identification, and hemostasis</strong>. Severity is assessed by <strong>hemodynamic stability</strong> (heart rate, blood pressure, orthostatic vital signs, shock index), <strong>rate and volume of bleeding</strong>, and <strong>comorbidities</strong> (elderly, cardiovascular disease, cirrhosis). Risk stratification tools like the <strong>Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS)</strong> for upper GI bleeding and <strong>Oakland Score</strong> for lower GI bleeding guide the need for transfusion, hospitalization, and endoscopy timing. Overall mortality for acute upper GI bleeding is <strong>~10%</strong> (rising to <strong>15-25% for variceal bleeding</strong>), reflecting the elderly and comorbid populations affected.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Resuscitation (all patients, immediate):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Two large-bore IVs</strong> (18 gauge or larger); consider central line if unstable</li>
<li><strong>Crystalloid</strong> (normal saline or lactated Ringer's) bolus 500 mL; reassess; repeat to hemodynamic stability</li>
<li><strong>Transfuse packed RBCs</strong> to hemoglobin <strong>&gt;7 g/dL</strong> (&gt;8 g/dL if active bleeding, cardiovascular disease, or elderly)</li>
<li><strong>Type and crossmatch</strong> early (2-4 units for moderate-severe bleeding)</li>
<li><strong>Monitor</strong>: vitals, urine output (Foley), mental status, hemoglobin every 4-6 hours</li>
<li><strong>Airway protection</strong> if hematemesis with altered mental status (avoid aspiration)</li>
<li><strong>Reverse anticoagulation</strong> if severe bleeding: prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) for warfarin; specific reversal agents (andexanet alfa for apixaban/rivaroxaban; idarucizumab for dabigatran); platelet transfusion for antiplatelets</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Identify the source:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>History and exam</strong> -- hematemesis vs hematochezia; pain; medications (NSAIDs, anticoagulants); alcohol; comorbidities; <strong>nasogastric aspirate</strong> can help distinguish upper from lower</li>
<li><strong>Upper endoscopy</strong> (EGD) -- within <strong>24 hours</strong> for upper GI bleed; allows diagnosis and therapy</li>
<li><strong>Colonoscopy</strong> -- for lower GI bleed; ideally after bowel preparation, but urgent (unprepped) colonoscopy for massive bleeding</li>
<li><strong>Capsule endoscopy</strong> and <strong>device-assisted enteroscopy</strong> for obscure bleeding (small bowel)</li>
<li><strong>CT angiography</strong> -- for massive, ongoing bleeding; identifies site for embolization</li>
<li><strong>Tagged RBC scan</strong> -- for intermittent, slow bleeding</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Upper GI bleeding -- specific therapies:</strong></p>
<p><strong>Non-variceal (peptic ulcer):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Proton pump inhibitor infusion</strong> -- <strong>pantoprazole 80 mg IV bolus, then 8 mg/h continuous infusion x 72 hours</strong> (reduces rebleeding and mortality)</li>
<li><strong>Endoscopic hemostasis</strong> -- epinephrine injection + <strong>hemoclip</strong> or <strong>thermal coagulation</strong> for actively bleeding or visible-vessel ulcers ( Forrest classification Ia-IIb)</li>
<li><strong>H. pylori eradication</strong> -- test and treat; reduces rebleeding</li>
<li><strong>Discontinue NSAIDs</strong> (resume with PPI co-therapy when stable, if needed)</li>
<li><strong>Resume aspirin</strong> for secondary cardiovascular prevention as soon as hemostasis achieved (~24-48 hours)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Variceal hemorrhage (highest mortality):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Octreotide 50 mcg IV bolus, then 50 mcg/h infusion x 3-5 days</strong> (splanchnic vasoconstriction)</li>
<li><strong>Antibiotics</strong> -- <strong>ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily x 7 days</strong> (prophylaxis against bacterial infections; reduces mortality ~10%)</li>
<li><strong>Endoscopic band ligation</strong> within 12 hours</li>
<li><strong>Balloon tamponade</strong> (Blakemore tube) or <strong>self-expanding metal stent</strong> for refractory bleeding as bridge to definitive therapy</li>
<li><strong>Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS)</strong> for failed endoscopic therapy or early rebleeding in high-risk patients</li>
<li><strong>Propranolol or nadolol</strong> for secondary prophylaxis after control</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Lower GI bleeding -- specific therapies:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Colonoscopic hemostasis</strong> (epinephrine injection, hemoclips, thermal coagulation) for diverticular, post-polypectomy, or angiodysplastic bleeding</li>
<li><strong>Angiographic embolization</strong> -- first-line for refractory or massive colonic bleeding (more common than surgery)</li>
<li><strong>Surgery</strong> (segmental colectomy) for refractory bleeding</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Obscure GI bleeding:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Capsule endoscopy</strong> first-line for small bowel evaluation</li>
<li><strong>Device-assisted enteroscopy</strong> (double-balloon, single-balloon, spiral) for diagnosis and therapy</li>
<li><strong>CT enterography</strong> for small bowel masses</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Long-term management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Confirm H. pylori eradication</strong> (urea breath test 4 weeks post-treatment)</li>
<li><strong>Long-term PPI</strong> for patients on NSAIDs or antiplatelets</li>
<li><strong>Beta-blocker or repeat band ligation</strong> for secondary prophylaxis of varices</li>
<li><strong>Surveillance colonoscopy</strong> per guidelines (3 years after index bleed for adenoma; 1 year for incomplete resection)</li>
<li>Address <strong>iron deficiency anemia</strong> (oral or IV iron)</li>
<li><strong>Risk factor modification</strong> -- smoking cessation, alcohol moderation</li>
</ul>
