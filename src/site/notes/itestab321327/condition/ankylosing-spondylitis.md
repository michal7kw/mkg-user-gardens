<h1>Ankylosing Spondylitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory arthritis belonging to the family of <strong>axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA)</strong> -- conditions unified by inflammation of the <strong>spine and sacroiliac joints</strong>, association with <strong>HLA-B27</strong>, and features of <strong>enthesitis</strong> (inflammation at tendon and ligament insertions into bone), <strong>dactylitis</strong> (sausage digits), and <strong>extra-articular manifestations</strong> (uveitis, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease). AS affects approximately <strong>0.2-0.5% of the general population</strong>, with <strong>2-3x male predominance</strong> (though this gap is narrowing as milder disease in women is increasingly recognized). Typical onset is in the <strong>late teens to 30s</strong>.</p>
<p>The hallmark clinical feature is <strong>inflammatory back pain</strong>, distinguished from mechanical back pain by: <strong>age of onset &lt;40, insidious onset, improvement with exercise (not rest), pain worse in the second half of the night, and morning stiffness &gt;30 minutes</strong>. The pain typically begins in the <strong>sacroiliac joints</strong> (alternating buttock pain) and ascends the spine. Untreated, chronic inflammation at entheses leads to <strong>new bone formation</strong> (syndesmophytes), progressive <strong>spinal fusion</strong> (&quot;bamboo spine&quot;), loss of spinal mobility, and restrictive lung disease from costovertebral joint involvement. <strong>Acute anterior uveitis</strong> (iritis) occurs in ~25-30% of patients at some point -- a unilateral red, painful, photophobic eye requiring urgent ophthalmology referral.</p>
<p>The pathogenesis involves a <strong>tripartite interaction of genetics (HLA-B27), immune dysregulation (Th17/IL-23 axis, TNF), and environmental triggers (microbiome, mechanical stress)</strong>. The role of HLA-B27 is incompletely understood but may involve <strong>misfolding-induced unfolded protein response</strong> or <strong>arthritis peptide presentation</strong>. The <strong>enthesis</strong> is the primary site of pathology -- mechanical stress triggers inflammation that, paradoxically, heals with excessive new bone formation, producing the characteristic ankylosis. Diagnosis uses the <strong>ASAS classification criteria</strong> (presence of sacroiliitis on imaging plus back pain, OR HLA-B27 plus 2+ features of spondyloarthritis). <strong>MRI</strong> is now central to early diagnosis, detecting <strong>active inflammatory lesions</strong> (bone marrow edema) in the sacroiliac joints before radiographic changes appear.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Non-pharmacologic (cornerstone for all patients):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Physical therapy and regular exercise</strong> -- <strong>the single most important intervention</strong>; maintains spinal mobility, posture, and chest expansion; reduces pain and stiffness. Daily stretching, posture exercises, swimming, and deep breathing are emphasized</li>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong> -- smoking accelerates radiographic progression and reduces biologic efficacy</li>
<li><strong>Patient education</strong> -- chronic nature, importance of lifelong exercise,姿势 management</li>
<li><strong>Ergonomic adjustments</strong> at work and home</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pharmacologic therapy:</strong></p>
<p><strong>NSAIDs (first-line for all patients):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Continuous NSAID therapy</strong> at full anti-inflammatory dose -- <strong>naproxen 500 mg BID</strong>, <strong>diclofenac 75 mg BID</strong>, <strong>celecoxib 200 mg BID</strong>, <strong>ibuprofen 800 mg TID</strong></li>
<li>Continuous (rather than PRN) use may <strong>slow radiographic progression</strong> (debatable but supported by some evidence)</li>
<li><strong>COX-2 selective</strong> (celecoxib) preferred for lower GI risk</li>
<li>Caution: renal, cardiovascular, GI risk</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>TNF inhibitors (second-line, for NSAID-resistant disease):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adalimumab</strong> 40 mg SC every 2 weeks</li>
<li><strong>Etanercept</strong> 50 mg SC weekly</li>
<li><strong>Infliximab</strong> 5 mg/kg IV every 6-8 weeks</li>
<li><strong>Golimumab</strong> 50 mg SC monthly</li>
<li><strong>Certolizumab pegol</strong> 400 mg SC every 2-4 weeks (preferred in pregnancy -- minimal placental transfer)</li>
<li>Highly effective for axial and peripheral symptoms, enthesitis, uveitis; rapid response (2-12 weeks)</li>
<li>Screen for <strong>TB and hepatitis B</strong> before starting; monitor for infections</li>
<li>~<strong>70-80% response rate</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>IL-17 inhibitors (alternative biologic):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Secukinumab</strong> 150-300 mg SC monthly (after loading)</li>
<li><strong>Ixekizumab</strong> 80 mg SC monthly (after loading)</li>
<li><strong>Brodalumab</strong>, <strong>netakimab</strong>, <strong>bimekizumab</strong> (newer)</li>
<li>Effective for axial, peripheral, and psoriasis manifestations</li>
<li><strong>Do NOT combine with TNF inhibitors</strong> (no added benefit, more side effects)</li>
<li>Caution: <strong>inflammatory bowel disease</strong> (IL-17 inhibitors may worsen IBD)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>JAK inhibitors (third-line):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Upadacitinib</strong> 15 mg daily</li>
<li><strong>Tofacitinib</strong> 5 mg BID</li>
<li>Effective for both axial and peripheral arthritis; oral route convenient</li>
<li><strong>Boxed warning</strong>: thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, cardiovascular events, malignancy, infection (especially in &gt;50 years with cardiovascular risk factors)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Conventional DMARDs (limited axial benefit):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sulfasalazine</strong> 2-3 g/day -- for <strong>peripheral arthritis</strong> (limited benefit for axial disease)</li>
<li><strong>Methotrexate</strong> -- limited evidence; may be combined with TNF inhibitor</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Other therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Local corticosteroid injections</strong> -- sacroiliac joint, entheses (Achilles, plantar fascia); avoid direct Achilles tendon injection (rupture risk)</li>
<li><strong>Pain management</strong> -- acetaminophen, topical agents; <strong>avoid chronic opioids</strong> if possible</li>
<li><strong>Bisphosphonate</strong> or denosumab for osteoporosis (common in AS)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Extra-articular manifestations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Uveitis</strong> -- <strong>topical corticosteroids</strong> (prednisolone acetate 1%), <strong>cycloplegic</strong> (atropine, homatropine for pain and synechiae prevention); <strong>urgent ophthalmology referral</strong>; recurrent uveitis may respond to systemic therapy (TNF inhibitor -- <strong>adalimumab</strong> or <strong>infliximab</strong> have best uveitis efficacy; etanercept is ineffective for uveitis)</li>
<li><strong>Psoriasis</strong> -- topical steroids; systemic therapy (IL-17, IL-23 inhibitors)</li>
<li><strong>Inflammatory bowel disease</strong> -- gastroenterology referral; TNF inhibitor (especially <strong>adalimumab</strong> or <strong>infliximab</strong>; avoid IL-17 inhibitors which may worsen IBD)</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular risk</strong> -- aggressive management of traditional risk factors (statins, BP control, smoking cessation)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Surgery:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Total hip replacement</strong> (arthroplasty) for hip arthritis -- transforms quality of life</li>
<li><strong>Spinal surgery</strong> (osteotomy) for severe kyphotic deformity; high complication rate; specialized centers only</li>
<li><strong>Cervical spine fracture</strong> -- high risk in fused spine; surgical stabilization</li>
</ul>
