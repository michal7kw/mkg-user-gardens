<h1>Vitamin B6</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin B6 is an essential water-soluble vitamin existing in six interconvertible vitamers: pyridoxine, pyridoxal, and pyridoxamine, together with their phosphorylated forms. The metabolically active coenzyme is pyridoxal-5'-phosphate (P5P), which serves as a cofactor for more than 150 distinct enzymes — roughly 4% of all catalogued enzyme activities — making it one of the most versatile coenzymes in human biochemistry. These reactions span amino-acid metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, one-carbon (homocysteine) metabolism, heme synthesis, glycogenolysis, and the formation of niacin from tryptophan.</p>
<p>Because P5P is required for the decarboxylase enzymes that produce serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, and GABA, vitamin B6 status directly influences mood, cognition, and autonomic regulation. It is equally central to the transsulfuration arm of homocysteine clearance, where it serves as the cofactor for cystathionine beta-synthase and cystathionine gamma-lyase; B6 deficiency therefore contributes to elevated homocysteine alongside folate and B12 insufficiency. Dietary sources include poultry, fish, organ meats, starchy vegetables, and fortified cereals, but status can be compromised by alcohol use, certain medications (isoniazid, hydralazine, oral contraceptives), and renal disease.</p>
<p>Clinically, vitamin B6 is used to correct frank deficiency, to treat the rare inherited disorder pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy, as a component of homocysteine-lowering B-vitamin regimens, and as an evidence-supported option for premenstrual syndrome and nausea of pregnancy. Uniquely among the B vitamins, its safety has an important upper bound: chronic intake above roughly 200 mg/day is causally associated with a dose- and duration-dependent sensory peripheral neuropathy, which is usually reversible on discontinuation.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level:</strong> 4/5</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>P5P operates through a Schiff-base (aldimine) condensation between its reactive aldehyde group and the amino group of substrate amino acids, stabilizing reaction intermediates so that diverse bond cleavages can occur. Through this single chemistry it supports several major functional categories:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Neurotransmitter synthesis:</strong> Cofactor for aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC), which converts 5-HTP to serotonin and L-DOPA to dopamine; dopamine is then converted to norepinephrine. P5P is also the cofactor for glutamate decarboxylase (GAD), which forms GABA from glutamate — coupling B6 status to the balance of excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission.</li>
<li><strong>One-carbon / homocysteine metabolism:</strong> Cofactor for cystathionine beta-synthase and cystathionine gamma-lyase in the transsulfuration pathway, channeling homocysteine toward cysteine. Working with folate and B12, adequate B6 helps keep plasma homocysteine low.</li>
<li><strong>Amino-acid metabolism:</strong> Required for transaminases (e.g., ALT, AST) and for serine/glycine interconversion, positioning B6 at the center of protein and nitrogen handling.</li>
<li><strong>Heme synthesis:</strong> Cofactor for delta-aminolevulinic acid synthase (ALA synthase), the rate-limiting step of heme biosynthesis; deficiency can produce a sideroblastic, microcytic anemia.</li>
<li><strong>Glycogen metabolism:</strong> Bound cofactor for glycogen phosphorylase, supporting glucose mobilization during fasting and exercise.</li>
<li><strong>Levodopa interaction:</strong> By driving peripheral AADC activity, high-dose B6 accelerates conversion of levodopa to dopamine outside the CNS — the basis of its contraindication with unprotected (non-carbidopa) levodopa.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Research Summary</h2>
<p>The strongest evidence supports repletion in deficiency and the treatment of pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy, where B6 is disease-defining therapy. In combined B-vitamin regimens, B6 reliably contributes to lowering plasma homocysteine; however, meta-analyses of homocysteine-lowering trials show that biomarker reduction has not consistently translated into reduced cardiovascular events, a key example of a surrogate-endpoint disconnect. For premenstrual syndrome, doses of roughly 50-100 mg/day improve mood and physical symptoms in pooled analyses, and B6 (often combined with doxylamine) is a first-line treatment for nausea and vomiting of pregnancy. Evidence for carpal tunnel syndrome remains mixed. Safety data are clear that chronic supraphysiologic intake causes sensory neuropathy, defining the practical ceiling for long-term use.</p>

<h3>Timing</h3>
<ul>
<li>Can be taken with or without food; pairing with a B-complex covers the interdependence of B6 with folate and B12 in homocysteine metabolism.</li>
<li>For nausea of pregnancy, divided dosing across the day improves tolerability.</li>
<li>Limit continuous high-dose use; cycle or cap at the UL for long-term supplementation.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<h3>Contraindications</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Levodopa without carbidopa:</strong> B6 accelerates peripheral levodopa conversion, reducing CNS efficacy (not an issue with carbidopa/levodopa combinations).</li>
<li><strong>Phenobarbital or phenytoin:</strong> B6 may lower anticonvulsant serum concentrations.</li>
<li><strong>Chronic intake &gt;200 mg/day:</strong> Cumulative sensory neuropathy risk.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Drug Interactions</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Isoniazid, hydralazine, cycloserine, penicillamine:</strong> These drugs antagonize B6 and increase requirements (isoniazid-induced neuropathy is prevented with B6).</li>
<li><strong>Oral contraceptives:</strong> May modestly lower B6 status.</li>
<li><strong>Levodopa (unprotected):</strong> Reduced efficacy as above.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Side Effects</h3>
<p>At physiologic and modest supplemental doses, B6 is very well tolerated. The defining toxicity is a dose- and duration-dependent sensory peripheral neuropathy (paresthesias, numbness, gait ataxia) seen with chronic high intake, typically above 200 mg/day; it is usually reversible on discontinuation. Less common effects include nausea, headache, photosensitivity, and somnolence.</p>
<h2>Quality Considerations</h2>
<ul>
<li>Choose products third-party tested (USP, NSF, or ConsumerLab) for label accuracy.</li>
<li>Prefer P5P where label transparency on the active form matters; verify elemental B6 content per serving against the UL.</li>
<li>Avoid stacking multiple high-dose B6 products (e.g., a standalone B6 plus a B-complex plus an energy formula), which is a common cause of inadvertent chronic overdose and neuropathy.</li>
</ul>
