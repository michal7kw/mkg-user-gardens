<h1>Omega-6</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Omega-6 fatty acids are essential polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), with linoleic acid (LA) being the parent compound that the body converts to gamma-linolenic acid (GLA), dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid (DGLA), and arachidonic acid (AA). Linoleic acid is classified as essential because humans cannot synthesize it and must obtain it from the diet. Omega-6 fatty acids are critical structural components of cell membranes, influencing membrane fluidity, signaling, and gene expression.</p>
<p>Arachidonic acid, the most physiologically active omega-6 fatty acid, serves as the precursor for series-2 prostaglandins, series-4 leukotrienes, and thromboxanes -- eicosanoids that mediate inflammation, pain, fever, platelet aggregation, and immune responses. While these inflammatory mediators are essential for acute immune defense and wound healing, chronic overproduction due to excess omega-6 relative to omega-3 intake drives systemic inflammation.</p>
<p>The optimal omega-6 to omega-3 ratio is estimated at 4:1 or lower, yet Western diets typically provide ratios of 15-20:1 due to widespread use of vegetable seed oils (soybean, corn, sunflower, safflower) in processed foods. Reducing omega-6 intake (particularly from industrial seed oils) is as metabolically important as increasing omega-3 consumption. Notably, GLA from evening primrose oil and borage oil has anti-inflammatory properties through its conversion to DGLA, which produces anti-inflammatory series-1 prostaglandins.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Provides essential structural lipids for cell membrane integrity</li>
<li>Serves as precursor for eicosanoids that mediate inflammatory and immune responses</li>
<li>Supports skin barrier function and prevents transepidermal water loss</li>
<li>Participates in blood clotting via thromboxane A2 production</li>
<li>Contributes to brain development and neurotransmitter function</li>
<li>Enables normal growth and reproductive function</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Vegetable seed oils (soybean, corn, sunflower, safflower, cottonseed)</li>
<li>Nuts (walnuts, pine nuts, pecans)</li>
<li>Seeds (sunflower seeds, sesame seeds)</li>
<li>Poultry (especially skin and dark meat)</li>
<li>Eggs</li>
<li>Processed and fried foods (major hidden source)</li>
<li>Margarine and shortening</li>
<li>Evening primrose oil and borage oil (GLA sources)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>True omega-6 deficiency is rare in modern diets but manifests as dermatitis, dry scaly skin, poor wound healing, hair loss, and growth retardation, particularly in infants fed fat-free formulas. Essential fatty acid deficiency impairs cell membrane function and immune responses.</p>
<p>Excess omega-6 relative to omega-3 intake is far more common and drives chronic systemic inflammation, elevated C-reactive protein, increased cardiovascular risk, and promotion of pro-inflammatory eicosanoid cascades. High omega-6 diets are epidemiologically associated with increased rates of inflammatory diseases, obesity, and metabolic syndrome. Reducing omega-6 intake from industrial seed oils is a key dietary intervention for inflammation management.</p>
