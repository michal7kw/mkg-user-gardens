<h1>HDL Cholesterol</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) is the fraction of total cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein (HDL) particles. HDL particles are synthesized by the liver and small intestine and perform <strong>reverse cholesterol transport (RCT)</strong> -- the process of removing excess cholesterol from peripheral tissues (especially macrophage foam cells in atherosclerotic plaques) and returning it to the liver for biliary excretion. Beyond RCT, HDL particles have anti-inflammatory, antioxidant (via paraoxonase), anti-thrombotic, and endothelial-protective effects that contribute to their cardioprotective reputation.</p>
<p>Epidemiologically, low HDL-C is <strong>inversely associated with cardiovascular risk</strong> -- each 1 mg/dL increase in HDL-C is associated with a ~2-3% reduction in CV risk in observational studies. Low HDL-C is one of the five diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome (NCEP ATP III), and HDL-C &gt;60 mg/dL counts as a negative risk factor in the ASCVD pooled cohort risk calculation. Low HDL-C typically clusters with high triglycerides, small dense LDL particles, and insulin resistance -- the &quot;atherogenic dyslipidemia&quot; of metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes.</p>
<p>However, the simple &quot;raise HDL-C and reduce CV events&quot; hypothesis has been challenged by Mendelian randomization studies and clinical trials of CETP inhibitors (which raise HDL-C 50-150% but failed to reduce CV events in trials like ILLUMINATE, dal-OUTCOMES, ACCELERATE). The current understanding is that <strong>HDL function</strong> (efflux capacity, anti-inflammatory properties) may be more important than HDL-C concentration -- a concept supported by the &quot;HDL dysfunction&quot; observed in chronic inflammation, CKD, and diabetes where HDL becomes pro-inflammatory. Nonetheless, low HDL-C remains a useful risk stratification marker and a target for lifestyle interventions.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (universally measured; ASCVD risk factor; lifestyle interventions robustly raise HDL-C).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>HDL-C is fasting-dependent (postprandial samples less reliable; LDL-C and triglycerides more affected)</li>
<li>Reference range is sex-dependent (women have ~10 mg/dL higher HDL-C due to estrogen)</li>
<li>Race/ethnicity: African American populations have higher average HDL-C</li>
<li>Pregnancy: HDL-C rises ~10-20% in second trimester</li>
<li>High triglycerides can interfere with some HDL-C assays</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase HDL-C (favorable):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Aerobic exercise</strong> (the most impactful lifestyle intervention; 150 min/week raises HDL ~3-6 mg/dL)</li>
<li><strong>Moderate alcohol consumption</strong> (1-2 drinks/day; wine, particularly red) -- raises HDL ~5-10%</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> 1-4 g/day EPA+DHA -- modest HDL increase (2-5 mg/dL)</li>
<li><strong>Niacin</strong> (vitamin B3) 1-3 g/day -- raises HDL ~15-35%; the most potent HDL-raising drug, but AIM-HIGH and HPS2-THRIVE showed no CV outcome benefit beyond statins</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy</strong> (oral contraceptives, HRT) -- raises HDL ~10%</li>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> (especially from visceral adiposity reduction)</li>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong> -- raises HDL ~5-10 mg/dL within months</li>
<li><strong>Purple / red produce</strong> (anthocyanins; small studies)</li>
<li><strong>CETP deficiency</strong> (genetic; very high HDL-C, not always protective)</li>
<li><strong>Apolipoprotein A-I Milano</strong> variant carriers (low atherosclerosis despite variable HDL)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease HDL-C (unfavorable):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Smoking</strong> (most impactful lifestyle factor lowering HDL)</li>
<li><strong>Sedentary lifestyle</strong></li>
<li><strong>Obesity</strong> (especially visceral adiposity)</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes / insulin resistance</strong></li>
<li><strong>Metabolic syndrome</strong></li>
<li><strong>Very low-fat diet</strong> (HDL-C is suppressed when dietary fat is too low)</li>
<li><strong>Trans fat intake</strong> (the worst dietary pattern for HDL)</li>
<li><strong>Refined carbohydrate / high-glycemic-load diet</strong></li>
<li><strong>Anabolic steroid use</strong></li>
<li><strong>Beta-blockers</strong> (chronic; modest reduction)</li>
<li><strong>Thiazide diuretics</strong> (modest)</li>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong></li>
<li><strong>End-stage renal disease, nephrotic syndrome</strong></li>
<li><strong>Tangier disease</strong> (ABCA1 mutation; near-absent HDL)</li>
<li><strong>LCAT deficiency</strong></li>
<li><strong>ApoA-I deficiency</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low HDL-C is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular disease:</strong> low HDL-C is a major CV risk factor; each 1 mg/dL decrease raises CV risk ~2-3%</li>
<li><strong>Metabolic syndrome:</strong> low HDL-C is one of the 5 NCEP ATP III criteria (M &lt;40, F &lt;50)</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes:</strong> low HDL-C with high triglycerides is the classic diabetic dyslipidemia</li>
<li><strong>Insulin resistance:</strong> HDL-C correlates inversely with HOMA-IR</li>
<li><strong>Atherosclerosis progression:</strong> low HDL-C correlates with coronary artery calcium progression</li>
<li><strong>Genetic disorders:</strong> Tangier disease (ABCA1 mutation), apoA-I deficiency, LCAT deficiency</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated HDL-C is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Reduced CV risk</strong> (most cases): regular exercise, moderate alcohol, healthy weight</li>
<li><strong>CETP deficiency</strong> (genetic; HDL &gt;100 mg/dL): paradoxically NOT clearly protective in trials of CETP inhibitors</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy</strong></li>
<li><strong>Primary biliary cholangitis</strong> (HDL rises paradoxically)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>HDL function vs concentration:</strong> raising HDL-C pharmacologically (niacin, CETP inhibitors) has NOT reduced CV events -- function may matter more</li>
<li><strong>Reverse causation:</strong> low HDL-C often clusters with high TG, insulin resistance, and obesity; the independent contribution is debated</li>
<li><strong>HDL-C is not the primary target</strong> of lipid-lowering therapy (LDL-C and ApoB are); HDL-C is more a risk marker than a target</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Cardiovascular risk stratification (pooled cohort ASCVD risk equation)</li>
<li>Metabolic syndrome diagnosis</li>
<li>Diabetes risk assessment</li>
<li>Monitoring lifestyle intervention response</li>
<li>Genetic dyslipidemia workup (Tangier disease, LCAT deficiency)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>HDL-C is highly modifiable through lifestyle and targeted supplements:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Aerobic exercise</strong> 150 min/week moderate intensity (or 75 min/week vigorous) -- raises HDL 3-6 mg/dL; the most impactful intervention</li>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong> -- raises HDL 5-10 mg/dL within months</li>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> (5-10% body weight if obese) -- raises HDL ~3-5 mg/dL</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> 1-4 g/day EPA+DHA -- modest HDL increase (2-5 mg/dL); lowers triglycerides more substantially</li>
<li><strong>Niacin</strong> 1-3 g/day -- most potent HDL-raising drug (~15-35%); limited by side effects (flushing, hyperglycemia) and lack of outcome benefit in statin era</li>
<li><strong>Moderate alcohol</strong> (1 drink women, 1-2 drinks men; wine preferred) -- raises HDL 5-10%; balance against cancer risk</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong> rich in olive oil, nuts, fish -- modest HDL increase</li>
<li><strong>Avocado, olive oil, nuts</strong> (monounsaturated fats) -- improve HDL function</li>
<li><strong>Purple/red produce</strong> (berries, red cabbage, grapes) -- anthocyanins modestly raise HDL</li>
<li><strong>Avoid trans fats</strong> (the worst dietary pattern for HDL)</li>
<li><strong>Limit refined carbohydrates</strong> (high-glycemic-load diets suppress HDL)</li>
<li><strong>Don't go too low-fat</strong> -- very low-fat diets suppress HDL</li>
<li><strong>Anabolic steroid cessation</strong> (if applicable)</li>
<li><strong>Optimize blood sugar</strong> in diabetics</li>
</ol>
