<h1>Autoimmune Hepatitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a <strong>chronic, progressive, immune-mediated inflammatory liver disease</strong> in which the body's immune system attacks <strong>hepatocytes</strong>, producing <strong>interface hepatitis</strong> (lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate at the portal-parenchymal junction), <strong>elevated transaminases</strong>, <strong>hypergammaglobulinemia</strong> (especially elevated IgG), and <strong>circulating autoantibodies</strong> (antinuclear antibody ANA, anti-smooth muscle antibody ASMA, anti-liver kidney microsomal antibody anti-LKM1). AIH affects predominantly <strong>women</strong> (4:1 ratio) with a bimodal age distribution (peaks in adolescence/young adulthood and in the 40s-60s). Prevalence is approximately <strong>10-25 per 100,000</strong> in European populations.</p>
<p>The pathophysiology involves a <strong>loss of immune tolerance</strong> to liver-specific antigens, driven by <strong>genetic susceptibility</strong> (HLA-DRB1<em>03 and HLA-DRB1</em>04) and <strong>environmental triggers</strong> (viruses, drugs). Autoreactive T cells (predominantly CD4+) infiltrate the liver and mediate hepatocyte destruction, with B cells producing autoantibodies (ANA, ASMA, anti-LKM1, anti-SLA/LP). The result is progressive <strong>interface hepatitis</strong>, fibrosis, and ultimately <strong>cirrhosis</strong> in untreated patients (~20% within 10-20 years). <strong>Two types</strong> are distinguished: <strong>Type 1 AIH</strong> (ANA and/or ASMA positive; ~80% of cases; affects adults and children) and <strong>Type 2 AIH</strong> (anti-LKM1 or anti-LC1 positive; ~20%; affects children and adolescents; more aggressive course).</p>
<p>The clinical spectrum ranges from <strong>asymptomatic</strong> abnormal liver tests (~25-30%, detected incidentally) to <strong>acute hepatitis</strong> (mimicking viral hepatitis) to <strong>fulminant liver failure</strong> (rare). The classic symptomatic presentation includes <strong>fatigue, jaundice, right upper quadrant discomfort, arthralgia, and amenorrhea</strong>. <strong>Extrahepatic autoimmune manifestations</strong> are common -- <strong>autoimmune thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes, celiac disease, ulcerative colitis, Sjogren's syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis</strong> -- and should be screened for. AIH may <strong>overlap with primary biliary cholangitis (AIH-PBC) or primary sclerosing cholangitis (AIH-PSC)</strong>, complicating management. Diagnosis uses the <strong>simplified or revised International Autoimmune Hepatitis Group (IAIHG) scoring system</strong>, integrating autoantibody titers, IgG level, histology, and absence of viral hepatitis.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Diagnosis (use the simplified IAIHG criteria):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Autoantibodies: ANA or ASMA &gt;=1:40 (2 points) or &gt;=1:80 (1 point); anti-LKM1 &gt;=1:40 (2 points)</li>
<li>IgG: &gt;1.10x ULN (2 points) or &gt;ULN (1 point)</li>
<li>Histology: compatible with AIH (1 point) or typical of AIH (2 points)</li>
<li>Absence of viral hepatitis (2 points)</li>
<li><strong>&gt;=6 points = probable AIH; &gt;=7 points = definite AIH</strong></li>
<li>Exclude viral hepatitis (HAV, HBV, HCV, EBV, CMV), Wilson disease, hemochromatosis, drug-induced liver injury, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>First-line therapy (achieves remission in 80%):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prednisone or prednisolone</strong> -- <strong>induction: 40-60 mg/day</strong>, taper over 4-8 weeks to maintenance dose of 5-10 mg/day. Combine with azathioprine from the start (steroid-sparing)</li>
<li><strong>Azathiurine</strong> -- <strong>1-2 mg/kg/day</strong>; introduce once transaminases &lt;3x ULN to avoid hepatotoxicity; monitor CBC and TPMT activity (test before starting; low activity = avoid or reduce dose)</li>
<li><strong>Budesonide</strong> (3 mg TID) -- alternative to prednisone in <strong>non-cirrhotic</strong> AIH; 85% less systemic steroid effect (first-pass hepatic metabolism); not for cirrhotics (portosystemic shunting reduces efficacy)</li>
<li><strong>Treatment goals</strong>: biochemical remission (AST/ALT and IgG normalization within 6-12 months); histological remission follows</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Maintenance and withdrawal:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Continue <strong>prednisone (5-10 mg/day) + azathioprine</strong> or <strong>azathioprine monotherapy</strong> until sustained remission (at least 2-3 years)</li>
<li><strong>Attempt withdrawal</strong> after 24+ months of biochemical remission; <strong>50-80% relapse within 12-18 months</strong>; most require <strong>lifelong therapy</strong></li>
<li><strong>Re-treat relapse</strong> with the original regimen; consider long-term maintenance azathioprine or mycophenolate</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Second-line therapy (intolerance or non-response to azathioprine):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mycophenolate mofetil</strong> (MMF) 1-2 g/day -- preferred second-line; effective in azathioprine-intolerant patients (but less effective in true non-responders)</li>
<li><strong>Tacrolimus</strong> -- potent; for refractory disease; nephrotoxicity monitoring</li>
<li><strong>Cyclosporine</strong> -- less commonly used</li>
<li><strong>6-mercaptopurine</strong> -- for azathioprine-intolerant patients (rarely used)</li>
<li><strong>Rituximab</strong> -- emerging; case series in refractory AIH</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Special situations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> -- <strong>do NOT stop therapy</strong>; untreated AIH risks flare and fetal loss. Prefer azathioprine (safe in pregnancy); avoid mycophenolate (teratogenic). Steroids safe. Aim for biochemical remission before conception</li>
<li><strong>Acute liver failure presentation</strong> -- <strong>IV methylprednisolone 60 mg/day</strong> may rescue; if no improvement within 7 days, <strong>liver transplantation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Overlap syndromes (AIH-PBC)</strong> -- add <strong>ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA)</strong> 13-15 mg/kg/day to immunosuppression</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Liver transplantation:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Indicated for <strong>decompensated cirrhosis</strong> (MELD-based), <strong>acute liver failure</strong> not responding to steroids, or <strong>hepatocellular carcinoma</strong></li>
<li>5-year survival ~80-90%</li>
<li><strong>AIH recurs in ~20-30% of recipients</strong> post-transplant</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive and supportive therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> (many AIH patients are deficient; vitamin D modulates immune function)</li>
<li><strong>Calcium and bone protection</strong> -- DEXA at baseline; bisphosphonate for glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis</li>
<li><strong>NAC</strong> (antioxidant; limited evidence)</li>
<li><strong>Probiotics</strong> -- gut-liver axis; limited evidence</li>
<li><strong>Avoid alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs</strong></li>
</ul>
