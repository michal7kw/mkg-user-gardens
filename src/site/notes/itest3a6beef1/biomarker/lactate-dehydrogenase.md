<h1>Lactate Dehydrogenase</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) is a cytoplasmic enzyme present in nearly every cell of the body that catalyzes the reversible conversion of pyruvate to lactate, coupled with the oxidation/reduction of the NADH/NAD+ cofactor pair. This reaction is the terminal step of anaerobic glycolysis, allowing continued ATP production and NAD+ regeneration when oxygen is limited. LDH is a tetramer assembled from two distinct subunits, M (muscle, encoded by <em>LDHA</em>) and H (heart, encoded by <em>LDHB</em>), which combine into five isoenzymes (LDH-1 through LDH-5) with tissue-characteristic distributions: LDH-1/LDH-2 predominate in heart and red blood cells, LDH-3 in lung and lymphoid tissue, and LDH-4/LDH-5 in liver and skeletal muscle.</p>
<p>Because LDH resides inside cells at high concentration, it leaks into the bloodstream whenever cell membranes are compromised by injury, ischemia, inflammation, or lysis. Total serum LDH therefore rises in a wide range of conditions, making it a sensitive but inherently nonspecific indicator of tissue damage. Historically, isoenzyme electrophoresis was used to localize the source of elevation (for example LDH-1 greater than LDH-2 suggesting myocardial or hemolytic origin), though troponin and more specific markers have largely replaced this use. In hemolysis, LDH released from ruptured erythrocytes is one of the core laboratory criteria, typically interpreted alongside low haptoglobin and elevated indirect bilirubin.</p>
<p>Clinically, LDH is measured to gauge the severity and progression of tissue injury, to detect and monitor hemolytic processes, and as a prognostic and staging biomarker in oncology. Elevated LDH reflects high cellular turnover and the Warburg effect in many cancers and is incorporated into prognostic indices for lymphoma, melanoma, germ cell tumors, and small cell lung cancer. LDH is also a routine component of pleural and other body-fluid analyses (Light's criteria) to distinguish exudates from transudates.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals vary substantially by assay method, temperature, and laboratory; always interpret against the reporting lab's range.</li>
<li>Children and neonates have physiologically higher LDH than adults.</li>
<li>Hemolyzed specimens cause spuriously high results because erythrocytes are LDH-rich; sample handling is a major pre-analytical pitfall.</li>
<li>No single universally standardized range exists across platforms.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase LDH:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Tissue necrosis or ischemia (myocardial infarction, infarcted bowel, severe trauma)</li>
<li>Hemolysis (in vivo from hemolytic anemia; in vitro from poor specimen handling)</li>
<li>Liver injury (hepatitis, cirrhosis, ischemic hepatitis)</li>
<li>Malignancy with high tumor burden or rapid turnover (lymphoma, leukemia, melanoma, germ cell tumors)</li>
<li>Skeletal muscle damage (rhabdomyolysis, intense exercise)</li>
<li>Pulmonary embolism and pneumonia</li>
<li>Sepsis and severe systemic inflammation</li>
<li>Tumor lysis syndrome</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease LDH:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Hereditary LDH subunit deficiency (rare, usually clinically mild)</li>
<li>Generally not a clinically pursued finding</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hemolytic anemia and intravascular hemolysis</strong> -- LDH released from lysed erythrocytes is a core hemolysis marker; markedly elevated LDH with low haptoglobin strongly supports intravascular hemolysis, including prosthetic heart valve-associated hemolysis.</li>
<li><strong>Myocardial infarction</strong> -- historically used (LDH-1 flip) but superseded by cardiac troponin; LDH still rises in large infarcts.</li>
<li><strong>Malignancy</strong> -- a prognostic and staging biomarker; high LDH reflects tumor burden and glycolytic activity and is built into prognostic models for lymphoma, melanoma, germ cell tumors, and small cell lung cancer.</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease</strong> -- hepatocellular injury releases LDH-5; very high LDH suggests ischemic (&quot;shock&quot;) liver.</li>
<li><strong>Rhabdomyolysis and muscle injury</strong> -- accompanies elevated creatine kinase.</li>
<li><strong>Pulmonary embolism and sepsis</strong> -- nonspecific but supportive of tissue hypoperfusion and injury.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hereditary LDH deficiency</strong> -- rare; LDH-A deficiency may cause exertional myopathy, LDH-B deficiency is usually asymptomatic.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Tracking hemolysis activity over time (e.g., in autoimmune hemolytic anemia or paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria)</li>
<li>Following tumor burden and treatment response in oncology</li>
<li>Body-fluid analysis: pleural fluid LDH and fluid/serum LDH ratio in Light's criteria for exudate classification</li>
<li>Composite indices such as the LDH-to-albumin ratio for risk stratification in critical illness and cancer</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>Because elevated LDH usually reflects an underlying disease process rather than a directly modifiable lifestyle factor, &quot;improvement&quot; means treating the root cause and supporting tissue integrity:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying condition</strong> -- the most important step; LDH normalizes as hemolysis, infarction, hepatitis, or tumor burden resolves with appropriate therapy.</li>
<li><strong>Ensure proper specimen handling</strong> -- avoid hemolysis during venipuncture and processing to prevent falsely elevated readings.</li>
<li><strong>Address hemolysis drivers</strong> -- correct mechanical, autoimmune, or oxidative causes; ensure adequate iron, folate, and B12 status to support healthy erythropoiesis.</li>
<li><strong>Support hepatic recovery</strong> -- in liver injury, antioxidant support such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC) may aid glutathione repletion and reduce hepatocellular enzyme release in selected settings.</li>
<li><strong>Moderate extreme exertion</strong> -- transient post-exercise LDH elevation resolves with adequate recovery; pacing intense training reduces spurious elevations.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain antioxidant defenses</strong> -- adequate intake of antioxidants (e.g., vitamin C, selenium) supports cellular membrane integrity, though direct LDH-lowering evidence is limited.</li>
</ol>
