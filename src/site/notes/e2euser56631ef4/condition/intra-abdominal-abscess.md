<h1>Intra-abdominal Abscess</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>An intra-abdominal abscess is a discrete, walled-off collection of pus located within the peritoneal cavity, retroperitoneum, or a visceral organ. It forms when the body contains a localized infection — typically following spillage of gastrointestinal contents — by surrounding it with fibrinous and inflammatory tissue. Intra-abdominal abscess is a defining example of a <em>complicated</em> intra-abdominal infection, meaning the infection has extended beyond a single organ into the peritoneal space and requires both antimicrobial therapy and physical source control.</p>
<p>Most abscesses are polymicrobial, reflecting the flora of the gastrointestinal tract: enteric gram-negative bacilli such as <em>Escherichia coli</em>, anaerobes such as <em>Bacteroides fragilis</em>, streptococci, and enterococci. They arise from a finite set of precipitating events — perforation of a viscus (appendicitis, diverticulitis, perforated ulcer), anastomotic leak after abdominal surgery, penetrating disease in Crohn's, and infected pancreatic necrosis being among the most common. Common anatomic locations include subphrenic, pelvic, paracolic, and interloop spaces.</p>
<p>Clinically, intra-abdominal abscess can be insidious. Fever — often persistent or spiking — is the most consistent feature, but abdominal findings may be subtle, especially in postoperative or immunocompromised patients, which is why a high index of suspicion and cross-sectional imaging are essential. Untreated, an abscess can seed the bloodstream and precipitate sepsis. The cornerstone of management is source control: drainage of the collection, increasingly achieved by minimally invasive percutaneous techniques, combined with appropriate antibiotics.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The classic presentation is fever with abdominal pain and tenderness, often with anorexia, nausea, and malaise. Tachycardia and other signs of a systemic inflammatory response are common. Localizing features depend on site — a subphrenic abscess may cause referred shoulder pain and respiratory symptoms, while a pelvic abscess may produce tenesmus or urinary symptoms. In postoperative patients, an unexplained persistent fever or failure to recover should prompt evaluation for an abscess.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis is the imaging modality of choice, defining the size, location, and number of collections and guiding drainage. Ultrasound and MRI are useful alternatives in selected patients. Laboratory studies typically show leukocytosis with neutrophilia and elevated inflammatory markers; culture of drained fluid identifies the responsible organisms and refines antimicrobial therapy.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant risks are events that introduce gastrointestinal contents into the peritoneum: recent abdominal or pelvic surgery (notably anastomotic leak), perforated viscus from appendicitis, diverticulitis, or peptic ulcer disease, penetrating Crohn's disease, and infected pancreatitis. Host factors such as immunocompromise and diabetes increase susceptibility and worsen outcomes.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Source control is the central principle. Percutaneous image-guided drainage is first-line for accessible, well-defined collections and has largely replaced open surgery for many abscesses. Surgical drainage is reserved for inaccessible, multiloculated, or recurrent collections and for managing the underlying source (e.g., resection of perforated bowel). Empiric antibiotics must cover enteric gram-negative bacilli and anaerobes; therapy is then tailored and de-escalated based on cultures. Hemodynamic support and treatment of the precipitating disease complete the approach.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most feared complication is progression to sepsis and septic shock with multi-organ dysfunction. Abscesses may persist or recur despite drainage, and chronic collections can erode into adjacent structures to form fistulae or precipitate bowel obstruction.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>With prompt source control and appropriate antibiotics, most patients recover, and outcomes from percutaneous drainage are generally favorable. Prognosis worsens with delayed diagnosis, sepsis at presentation, multiple or poorly drainable collections, and significant comorbidity.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Intra-abdominal abscess is a common complication of intra-abdominal infections and abdominal surgery. Its incidence tracks with rates of complicated appendicitis and diverticulitis, surgical volume, and the prevalence of inflammatory bowel disease, with postoperative and Crohn's-related abscesses representing frequently studied subgroups.</p>
