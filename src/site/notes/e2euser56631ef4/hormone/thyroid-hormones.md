<h1>Thyroid Hormones</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Thyroid hormones — thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) — are iodine-containing, tyrosine-derived hormones synthesized by the follicular cells of the thyroid gland. They are the master regulators of basal metabolic rate, influencing oxygen consumption, heat production, and the metabolism of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins in nearly every nucleated cell of the body. Beyond metabolism, thyroid hormones are indispensable for normal fetal and neonatal brain development, linear growth, cardiac contractility and heart rate, and the maintenance of mood and cognitive function.</p>
<p>T4 is the predominant secreted form and functions largely as a circulating prohormone, while T3 is the biologically active hormone with roughly 3-5 times the receptor affinity of T4. The thyroid secretes about 80% of the body's T4 and only a minority of its T3; most circulating T3 is generated in peripheral tissues — particularly the liver, kidney, and muscle — by the enzymatic removal of an iodine atom from T4 via the deiodinase enzymes (DIO1, DIO2). This peripheral conversion is a key control point, and disturbances in it (for example, during illness, caloric restriction, or selenium deficiency) can shift the balance toward inactive reverse T3.</p>
<p>Clinically, thyroid hormone status is among the most commonly assessed endocrine functions. Deficiency (hypothyroidism, including autoimmune Hashimoto's thyroiditis) produces fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, bradycardia, dyslipidemia, and depression, whereas excess (hyperthyroidism, classically Graves' disease) causes weight loss, heat intolerance, tachycardia, anxiety, and tremor. Because the effects are so widespread, even mild or subclinical thyroid dysfunction carries measurable cardiovascular and metabolic consequences, making this hormone axis central to both general health and longevity.</p>
<p>Evidence Level: 5/5</p>
<h2>Biochemistry and Synthesis</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Building blocks</strong>: Synthesized from the amino acid tyrosine and dietary iodine. T4 (thyroxine) contains four iodine atoms; T3 (triiodothyronine) contains three.</li>
<li><strong>Thyroglobulin scaffold</strong>: Iodide is oxidized by thyroid peroxidase (TPO) and coupled to tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin, forming mono- and di-iodotyrosines that combine into T4 and T3.</li>
<li><strong>Storage and release</strong>: Stored extracellularly in colloid; proteolysis releases T4 and T3 into circulation.</li>
<li><strong>Transport</strong>: ~99.7% of circulating hormone is bound to carrier proteins (thyroxine-binding globulin, transthyretin, albumin); only the free fractions (Free T4, Free T3) are biologically available.</li>
<li><strong>Peripheral activation</strong>: Deiodinase enzymes (DIO1, DIO2) convert T4 → active T3; DIO3 inactivates hormone to reverse T3 and T2.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Thyroid hormones act primarily through <strong>nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (TRα, TRβ)</strong>, which are ligand-activated transcription factors. T3 binds these receptors on thyroid hormone response elements in target gene promoters, modulating transcription of genes that govern mitochondrial biogenesis, Na+/K+-ATPase activity, uncoupling proteins, beta-adrenergic receptor density, and lipid and carbohydrate metabolizing enzymes. Non-genomic actions at the plasma membrane and mitochondria contribute additional rapid metabolic effects. The net result is increased basal metabolic rate, thermogenesis, and tissue oxygen consumption.</p>
<h2>Regulation (HPT Axis)</h2>
<ul>
<li>The <strong>hypothalamus</strong> secretes thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH).</li>
<li>TRH stimulates the <strong>anterior pituitary</strong> to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH/thyrotropin).</li>
<li>TSH drives thyroid synthesis and release of T4 and T3.</li>
<li>Circulating T4/T3 exert <strong>negative feedback</strong> on both the pituitary and hypothalamus, suppressing TSH and TRH.</li>
<li>This feedback loop keeps free hormone within a tight set-point; a rising TSH with low Free T4 signals primary hypothyroidism, while a suppressed TSH with high Free T4 signals hyperthyroidism.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong> — Low hormone output; fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, bradycardia, elevated LDL, depression. Most common cause is autoimmune Hashimoto's thyroiditis.</li>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong> — Excess hormone; weight loss, heat intolerance, palpitations, anxiety, tremor, diarrhea. Graves' disease is the classic autoimmune cause.</li>
<li><strong>Subclinical thyroid dysfunction</strong> — Abnormal TSH with normal free hormones; associated with cardiovascular risk, dyslipidemia, and atherosclerosis.</li>
<li><strong>Euthyroid sick syndrome (non-thyroidal illness)</strong> — Reduced peripheral T4→T3 conversion and elevated reverse T3 during acute illness or starvation.</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular impact</strong> — Thyroid hormones strongly influence heart rate, contractility, and vascular resistance; both deficiency and excess raise cardiovascular morbidity.</li>
<li><strong>Neurodevelopment</strong> — Maternal and neonatal hypothyroidism cause irreversible cognitive impairment if untreated (historically, cretinism from iodine deficiency).</li>
</ul>
<h2>Nutrient Dependencies</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iodine</strong> — Essential substrate for hormone synthesis; deficiency is the leading global cause of preventable hypothyroidism and goiter.</li>
<li><strong>Selenium</strong> — Cofactor for deiodinase enzymes and glutathione peroxidases protecting the gland; deficiency impairs T4→T3 conversion.</li>
<li><strong>Zinc and iron (ferritin)</strong> — Required for TPO activity and hormone synthesis; deficiency contributes to hypothyroid features.</li>
<li><strong>Tyrosine</strong> — Amino acid backbone of the iodothyronine ring.</li>
</ul>
