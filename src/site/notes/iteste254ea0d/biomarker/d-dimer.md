<h1>D-Dimer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>D-Dimer is the smallest soluble fibrin degradation product, formed when the enzyme plasmin cleaves cross-linked fibrin within a stabilized blood clot. Its name reflects its structure: two adjacent fibrin &quot;D&quot; fragments held together by the factor XIIIa-mediated cross-link, which uniquely distinguishes it from fibrinogen degradation products that arise without prior clot formation. Detectable D-Dimer therefore signals that the coagulation cascade has generated cross-linked fibrin and that the fibrinolytic system has subsequently degraded it, making it a direct readout of recent or ongoing thrombus turnover.</p>
<p>Plasma D-Dimer is measured by quantitative immunoassays (most commonly automated latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetric or ELISA-derived methods) that use monoclonal antibodies directed against the cross-linked D-dimer neoepitope. Results are reported in either fibrinogen-equivalent units (FEU) or D-dimer units (DDU), and the two scales differ roughly two-fold, which is a frequent source of cross-laboratory confusion. Levels rise with any process that increases fibrin formation and breakdown, and they also increase physiologically with age, pregnancy, inflammation, recent surgery, and hospitalization.</p>
<p>Clinically, D-Dimer's defining property is high sensitivity paired with low specificity. A normal D-Dimer combined with a low or intermediate pre-test clinical probability (for example, a low Wells score) safely excludes venous thromboembolism without imaging, sparing many patients from CT pulmonary angiography. An elevated value, by contrast, is non-specific and requires confirmatory imaging or further evaluation. D-Dimer is also a core laboratory criterion in scoring systems for disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and has emerged as a prognostic marker in sepsis, COVID-19, and cancer-associated thrombosis.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Assay-dependent: results may be reported in FEU or DDU; DDU values are approximately half the corresponding FEU value. Always confirm the unit and the laboratory's validated cutoff before interpreting.</li>
<li>The standard 500 ng/mL FEU cutoff is validated for <em>excluding</em> VTE, not for confirming it.</li>
<li>Age-adjusted cutoffs (ADJUST-PE strategy) increase specificity in patients over 50 while maintaining a high negative predictive value.</li>
<li>Physiologically elevated in pregnancy (rising across trimesters), the elderly, hospitalized patients, and the postoperative period, reducing diagnostic usefulness in these groups.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase D-Dimer:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Venous thromboembolism — deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism (active fibrin turnover)</li>
<li>Disseminated intravascular coagulation (widespread microthrombosis with secondary fibrinolysis)</li>
<li>Recent surgery, trauma, or burns (tissue injury activates coagulation)</li>
<li>Pregnancy and the postpartum period (progressive physiologic hypercoagulability)</li>
<li>Advancing age (baseline fibrin turnover rises)</li>
<li>Active infection, sepsis, and severe COVID-19 (inflammation-driven coagulopathy)</li>
<li>Malignancy (tumor-associated procoagulant activity)</li>
<li>Acute inflammation and large hematomas</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease D-Dimer:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Effective anticoagulant therapy reducing new clot formation</li>
<li>Resolution of an acute thrombotic event over time</li>
<li>Very early or very small thrombi may yield false-negative results</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Venous Thromboembolism</strong> — D-Dimer is central to VTE diagnostic algorithms; a positive result in a patient with elevated clinical probability prompts confirmatory ultrasound or CT angiography.</li>
<li><strong>Disseminated intravascular coagulation</strong> — markedly elevated D-Dimer, together with low platelets, prolonged clotting times, and low fibrinogen, supports the diagnosis of DIC under ISTH scoring.</li>
<li><strong>Sepsis and severe infection</strong> — elevated D-Dimer reflects inflammation-induced coagulation activation and correlates with severity.</li>
<li><strong>Cancer</strong> — tumor-associated hypercoagulability raises D-Dimer and predicts cancer-associated thrombosis risk.</li>
<li><strong>Severe COVID-19 (including Long COVID vascular sequelae)</strong> — high D-Dimer is an established marker of disease severity, thrombotic complications, and mortality.</li>
<li><strong>Myocardial Infarction, Stroke, and Atrial Fibrillation</strong> — elevated D-Dimer reflects a prothrombotic state and carries adverse prognostic weight in arterial and cardioembolic disease.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Diagnostic approach and limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>D-Dimer is a <em>rule-out</em> test: its strength is a high negative predictive value, not a positive confirmation.</li>
<li>It must always be interpreted alongside a validated clinical probability score (e.g., Wells). A normal D-Dimer in a low/intermediate-probability patient excludes VTE; it should not be ordered in high-probability patients, who proceed directly to imaging.</li>
<li>Low specificity means many non-thrombotic conditions raise D-Dimer, so a positive value alone never confirms thrombosis.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Excluding deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism in the emergency department</li>
<li>Supporting and serially monitoring DIC</li>
<li>Assessing recurrence risk after stopping anticoagulation for unprovoked VTE</li>
<li>Risk stratification in sepsis, COVID-19, and cancer-associated thrombosis</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>D-Dimer reflects underlying clot turnover rather than a directly &quot;optimizable&quot; lifestyle parameter, so the goal is to lower thrombotic risk and treat the underlying cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying condition</strong> — appropriate anticoagulation for confirmed VTE or DIC is the definitive way to reduce pathologic D-Dimer elevation; never self-treat suspected thrombosis.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain mobility and avoid prolonged immobilization</strong> — regular movement, calf exercises, and compression during long travel reduce venous stasis and clot formation.</li>
<li><strong>Hydration</strong> — adequate fluid intake reduces blood viscosity and venous stasis.</li>
<li><strong>Address cardiovascular risk factors</strong> — control of blood pressure, glycemia, and smoking cessation lowers the prothrombotic burden.</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acid intake</strong> — dietary or supplemental omega-3 has modest, evidence-level-2 effects on thrombotic markers in cardiovascular and atrial-fibrillation populations and may favorably influence hemostatic balance.</li>
<li><strong>Follow anticoagulation guidance precisely</strong> — adherence to prescribed therapy is the principal driver of D-Dimer normalization in treated thrombosis.</li>
</ol>
