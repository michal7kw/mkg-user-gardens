<h1>Pituitary Disorder</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The pituitary gland (&quot;master gland&quot;) sits in the sella turcica at the base of the skull and controls virtually every endocrine axis: it secretes <strong>growth hormone (GH), prolactin (PRL), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinizing hormone (LH)</strong>, and posteriorly stores <strong>vasopressin (ADH)</strong> and oxytocin synthesized in the hypothalamus. Pituitary disorders produce symptoms through three mechanisms: <strong>hormone hypersecretion</strong> (functioning adenoma), <strong>hormone hyposecretion</strong> (mass effect destroying normal tissue, or stalk compression), and <strong>mass effect</strong> (headache, vision loss, cranial nerve palsy).</p>
<p><strong>Pituitary adenomas</strong> are the most common pituitary disorder (~10% of intracranial tumors; prevalence ~1:1000 clinically, ~10-15% at autopsy). They are classified by size (<strong>microadenoma &lt;10 mm; macroadenoma &gt;=10 mm</strong>) and by secretory status. The most common functioning adenomas are <strong>prolactinomas</strong> (~40%, causing galactorrhea, amenorrhea, infertility, hypogonadism), followed by <strong>somatotroph adenomas</strong> causing <strong>acromegaly</strong> (excess GH -&gt; hepatized IGF-1 -&gt; tissue overgrowth; cardiovascular, metabolic, and increased cancer risk; 2-4x mortality if untreated), <strong>corticotroph adenomas</strong> causing <strong>Cushing disease</strong> (excess ACTH -&gt; cortisol; central obesity, hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis; 4x mortality untreated), and <strong>TSH-secreting adenomas</strong> (rare; hyperthyroidism). <strong>Non-functioning adenomas</strong> (~30%) present with mass effect or hypopituitarism.</p>
<p><strong>Hypopituitarism</strong> can result from adenoma mass effect, <strong>surgery or radiation</strong>, <strong>traumatic brain injury</strong> (increasingly recognized), <strong>autoimmune hypophysitis</strong> (and <strong>immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced hypophysitis</strong>, seen with ipilimumab and combination immunotherapy), <strong>infiltrative disease</strong> (sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis), <strong>Sheehan syndrome</strong> (postpartum pituitary necrosis from hemorrhagic shock), and <strong>pituitary apoplexy</strong> (sudden hemorrhage into an adenoma; emergency with headache, ophthalmoplegia, altered mental status). The presentation depends on which axes are affected; <strong>cortisol deficiency is the most urgent</strong> (can cause adrenal crisis and death) and must be treated before thyroid replacement (which can precipitate adrenal crisis by increasing cortisol clearance).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Diagnostic workup:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>MRI of the sella</strong> (gadolinium-enhanced; the imaging gold standard) to identify and characterize lesions</li>
<li><strong>Visual field testing</strong> (automated perimetry) for any lesion near the chiasm</li>
<li><strong>Hormonal testing</strong> -- pituitary axes (prolactin, IGF-1 + GH, 8 AM cortisol or corticotropin stimulation, TSH + free T4, LH/FSH + testosterone/estradiol); dynamic tests as needed (oral glucose tolerance for acromegaly, dexamethasone-CRH or inferior petrosal sinus sampling for Cushing)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Prolactinoma (first-line medical):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cabergoline</strong> (0.25-1 mg weekly; titrate to prolactin normalization) -- dopamine agonist; reduces tumor size in ~80% and restores fertility. Preferred over bromocriptine (better tolerability)</li>
<li>Surgery reserved for resistance or intolerance; dopamine agonist should be stopped once pregnant (microadenoma) or continued (macroadenoma risk of growth)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Acromegaly:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Transsphenoidal surgery</strong> -- first-line curative for confined adenoma</li>
<li><strong>Somatostatin analogs</strong> (octreotide LAR 20 mg monthly, lanreotide 120 mg monthly) -- first-line if surgery not curative; reduce GH/IGF-1 and shrink tumor</li>
<li><strong>Pegvisomant</strong> (GH receptor antagonist) -- normalizes IGF-1 in &gt;90%; for refractory disease</li>
<li><strong>Dopamine agonists</strong> (cabergoline) -- adjunctive in co-secreting tumors</li>
<li><strong>Stereotactic radiosurgery</strong> -- adjuvant for residual tumor</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cushing disease:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Transsphenoidal surgery</strong> -- first-line; remission in 65-90% of microadenomas</li>
<li><strong>Steroidogenesis inhibitors</strong> -- ketoconazole, metyrapone, <strong>osilodrostat</strong> (11beta-hydroxylase inhibitor; FDA-approved 2020) for persistent hypercortisolism</li>
<li><strong>Pasireotide</strong> (somatostatin analog) for ACTH-secreting adenoma</li>
<li><strong>Mifepristone</strong> (glucocorticoid receptor antagonist) for hyperglycemia</li>
<li><strong>Bilateral adrenalectomy</strong> -- last resort; rapid cortisol control, but Nelson syndrome risk</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Non-functioning macroadenoma:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Transsphenoidal surgery</strong> for mass effect, visual compromise, or growth</li>
<li>Surveillance for incidentally found asymptomatic microadenomas</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Hypopituitarism -- hormone replacement (in order of priority):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Glucocorticoid FIRST</strong> -- hydrocortisone 15-25 mg/day (split morning/afternoon) or prednisone 5 mg morning; <strong>stress-dose escalation</strong> during illness/surgery (double or triple; or hydrocortisone 100 mg IV for major stress)</li>
<li><strong>Levothyroxine</strong> AFTER cortisol -- 1.6 mcg/kg/day; monitor TSH (unreliable in central hypothyroidism; use free T4)</li>
<li><strong>Sex hormone replacement</strong> -- testosterone in men (gel, injection, patch); estrogen/progestin in premenopausal women</li>
<li><strong>Growth hormone</strong> -- recombinant GH in adults with documented deficiency; improves body composition and quality of life</li>
<li><strong>Desmopressin (DDAVP)</strong> for diabetes insipidus (post-surgical or stalk effect)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pituitary apoplexy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Emergency -- hydrocortisone 100 mg IV, urgent MRI, surgical decompression if vision compromised</li>
</ul>
