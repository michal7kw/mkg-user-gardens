<h1>Omega-3 (EPA)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) is a long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid with particularly potent anti-inflammatory and cardiovascular properties. EPA directly competes with arachidonic acid (AA, an omega-6 fatty acid) for cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase (LOX) enzymes, shifting eicosanoid production from pro-inflammatory series-2 prostaglandins and series-4 leukotrienes toward less inflammatory series-3 prostaglandins and series-5 leukotrienes.</p>
<p>EPA is the primary omega-3 responsible for triglyceride reduction, with pharmaceutical-grade EPA (icosapent ethyl/Vascepa) demonstrating a 25% relative risk reduction in cardiovascular events in the REDUCE-IT trial. EPA lowers triglycerides by inhibiting hepatic lipogenesis (via SREBP-1c downregulation), increasing fatty acid beta-oxidation, and reducing VLDL secretion. It also stabilizes atherosclerotic plaques and improves endothelial function.</p>
<p>Beyond cardiovascular effects, EPA shows significant benefits for mood disorders. Meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that supplements with an EPA:DHA ratio greater than 2:1 are more effective for depression than DHA-dominant formulations, likely through anti-inflammatory mechanisms in the brain and modulation of serotonin signaling. EPA generates E-series resolvins, which actively resolve inflammation and promote tissue healing.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Competes with arachidonic acid to reduce pro-inflammatory eicosanoid production</li>
<li>Lowers triglycerides by inhibiting hepatic VLDL synthesis and secretion</li>
<li>Generates E-series resolvins for active inflammation resolution</li>
<li>Supports cardiovascular health through plaque stabilization and endothelial function</li>
<li>Modulates mood and reduces depressive symptoms via neuroinflammatory pathways</li>
<li>Improves vascular reactivity and reduces blood viscosity</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines, anchovies)</li>
<li>Fish oil supplements</li>
<li>Krill oil</li>
<li>Algae oil (some formulations provide EPA)</li>
<li>Cod liver oil</li>
<li>Caviar and fish roe</li>
<li>Grass-fed meat (small amounts)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>EPA deficiency contributes to chronic low-grade inflammation, elevated triglycerides, increased cardiovascular risk, mood disturbances (particularly depression), and dry skin. A low omega-3 index (&lt;4%) is considered a cardiovascular risk factor comparable to elevated LDL cholesterol or smoking.</p>
<p>Excess EPA at very high doses (&gt;3 g/day total EPA+DHA) may increase bleeding risk, particularly in individuals on anticoagulant therapy. Gastrointestinal side effects include nausea, fishy burps, and loose stools. Very high doses may suppress immune function, though this effect has not been confirmed at typical supplemental doses (1-2 g/day). Some individuals experience a transient rise in LDL cholesterol with high-dose fish oil.</p>
