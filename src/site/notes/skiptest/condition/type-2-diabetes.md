<h1>Type 2 Diabetes</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a chronic, progressive metabolic disease characterized by sustained hyperglycemia resulting from two converging defects: <strong>insulin resistance</strong> in muscle, liver, and adipose tissue, and <strong>relative insulin deficiency</strong> caused by progressive pancreatic beta-cell dysfunction. Unlike type 1 diabetes, the pancreas continues to produce insulin, but the amount is insufficient to overcome peripheral resistance, so blood glucose rises. T2D represents roughly 90-95% of all diabetes cases worldwide and is one of the fastest-growing non-communicable diseases of the modern era.</p>
<p>The disease usually develops over many years, often preceded by a long, asymptomatic prediabetic phase. Excess visceral adiposity drives a state of low-grade chronic inflammation and elevated free fatty acids that impair insulin signaling. As resistance worsens, the beta cells compensate by hypersecreting insulin (hyperinsulinemia); over time this compensation fails, insulin output declines, and overt hyperglycemia emerges. DeFronzo's &quot;ominous octet&quot; framework expanded the classic muscle-liver-beta-cell triumvirate to include adipose tissue, the gut (incretin defect), alpha cells (excess glucagon), the kidney (increased glucose reabsorption), and the brain (impaired appetite regulation), explaining why multi-targeted therapy is often required.</p>
<p>Clinically, T2D matters because of its complications rather than the hyperglycemia itself. Chronic elevation of glucose damages both small vessels (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and large vessels (coronary artery disease, stroke, peripheral arterial disease), making cardiovascular disease the leading cause of death among people with diabetes. Because the condition is largely driven by modifiable factors, it is substantially preventable and, in early stages, can sometimes be put into remission through significant weight loss and lifestyle change.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Many people remain asymptomatic for years, and a substantial fraction are diagnosed only after a complication appears or during routine screening. When symptoms occur, they reflect osmotic effects of glucosuria and cellular energy deprivation:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Classic hyperglycemic symptoms</strong>: polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, fatigue, and blurred vision</li>
<li><strong>Insidious signs</strong>: slow wound healing, recurrent skin/urinary/fungal infections, and acanthosis nigricans (a velvety hyperpigmentation in skin folds reflecting hyperinsulinemia)</li>
<li><strong>Late presentation</strong>: peripheral neuropathy (numbness, tingling, burning pain in the feet) and, rarely, hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state in severe uncontrolled disease</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Prediabetes</strong> is defined by HbA1c 5.7-6.4%, fasting glucose 100-125 mg/dL, or 2-hour OGTT 140-199 mg/dL. C-peptide and autoantibody testing can help distinguish T2D from type 1 or latent autoimmune diabetes when the clinical picture is ambiguous.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Risk is the product of genetic susceptibility and a permissive environment. Major modifiable contributors are overweight/obesity (particularly visceral fat), physical inactivity, and an energy-dense, ultra-processed diet. Non-modifiable contributors include older age, family history, high-risk ethnicity, and a history of gestational diabetes or PCOS. Coexisting hypertension and atherogenic dyslipidemia (high triglycerides, low HDL) frequently cluster with insulin resistance as part of the metabolic syndrome.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is staged and individualized around glycemic, weight, and cardiorenal goals:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Lifestyle (foundation for everyone)</strong>: structured weight loss (5-10% of body weight yields meaningful glycemic benefit), a Mediterranean or lower-carbohydrate dietary pattern, and at least 150 minutes/week of combined aerobic and resistance exercise. The Diabetes Prevention Program showed lifestyle intervention reduced progression from prediabetes to diabetes by 58%.</li>
<li><strong>First-line pharmacotherapy</strong>: metformin remains the usual initial drug; in overweight patients UKPDS 34 demonstrated it reduced diabetes-related endpoints and mortality.</li>
<li><strong>Agents with cardiorenal benefit</strong>: GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., semaglutide) and SGLT2 inhibitors are now preferred early in patients with established cardiovascular disease, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease, independent of HbA1c.</li>
<li><strong>Advanced therapy</strong>: insulin is added when beta-cell reserve is exhausted or targets are not met on oral/injectable agents.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Sustained hyperglycemia produces <strong>microvascular</strong> complications (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and <strong>macrovascular</strong> complications (coronary artery disease, stroke, peripheral arterial disease). Diabetic kidney disease and diabetic retinopathy are leading causes of end-stage renal disease and acquired blindness, respectively, while neuropathy plus poor circulation drives foot ulceration and amputation risk. T2D also strongly associates with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>With early diagnosis and comprehensive risk-factor control, many people live long, complication-free lives, and substantial weight loss in early disease can induce remission. Prognosis worsens with longer disease duration, poor glycemic control, and the presence of cardiovascular or renal complications; cardiovascular disease remains the predominant cause of premature death.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>T2D is a global epidemic. International Diabetes Federation estimates placed the worldwide diabetes population near half a billion adults in 2019, with projections of roughly 700 million by 2045, the great majority being type 2. Rising prevalence tracks closely with increasing obesity, aging populations, and urbanization, and a disproportionate burden falls on low- and middle-income countries.</p>
