<h1>Vitamin D (25-OH)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>25-Hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH Vitamin D, calcidiol) is the primary circulating form of vitamin D and the <strong>standard biomarker used to assess vitamin D status</strong>. It is produced in the liver by hydroxylation of vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol, from sun exposure and animal sources) or vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol, from plant/fungal sources). With a half-life of approximately <strong>2-3 weeks</strong>, 25-OH D provides a reliable snapshot of vitamin D stores accumulated from both dietary intake and endogenous skin synthesis over the preceding 1-2 months. The active form of vitamin D is 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25-OH2 D, calcitriol), produced by renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase; however, 1,25-OH2 D has a short half-life (~4-6 h) and is tightly regulated, making it a poor biomarker of overall vitamin D status -- 25-OH D is the marker to measure.</p>
<p>Vitamin D functions as a <strong>prohormone</strong> with receptors (VDR) in virtually every tissue in the body. Beyond its classical role in calcium absorption and bone mineralization, vitamin D modulates immune function (both innate and adaptive immunity), regulates cell proliferation and differentiation, supports neuromuscular function, and influences gene expression of over 200 genes. Deficiency is remarkably prevalent worldwide, affecting an estimated <strong>1 billion people globally</strong>, and is associated with increased risk of osteoporosis, fractures, autoimmune diseases (multiple sclerosis, type 1 diabetes, RA), cardiovascular disease, certain cancers, depression, and all-cause mortality.</p>
<p>25-OH Vitamin D is ordered as a routine screening test in patients with risk factors for deficiency (limited sun exposure, dark skin, obesity, malabsorption, older age, chronic kidney disease) and to monitor supplementation adequacy. It is also used in the workup of unexplained hypocalcemia, elevated parathyroid hormone, bone pain, muscle weakness, and recurrent fractures.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (universally measured; Endocrine Society guideline-endorsed; foundational vitamin biomarker).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>The Endocrine Society defines sufficiency as &gt;=30 ng/mL; many functional/integrative practitioners target 40-60 ng/mL</li>
<li>Conversion: 1 ng/mL = 2.5 nmol/L</li>
<li>Pregnancy: 40-60 ng/mL recommended to support fetal skeletal development and reduce pre-eclampsia risk</li>
<li>African American populations have lower 25-OH D but higher bone density (genetic variation in vitamin D binding protein) -- debate on reference range adjustment</li>
<li>Seasonal variation: higher in summer, lower in winter</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Decrease 25-OH Vitamin D (deficiency):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insufficient sun exposure</strong> (indoor lifestyle, high latitude, sunscreen use, winter season)</li>
<li><strong>Dark skin</strong> (melanin reduces UVB-mediated synthesis)</li>
<li><strong>Dietary deficiency</strong> (low intake of fatty fish, fortified foods; vegan diet)</li>
<li><strong>Obesity</strong> (vitamin D sequestered in adipose tissue)</li>
<li><strong>Malabsorption</strong> (celiac disease, Crohn's disease, gastric bypass, cystic fibrosis)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic kidney disease</strong> (impaired 1-alpha-hydroxylation; also low 25-OH D from reduced skin synthesis)</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease</strong> (impaired 25-hydroxylation)</li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> anticonvulsants, glucocorticoids, rifampin, cholestyramine, orlistat, anti-HIV drugs</li>
<li><strong>Aging</strong> (reduced skin synthesis by ~75% from age 20 to 70)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (fetal demand)</li>
<li><strong>Premature birth</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Increase 25-OH Vitamin D:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Excessive supplementation</strong> (most common cause of toxicity; toxicity typically &gt;150 ng/mL)</li>
<li><strong>Granulomatous diseases</strong> (sarcoidosis, tuberculosis) -- extrarenal 1-alpha-hydroxylase activity</li>
<li><strong>Some lymphomas</strong></li>
<li><strong>High sun exposure</strong> (lifeguards, outdoor workers)</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D toxicity</strong> -- hypercalcemia, nausea, vomiting, polyuria, renal stones, soft tissue calcification</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low 25-OH Vitamin D is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Secondary hyperparathyroidism</strong> -- PTH rises to compensate; bone resorption accelerates</li>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis and fractures</strong> -- especially hip fractures in elderly</li>
<li><strong>Osteomalacia (adults) / rickets (children)</strong> -- defective bone mineralization</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune disease</strong> -- MS, type 1 diabetes, RA, SLE, IBD (observational evidence)</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular disease</strong> -- low vitamin D associated with hypertension, CAD, heart failure</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes / metabolic syndrome</strong> -- vitamin D improves insulin sensitivity</li>
<li><strong>Depression and cognitive decline</strong> -- VDR in brain</li>
<li><strong>Cancer</strong> -- colorectal, breast, prostate (observational; RCTs mostly negative for prevention)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy complications</strong> -- pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, gestational diabetes</li>
<li><strong>Muscle weakness, falls</strong> in elderly</li>
<li><strong>Increased all-cause mortality</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated 25-OH Vitamin D (&gt;100-150 ng/mL) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin D toxicity</strong> -- hypercalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, nephrocalcinosis, soft tissue calcification</li>
<li><strong>Granulomatous disease</strong> -- sarcoidosis, TB, lymphoma (extrarenal calcitriol production)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>25-OH D reflects status, not action</strong> -- 1,25-OH2 D (calcitriol) is the active form but is tightly regulated and not a useful clinical marker</li>
<li><strong>African American paradox</strong> -- lower 25-OH D but higher bone density; bioavailable 25-OH D may be the better marker (genetic variation in vitamin D binding protein)</li>
<li><strong>Seasonal variation</strong> complicates single measurement</li>
<li><strong>Assay variability</strong> across methods (improving with VDSP standardization)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Routine screening in at-risk populations (Endocrine Society recommendation)</li>
<li>Treatment monitoring for vitamin D supplementation (target 30-50 ng/mL)</li>
<li>Osteoporosis workup (with calcium, PTH, ALP, 24-h urine calcium)</li>
<li>Hypocalcemia / hypercalcemia evaluation</li>
<li>Autoimmune disease adjunctive management</li>
<li>Pregnancy screening</li>
<li>Pre-bariatric surgery evaluation</li>
<li>CKD monitoring</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>Vitamin D status is highly modifiable through sun exposure, diet, and supplementation:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) supplementation</strong> -- the principal intervention:
<ul>
<li><strong>Deficient (&lt;20 ng/mL):</strong> 50,000 IU weekly x 8 weeks, then 1000-2000 IU daily maintenance</li>
<li><strong>Insufficient (20-29 ng/mL):</strong> 2000-4000 IU daily x 3 months, then 1000-2000 IU daily</li>
<li><strong>Maintenance:</strong> 1000-2000 IU daily in most adults; 2000-4000 IU in obese, elderly, dark-skinned, or malabsorption patients</li>
<li><strong>Upper safety limit:</strong> 10,000 IU/day in adults (no hypercalcemia observed in trials)</li>
<li>D3 (cholecalciferol) preferred over D2 (ergocalciferol) -- more effective at raising 25-OH D</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Sensible sun exposure</strong> -- 10-30 min midday sun on arms/legs 2-3x/week (skin type dependent); SPF blocks synthesis</li>
<li><strong>Dietary sources:</strong> fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), cod liver oil, egg yolks, fortified milk, fortified cereals, mushrooms exposed to UV</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin K2 (MK-7)</strong> 100-200 mcg daily -- synergistic with vitamin D; directs calcium to bone; prevents arterial calcification</li>
<li><strong>Calcium</strong> 1000-1200 mg/day (diet + supplement) -- necessary co-substrate for bone mineralization</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium</strong> 200-400 mg daily -- required for vitamin D metabolism (hepatic and renal hydroxylases)</li>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> in obese patients (reduces adipose sequestration)</li>
<li><strong>Treat malabsorption:</strong> celiac disease (gluten-free diet); pancreatic enzymes (CF)</li>
<li><strong>Adjust dose for BMI</strong> -- obese patients need 2-3x the standard dose</li>
<li><strong>Recheck 25-OH D after 3-6 months</strong> of supplementation; adjust dose</li>
</ol>
