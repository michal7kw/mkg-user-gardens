<h1>Hematuria</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Hematuria -- the presence of red blood cells in the urine -- is one of the most important <strong>red-flag symptoms in medicine</strong> because <strong>painless gross hematuria has a 20-30% probability of underlying urothelial malignancy</strong> (bladder, kidney, or prostate cancer). It is classified as <strong>gross</strong> (visible to the naked eye -- red, pink, tea-colored, or brown urine) or <strong>microscopic</strong> (detectable only on dipstick or microscopy, defined as &gt;=3 RBCs per high-power field on urine sediment). <strong>Dipstick-positive urine without RBCs on microscopy</strong> suggests myoglobinuria or hemoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis), not true hematuria.</p>
<p>The differential diagnosis is broad and organized by anatomical source. <strong>Urologic causes</strong> ( Kidney collecting system, ureter, bladder, prostate) include <strong>urothelial carcinoma</strong> (bladder cancer is 3x more common than renal cell carcinoma), <strong>benign prostatic hyperplasia</strong>, <strong>urinary tract stones</strong>, <strong>UTI</strong>, and <strong>trauma</strong>. These typically present with <strong>painful or painless gross hematuria</strong>, and the workup centers on <strong>CT urography</strong> and <strong>cystoscopy</strong>. <strong>Glomerular (nephrologic) causes</strong> include <strong>IgA nephropathy</strong> (Berger disease; most common glomerulonephritis), <strong>post-streptococcal GN</strong>, <strong>thin basement membrane nephropathy</strong>, <strong>Alport syndrome</strong>, and <strong>ANCA-associated vasculitis</strong>. These present with <strong>microscopic hematuria with dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts, and proteinuria</strong>, and the workup centers on <strong>renal biopsy</strong> and serology.</p>
<p>The critical initial decision is whether the hematuria is <strong>glomerular</strong> (medical, nephrology workup) or <strong>non-glomerular</strong> (urologic workup with imaging and cystoscopy). <strong>RBC casts, dysmorphic RBCs, proteinuria &gt;500 mg/day, and renal insufficiency</strong> all point to a glomerular source. <strong>Blood clots</strong> never come from glomeruli (glomerular bleeding is too distal to clot). Any adult with <strong>gross hematuria or persistent microscopic hematuria</strong> requires a full urologic workup (CT urography + cystoscopy) to exclude malignancy.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Initial workup:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Urinalysis with microscopy</strong> -- the single most important test. Look for RBC casts (glomerular), dysmorphic RBCs (glomerular), WBCs/bacteria (infection), crystals (stones)</li>
<li><strong>Urine culture</strong> if UTI suspected</li>
<li><strong>Urine cytology</strong> -- for atypical cells (high specificity but low sensitivity for bladder cancer)</li>
<li><strong>Blood tests</strong> -- renal function (BUN, creatinine, eGFR), CBC (anemia), coagulation studies, PSA in men &gt;40</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Urologic workup (for non-glomerular hematuria):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>CT urography</strong> (triple-phase CT with contrast) -- the gold standard for evaluating the upper urinary tract (kidney, ureter) for masses and stones</li>
<li><strong>Cystoscopy</strong> -- the gold standard for evaluating the bladder and urethra; mandatory for any adult with gross hematuria or persistent microscopic hematuria</li>
<li><strong>Renal ultrasound</strong> -- alternative for patients who cannot receive contrast (pregnancy, renal failure)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Glomerular workup (for glomerular hematuria with casts/proteinuria):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Serology</strong> -- ANA, ANCA, anti-GBM, complement C3/C4, hepatitis B/C, HIV</li>
<li><strong>Serum protein electrophoresis</strong> (if myeloma suspected)</li>
<li><strong>Renal biopsy</strong> -- definitive diagnosis for glomerular disease</li>
<li><strong>Nephrology referral</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Management of massive hemorrhage (rare):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>3-way Foley catheter</strong> with continuous bladder irrigation to prevent clot retention</li>
<li><strong>Cystoscopic fulguration</strong> of bleeding sites</li>
<li><strong>Intravesical alum or formalin</strong> for refractory hemorrhagic cystitis</li>
<li><strong>Selective arterial embolization</strong> or <strong>cystectomy</strong> as last resort</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Specific causes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bladder cancer</strong> -- transurethral resection (TURBT); BCG or chemotherapy for high-grade</li>
<li><strong>Urinary tract stones</strong> -- removal or lithotripsy</li>
<li><strong>UTI</strong> -- appropriate antibiotics</li>
<li><strong>BPH</strong> -- 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride) reduce prostatic bleeding</li>
<li><strong>Glomerulonephritis</strong> -- immunosuppression per nephrology protocol</li>
<li><strong>Radiation cystitis</strong> -- hyperbaric oxygen; intravesical treatments</li>
</ul>
