<h1>Pseudomembranous Colitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Pseudomembranous colitis is an acute inflammatory disorder of the colon defined by the formation of adherent yellow-white plaques — pseudomembranes — composed of fibrin, inflammatory cells, mucus, and cellular debris on the colonic mucosa. In the overwhelming majority of cases it is the severe end of the spectrum of <strong>Clostridioides difficile</strong> infection (CDI). Toxin-producing C. difficile overgrows the colon after the normal protective microbiota has been disrupted, most often by antibiotic exposure, and its toxins (TcdA and TcdB) injure the epithelium and drive the inflammatory response.</p>
<p>The disease spans a wide clinical range. At the milder end it overlaps with simple antibiotic-associated diarrhea; at the severe end it produces fulminant colitis with systemic toxicity, toxic megacolon, and the risk of perforation. The central pathophysiologic event is <strong>dysbiosis</strong>: loss of colonization resistance allows spores of C. difficile, which are resistant to many antibiotics and to environmental decontamination, to germinate and proliferate. This explains why the condition is strongly tied to healthcare exposure, where both antibiotic use and environmental spore burden are high.</p>
<p>Clinically, pseudomembranous colitis is important because it is common, frequently recurs, and can be life-threatening. Diagnosis combines clinically significant diarrhea with stool testing for C. difficile and, in selected cases, direct endoscopic visualization of pseudomembranes. Management centers on stopping the offending antibiotic where possible, treating with C. difficile-active agents (fidaxomicin or vancomycin), and, for recurrent disease, restoring the microbiota through fecal transplantation.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The hallmark is watery diarrhea, which may be profuse and accompanied by mucus, lower abdominal cramping, and tenderness. Fever, anorexia, nausea, and a marked leukocytosis are common. Symptoms typically begin during or shortly after antibiotic therapy, though they can be delayed by weeks. Severe disease presents with dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, abdominal distension, and systemic toxicity; the development of ileus, marked colonic dilation, or peritoneal signs heralds fulminant colitis and toxic megacolon.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis requires both a compatible clinical picture (clinically significant, unexplained diarrhea) and laboratory confirmation of C. difficile, typically using a multistep algorithm combining glutamate dehydrogenase antigen, toxin enzyme immunoassay, and/or nucleic acid amplification testing. Endoscopy is not required but, when performed, reveals the diagnostic adherent pseudomembranes. In severe disease, abdominal imaging shows colonic wall thickening, mucosal edema (the &quot;accordion&quot; sign), and may detect megacolon or perforation.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Antibiotic exposure is the central risk factor, with clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, and broad-spectrum penicillins most strongly implicated. Healthcare contact (hospitalization or long-term care), advanced age, proton pump inhibitor use, immunosuppression, serious comorbidity, and a history of prior CDI all increase risk.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>The first step is to discontinue the inciting antibiotic whenever clinically feasible. First-line antimicrobial therapy is oral fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin, with supportive fluid and electrolyte management. Recurrent infection — a frequent and frustrating feature of CDI — is increasingly managed with fecal microbiota transplantation to restore colonization resistance. Fulminant disease complicated by toxic megacolon, perforation, or systemic deterioration may require urgent surgical colectomy.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most feared complications are toxic megacolon and colonic perforation, which can precipitate sepsis and multi-organ failure. Severe diarrhea causes dehydration and electrolyte derangement. Recurrence after apparently successful treatment is common and contributes substantially to overall morbidity.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Most patients respond to appropriate antimicrobial therapy and recover, but a significant proportion experience one or more recurrences. Fulminant disease carries a high mortality, especially among elderly and medically complex patients, underscoring the value of early recognition and, where indicated, timely surgical intervention.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>C. difficile infection is among the most common healthcare-associated infections and a leading cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhea. Its incidence rose substantially in the early 2000s with the emergence of hypervirulent strains, and community-associated cases have since become increasingly recognized. The burden falls disproportionately on older adults and those with recent healthcare exposure.</p>
