<h1>Sodium</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Sodium (Na⁺) is the principal cation of the extracellular fluid and the single most important determinant of plasma osmolality and effective circulating volume. Roughly 85–90% of plasma osmotic activity is attributable to sodium and its accompanying anions (chloride and bicarbonate), so the serum sodium concentration is, in effect, a direct readout of the ratio between total body water and total exchangeable solute. Despite its central role, the body holds serum sodium within an exceptionally narrow window (136–145 mEq/L) through tightly coupled control of water intake (thirst) and water excretion (renal handling under the influence of antidiuretic hormone).</p>
<p>Serum sodium concentration is governed more by water balance than by sodium balance per se. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, also called vasopressin), released from the posterior pituitary in response to osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus, controls how much free water the kidney reabsorbs; the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, by contrast, primarily regulates total body sodium content and volume rather than concentration. As a result, a low serum sodium usually reflects relative water excess rather than true sodium deficit, and a high serum sodium reflects relative water deficit. Spurious results can also arise: marked hyperglycemia or mannitol infusion draws water into the vascular space and lowers measured sodium (translocational hyponatremia), and severe hyperlipidemia or hyperproteinemia can cause artifactual pseudohyponatremia with certain assay methods.</p>
<p>Sodium is one of the most frequently ordered laboratory tests, included by default in the basic and comprehensive metabolic panels. It is measured whenever clinicians suspect a disorder of fluid status, in critically ill patients, in those on diuretics or intravenous fluids, and as part of routine health screening. Because both hyponatremia and hypernatremia are independently associated with increased morbidity and mortality, and because rapid correction of chronic dysnatremia can itself cause harm, accurate measurement and serial monitoring of serum sodium are clinically essential.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals are highly consistent across laboratories because sodium is measured by ion-selective electrode (ISE), a well-standardized method.</li>
<li>Direct (whole-blood/point-of-care) ISE and indirect (diluted serum/plasma) ISE can differ in the presence of severe hyperlipidemia or hyperproteinemia; direct ISE avoids pseudohyponatremia.</li>
<li>In hyperglycemia, apply a correction of approximately +1.6 to +2.4 mEq/L to measured sodium for every 100 mg/dL that glucose exceeds 100 mg/dL to estimate the &quot;true&quot; sodium.</li>
<li>The clinical severity of a dysnatremia depends as much on the rate of change as on the absolute value; chronic, slowly developing abnormalities are far better tolerated than acute ones.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Sodium (hypernatremia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Inadequate water intake or impaired thirst (elderly, infants, altered consciousness)</li>
<li>Pure water loss (fever, insensible losses, diabetes insipidus from ADH deficiency or renal resistance)</li>
<li>Hypotonic fluid loss (osmotic diuresis, diarrhea, sweating)</li>
<li>Excess mineralocorticoid activity (Cushing syndrome, primary hyperaldosteronism)</li>
<li>Excessive sodium intake or hypertonic saline/sodium bicarbonate administration</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Sodium (hyponatremia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)</li>
<li>Hypervolemic states with impaired water excretion (heart failure, liver cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome)</li>
<li>Thiazide diuretic use</li>
<li>Adrenal insufficiency and cortisol deficiency</li>
<li>Primary polydipsia and excessive free-water intake (including exercise-associated hyponatremia)</li>
<li>Severe hyperglycemia (translocational/dilutional effect)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels (hypernatremia) are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dehydration / water deficit</strong> — the most common cause; reflects loss of free water in excess of sodium, typically with impaired access to water.</li>
<li><strong>Diabetes insipidus</strong> — central (ADH-deficient) or nephrogenic (ADH-resistant) forms cause excessive renal free-water loss and rising sodium.</li>
<li><strong>Cushing syndrome / mineralocorticoid excess</strong> — sodium retention with relative water handling shifts can raise serum sodium.</li>
<li><strong>Hypertension</strong> — chronic positive sodium balance contributes to volume expansion and blood pressure elevation.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels (hyponatremia) are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>SIADH</strong> — inappropriate ADH release retains free water, diluting serum sodium; a leading cause of euvolemic hyponatremia.</li>
<li><strong>Heart failure and liver cirrhosis</strong> — reduced effective arterial blood volume triggers ADH release and water retention; hyponatremia is a marker of disease severity and worse prognosis.</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal insufficiency</strong> — cortisol and aldosterone deficiency impair free-water excretion and sodium retention.</li>
<li><strong>Diuretic therapy</strong> — particularly thiazides, which impair urinary dilution.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Serial monitoring during correction of dysnatremia to avoid overly rapid changes (limit chronic hyponatremia correction to about 6–8 mEq/L per 24 hours to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome).</li>
<li>Surveillance in patients on diuretics, IV fluids, or with heart failure, cirrhosis, or kidney disease.</li>
<li>Assessment of volume and water status in critically ill and post-operative patients.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Identify and treat the underlying cause</strong> — dysnatremia is a sign, not a disease; management hinges on classifying volume status (hypo-, eu-, or hypervolemic) and treating the driver (e.g., stopping the offending diuretic, treating heart failure, addressing SIADH).</li>
<li><strong>Correct chronic hyponatremia slowly</strong> — limit the rise to ~6–8 mEq/L per 24 hours and ~18 mEq/L per 48 hours to avoid osmotic demyelination; severe symptomatic cases may warrant cautious 3% hypertonic saline under close monitoring.</li>
<li><strong>Restrict or liberalize free water appropriately</strong> — fluid restriction for SIADH and hypervolemic hyponatremia; replace free-water deficit (oral water or hypotonic IV fluid) for hypernatremia, again correcting chronic cases gradually.</li>
<li><strong>Adjust medications</strong> — review diuretics, antidepressants (SSRIs), and other drugs known to provoke SIADH or hyponatremia.</li>
<li><strong>Address dietary sodium in the context of comorbidity</strong> — sodium restriction is appropriate for hypertension and fluid-overload states, while ensuring adequate water and electrolyte intake during heavy exertion or illness to prevent dysnatremia.</li>
</ol>
