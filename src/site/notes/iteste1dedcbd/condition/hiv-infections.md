<h1>HIV Infections</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection is a <strong>chronic, progressive viral infection</strong> caused by HIV-1 (predominant globally) or HIV-2 (mainly West Africa). The virus infects and destroys <strong>CD4+ T lymphocytes</strong>, macrophages, and dendritic cells, producing progressive immunodeficiency that -- untreated -- leads to <strong>AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome)</strong> and death. Approximately <strong>39 million people</strong> are living with HIV worldwide, with <strong>~1.3 million new infections and 630,000 deaths annually</strong> (2022 estimates). The introduction of <strong>combination antiretroviral therapy (ART)</strong> in 1996 transformed HIV from a universally fatal disease into a <strong>manageable chronic condition</strong> with near-normal life expectancy; viral suppression also eliminates sexual transmission (<strong>U=U: undetectable = untransmittable</strong>).</p>
<p>The virus enters cells via <strong>CD4</strong> and a co-receptor (<strong>CCR5</strong> in early infection; <strong>CXCR4</strong> in later disease). After reverse transcription and integration into the host genome, HIV establishes a <strong>latent reservoir</strong> in resting memory CD4+ T cells that current therapy cannot eradicate, making cure elusive. Untreated, the typical course progresses from <strong>acute retroviral syndrome</strong> (2-4 weeks post-exposure; a mononucleosis-like illness with fever, lymphadenopathy, pharyngitis, rash) through a <strong>clinical latency period</strong> of 8-10 years (asymptomatic but with ongoing viral replication and CD4 decline) to <strong>AIDS</strong> (CD4 &lt;200 cells/mm3), characterized by <strong>opportunistic infections</strong> (<em>Pneumocystis</em> pneumonia, toxoplasmosis, cryptococcal meningitis, CMV, MAC), <strong>malignancy</strong> (Kaposi sarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, cervical cancer), and <strong>HIV wasting syndrome</strong>.</p>
<p>The modern approach to HIV is rooted in three principles: <strong>(1) universal testing</strong> (identify all infected individuals), <strong>(2) immediate ART for all</strong> (regardless of CD4 count -- prevents disease progression, transmission, and immune destruction), and <strong>(3) biomedical prevention</strong> (PrEP, PEP, and treatment-as-prevention). With these strategies, <strong>new infections have declined ~40%</strong> since their peak in the late 1990s, and several countries are approaching epidemic control. The remaining challenges are <strong>diagnosis of the ~15% of infected individuals who are unaware</strong>, <strong>retention in care and adherence</strong>, <strong>stigma and discrimination</strong>, and <strong>comorbidities</strong> (cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, neurocognitive dysfunction, hepatitis co-infection) that accumulate with chronic infection and therapy.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Diagnosis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fourth-generation antigen/antibody combination immunoassay</strong> (detects HIV-1/HIV-2 antibodies and p24 antigen; window period ~2-6 weeks) -- standard screening test</li>
<li><strong>HIV-1/HIV-2 differentiation immunoassay</strong> (follow-up if screen positive)</li>
<li><strong>HIV RNA nucleic acid test (NAT)</strong> -- if acute infection suspected (window 10-33 days) or to confirm indeterminate results</li>
<li><strong>Baseline labs</strong> after diagnosis: CD4 count, HIV viral load, genotypic resistance testing, HLA-B*5701 (if abacavir planned), hepatitis B and C serology, TORCH, TB screening, renal function, lipid panel</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Antiretroviral therapy (ART) -- start immediately for all patients regardless of CD4:</strong></p>
<p><strong>First-line regimens (INSTI-based):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (Biktarvy)</strong> -- single tablet daily; preferred for most patients; high barrier to resistance</li>
<li><strong>Dolutegravir + tenofovir/emtricitabine (or lamivudine)</strong> -- alternative single-tablet or multi-tablet</li>
<li><strong>Dolutegravir + lamivudine (Dovato)</strong> -- 2-drug regimen; for treatment-naive with viral load &lt;500,000 and no resistance</li>
<li><strong>Cabotegravir + rilpivirine</strong> -- long-acting injectable monthly or bimonthly; for virologically suppressed patients who prefer injections over daily pills</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Special situations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> -- <strong>dolutegravir-based regimen</strong> is now first-line in pregnancy (concerns about neural tube defects largely resolved); goal is viral suppression by delivery</li>
<li><strong>Renal impairment</strong> -- avoid tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF; nephrotoxic); use tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) or abacavir</li>
<li><strong>Tuberculosis co-infection</strong> -- adjust ART timing relative to TB therapy (rifampin interactions with INSTIs/NNRTIs); start ART within 2 weeks for CD4 &lt;50</li>
<li><strong>Hepatitis B co-infection</strong> -- use tenofovir-containing regimen (treats both HIV and HBV); never stop tenofovir abruptly (HBV flare)</li>
<li><strong>Hepatitis C co-infection</strong> -- treat HCV with direct-acting antivirals; check ART-HCV drug interactions</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>HIV viral load</strong> at 2-4 weeks, then every 3-6 months (target: undetectable &lt;50 copies/mL within 3-6 months of starting)</li>
<li><strong>CD4 count</strong> every 3-6 months until &gt;300 stable, then annually</li>
<li><strong>Resistance testing</strong> if virologic failure (viral load &gt;200)</li>
<li><strong>Renal function, lipids, glucose</strong> annually</li>
<li><strong>Bone density</strong> (DEXA) if risk factors</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Opportunistic infection prophylaxis (based on CD4 count):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>CD4 &lt;200</strong> -- <strong>trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX)</strong> for <em>Pneumocystis</em> pneumonia prophylaxis (single-strength daily)</li>
<li><strong>CD4 &lt;50</strong> -- add <strong>azithromycin weekly</strong> for <em>Mycobacterium avium complex</em> (MAC) prophylaxis</li>
<li><strong>Discontinue prophylaxis</strong> when CD4 rises above thresholds for 3+ months on ART</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Prevention:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)</strong> -- <strong>tenofovir/emtricitabine daily</strong> (FDA-approved; reduces sexual transmission by ~99%) OR <strong>cabotegravir long-acting injection</strong> every 2 months (FDA-approved 2021; CABOTYA). Monitor renal function and HIV status quarterly</li>
<li><strong>Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)</strong> -- 3-drug regimen (e.g., tenofovir/emtricitabine + raltegravir or dolutegravir) for 28 days, started within <strong>72 hours</strong> of exposure</li>
<li><strong>Treatment as prevention</strong> -- viral suppression prevents sexual transmission (U=U)</li>
<li><strong>Perinatal</strong> -- ART in pregnancy reduces mother-to-child transmission to &lt;1%</li>
<li><strong>Vaccination</strong> -- hepatitis A and B, influenza, pneumococcal, HPV, COVID-19, zoster</li>
</ul>
