<h1>Vitamin A</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient encompassing preformed retinoids (retinol and its esters, retinyl palmitate and retinyl acetate) found in animal foods, as distinct from provitamin-A carotenoids such as beta-carotene. As a supplement, it is given as preformed retinol or retinyl esters to correct or prevent deficiency.</p>
<p>Vitamin A deficiency is one of the most significant preventable causes of childhood blindness and contributes to excess mortality from infectious disease, particularly measles and diarrheal illness, in regions where intake is inadequate. Beyond vision, vitamin A is central to epithelial and mucosal health and to a properly functioning immune system: its active metabolite, retinoic acid, regulates the differentiation of epithelial cells and shapes adaptive immunity, including the production of secretory IgA at mucosal surfaces.</p>
<p>This supplemental retinol entity is deliberately distinct from the dietary &quot;Vitamin A&quot; nutrient and from serum-retinol biomarkers—it represents the supplemented compound, its dosing, and its clinical uses.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> — Strong clinical-trial and systematic-review evidence (including Cochrane reviews) that vitamin A supplementation reduces morbidity and mortality in deficient children and improves outcomes in measles; robust mechanistic evidence for its roles in vision, epithelial integrity, and mucosal immunity. Benefit is greatest in deficiency; routine supplementation in replete individuals carries toxicity risk.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Retinol is reversibly oxidized to retinal and then irreversibly to <strong>retinoic acid</strong>, the principal signaling metabolite. Retinoic acid binds nuclear retinoic-acid receptors (RAR) and retinoid-X receptors (RXR), which act as transcription factors controlling genes for epithelial differentiation, mucin secretion, and immune-cell development.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vision:</strong> In retinal photoreceptors, 11-cis-retinal binds opsin to form rhodopsin; light isomerizes it, initiating the phototransduction cascade required for low-light (scotopic) vision. Deficiency causes night blindness and, if severe, xerophthalmia.</li>
<li><strong>Mucosal immunity:</strong> Intestinal dendritic cells convert vitamin A to retinoic acid, which imprints gut-homing receptors on lymphocytes and promotes IgA class-switch recombination, supporting secretory IgA at mucosal surfaces.</li>
<li><strong>Barrier integrity:</strong> Retinoic acid maintains the differentiation and turnover of respiratory, gastrointestinal, and ocular epithelia, reinforcing physical defenses against pathogens.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dosage</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adult RDA:</strong> ~700 mcg RAE/day (women), ~900 mcg RAE/day (men)</li>
<li><strong>Supplemental repletion:</strong> 1500-3000 mcg RAE (≈5,000-10,000 IU)/day</li>
<li><strong>Deficiency / high-dose protocols:</strong> 60,000-200,000 IU under medical supervision (e.g. WHO measles and xerophthalmia regimens)</li>
<li><strong>Tolerable upper intake:</strong> 3000 mcg RAE/day for adults; chronic excess is hepatotoxic and, in pregnancy, teratogenic</li>
<li><strong>Timing:</strong> With a fat-containing meal to optimize absorption</li>
</ul>
