<h1>Hematocrit</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Hematocrit (Hct), also called packed cell volume (PCV), is the fraction of whole blood composed of red blood cells by volume. After anticoagulated blood is centrifuged, the hematocrit is the ratio of the red cell column to the total blood column. Modern automated hematology analyzers calculate hematocrit from the measured red blood cell count and mean corpuscular volume (Hct = RBC x MCV), providing results within minutes.</p>
<p>Hematocrit is regulated primarily by erythropoietin (EPO), a hormone produced by the kidneys in response to tissue oxygen sensing. Increased EPO stimulates erythropoiesis in the bone marrow, raising hematocrit, while decreased EPO production or bone marrow suppression lowers it. Hydration status also significantly influences hematocrit acutely through plasma volume changes -- dehydration concentrates red cells (apparent elevation), while overhydration dilutes them (apparent reduction).</p>
<p>Hematocrit is measured as part of the complete blood count (CBC), one of the most commonly ordered laboratory tests. It is used to screen for anemia and polycythemia, monitor response to iron or vitamin supplementation, assess hydration, and evaluate chronic disease progression. Because elevated hematocrit increases blood viscosity, persistently high values are associated with increased cardiovascular and thrombotic risk.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference ranges vary by laboratory and analyzer; always compare to local lab reference values</li>
<li>Values are approximately 3% lower when measured by centrifugation compared to automated calculation</li>
<li>Hematocrit is typically 1-2% higher in capillary blood samples compared to venous samples</li>
<li>Newborns have higher hematocrit (44-64%) that gradually declines to adult ranges by adolescence</li>
<li>Pregnancy causes hemodilution, reducing hematocrit by 5-10% from baseline</li>
<li>High-altitude residents have higher baseline hematocrit due to chronic physiological adaptation</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Hematocrit:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Dehydration (reduced plasma volume concentrates red cells)</li>
<li>High altitude living (chronic hypoxia stimulates erythropoietin)</li>
<li>Chronic hypoxia from lung disease (COPD, sleep apnea) stimulates EPO</li>
<li>Polycythemia vera (JAK2 mutation causes uncontrolled erythropoiesis)</li>
<li>Erythropoietin administration (exogenous EPO stimulates RBC production)</li>
<li>Testosterone therapy (stimulates erythropoiesis via androgen receptors in marrow)</li>
<li>Smoking (carbon monoxide reduces oxygen carrying capacity, compensatory erythropoiesis)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Hematocrit:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Iron deficiency (impaired hemoglobin synthesis limits RBC production)</li>
<li>Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency (impaired DNA synthesis causes megaloblastic anemia)</li>
<li>Chronic kidney disease (reduced EPO production)</li>
<li>Blood loss (acute hemorrhage or chronic GI losses)</li>
<li>Hemolysis (premature RBC destruction exceeds marrow production capacity)</li>
<li>Bone marrow failure (aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndromes)</li>
<li>Overhydration (expanded plasma volume dilutes RBC concentration)</li>
<li>Pregnancy (physiological hemodilution from expanded plasma volume)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Polycythemia Vera</strong> -- a myeloproliferative neoplasm driven by JAK2 mutation, causing uncontrolled RBC production. Hematocrit &gt;52% in men or &gt;48% in women is a major diagnostic criterion (PMID: 39556352)</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular Risk</strong> -- elevated hematocrit increases blood viscosity, promoting thrombosis. The Framingham Study demonstrated a 34-year association between high hematocrit and cardiovascular disease (PMID: 8122618)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease</strong> -- chronic hypoxia drives compensatory erythropoiesis, raising hematocrit as a secondary polycythemia</li>
<li><strong>Obstructive Sleep Apnea</strong> -- intermittent nocturnal hypoxia stimulates EPO production, leading to elevated hematocrit in moderate to severe cases</li>
<li><strong>Thrombotic Risk</strong> -- higher hematocrit increases red cell-mediated procoagulant activity and impairs microvascular perfusion (PMID: 28811305)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron Deficiency Anemia</strong> -- the most common cause of low hematocrit worldwide, with microcytic RBCs reducing the packed cell volume</li>
<li><strong>Chronic Kidney Disease</strong> -- progressive loss of renal EPO production leads to normocytic anemia, with hematocrit declining as GFR falls (PMID: 16377382)</li>
<li><strong>Megaloblastic Anemia</strong> -- B12 or folate deficiency causes macrocytic RBCs; despite larger cells, total RBC count is low, reducing hematocrit</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Screening for anemia in routine health examinations</li>
<li>Monitoring response to iron, B12, or folate supplementation</li>
<li>Surveillance of polycythemia vera treatment targets</li>
<li>Assessing hydration status in acute care settings</li>
<li>Pre-operative evaluation for surgical fitness</li>
<li>Monitoring hematocrit targets during testosterone therapy</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Iron supplementation</strong> -- for iron-deficient individuals, 65-100 mg elemental iron daily (as ferrous sulfate, gluconate, or bisglycinate) can raise hematocrit by 3-6% over 8-12 weeks</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 supplementation</strong> -- for B12-deficient individuals, 1000 mcg cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin daily corrects megaloblastic anemia and restores hematocrit within 4-8 weeks</li>
<li><strong>Folate supplementation</strong> -- 400-1000 mcg folic acid daily corrects folate-deficiency anemia, with hematocrit improvement typically within 4-6 weeks</li>
<li><strong>Hydration optimization</strong> -- for dehydration-related elevations, adequate fluid intake normalizes plasma volume and reduces falsely elevated hematocrit within 24-48 hours</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying hypoxia</strong> -- addressing COPD, sleep apnea, or smoking eliminates chronic hypoxic erythropoietin drive and reduces secondary polycythemia</li>
<li><strong>Blood donation or phlebotomy</strong> -- for polycythemia vera, therapeutic phlebotomy maintains hematocrit below 45% to reduce thrombotic risk (PMID: 32974939)</li>
</ol>
