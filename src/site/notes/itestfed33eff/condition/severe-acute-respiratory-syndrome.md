<h1>Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) is a viral respiratory illness caused by the <strong>SARS-associated coronavirus (SARS-CoV-1)</strong>, a novel coronavirus that emerged in <strong>Guangdong province, China, in November 2002</strong> and spread globally through air travel to infect <strong>8,096 people across 29 countries</strong>, causing <strong>774 deaths</strong> (case fatality rate ~9.6%) before being contained in July 2003. No cases have been reported since 2004.</p>
<p>SARS-CoV-1 is a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA betacoronavirus closely related to bat coronaviruses. The virus is believed to have jumped from <strong>bats to civet cats</strong> (an intermediate host sold in live animal markets) and then to humans. The virus enters human cells via the <strong>ACE2 receptor</strong> (angiotensin-converting enzyme 2), which is highly expressed in the lungs and gastrointestinal tract.</p>
<p>The clinical syndrome is characterized by an <strong>incubation period of 2-10 days</strong>, followed by a <strong>prodrome of high fever (&gt;38C)</strong>, malaise, headache, and myalgia. After 2-7 days, the respiratory phase begins with dry cough, dyspnea, and rapidly progressive <strong>patchy interstitial pneumonia</strong> visible on chest imaging. Approximately <strong>20-30% of patients required intensive care</strong> and mechanical ventilation. Superspreading events (where a single individual infected many contacts) were a hallmark of the outbreak, particularly in healthcare settings (Hong Kong, Toronto, Singapore).</p>
<p>The SARS outbreak was contained through <strong>aggressive public health measures</strong>: case isolation, contact tracing and quarantine, travel restrictions, airport screening, and stringent infection control (N95 respirators, negative-pressure rooms). The SARS experience informed global pandemic preparedness and directly influenced the response to SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) in 2020.</p>
<p><strong>Distinguishing SARS-CoV-1 from SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19):</strong> SARS-CoV-2 has a much higher transmissibility (R0 ~2.5-6 vs ~2-3 for SARS-CoV-1), longer asymptomatic infectious period, lower case fatality (~1-3% vs ~9.6%), and much larger global spread (&gt;700 million cases vs ~8,000). However, SARS-CoV-1 caused more severe disease per case and had higher mortality in those infected.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>No specific antiviral therapy proven effective:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ribavirin</strong> was widely used during the 2003 outbreak but showed limited efficacy and significant toxicity (hemolytic anemia, teratogenicity)</li>
<li><strong>Lopinavir/ritonavir</strong> (HIV protease inhibitors) showed no benefit in the only RCT conducted during the outbreak</li>
<li><strong>Interferon</strong> had in vitro activity but limited clinical evidence</li>
<li><strong>Remdesivir</strong> (developed post-SARS) has in vitro activity against SARS-CoV-1 but was not available during the outbreak</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Supportive care (the mainstay):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Oxygen supplementation</strong> for hypoxemia</li>
<li><strong>Mechanical ventilation</strong> for respiratory failure (lung-protective ventilation: low tidal volume 6 mL/kg ideal body weight; plateau pressure &lt;30 cm H2O)</li>
<li><strong>Prone positioning</strong> for severe ARDS</li>
<li><strong>ECMO</strong> (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) for refractory respiratory failure</li>
<li><strong>Hemodynamic support</strong> for septic shock</li>
<li><strong>Renal replacement therapy</strong> for AKI</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Corticosteroids (controversial):</strong> Methylprednisolone was widely used during the 2003 outbreak for the severe inflammatory response, but evidence was mixed; some studies suggested benefit for late-stage disease but increased risk of secondary infection, avascular necrosis, and neuromuscular weakness</p>
<p><strong>Infection control (critical for prevention of nosocomial spread):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Airborne precautions:</strong> negative-pressure isolation rooms; N95 respirators or PAPR for healthcare workers</li>
<li><strong>Contact precautions:</strong> gowns, gloves</li>
<li><strong>Eye protection:</strong> face shields or goggles</li>
<li><strong>Hand hygiene</strong></li>
<li><strong>Visitor restriction</strong></li>
</ul>
