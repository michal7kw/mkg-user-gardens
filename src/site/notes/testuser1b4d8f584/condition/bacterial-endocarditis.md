<h1>Bacterial Endocarditis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Infective endocarditis (IE) is a <strong>microbial infection of the endocardial surface of the heart</strong>, most commonly involving the <strong>cardiac valves</strong> but also possible on septal defects, mural endocardium, or prosthetic material. Despite advances in diagnosis and therapy, IE remains a <strong>life-threatening disease</strong> with <strong>in-hospital mortality of 15-25% and 1-year mortality approaching 40%</strong>. The incidence is <strong>3-10 per 100,000 person-years</strong> and rising, driven by an aging population, more prosthetic valves and indwelling devices, and the <strong>opioid epidemic</strong> (injection drug use-associated IE now accounts for ~10-20% of cases in the US).</p>
<p>The pathophysiology involves <strong>endothelial damage</strong> (from turbulent flow, prosthetic material, or catheter trauma) followed by <strong>deposition of platelets and fibrin</strong> (non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis), which then becomes <strong>seeded with bacteria during transient bacteremia</strong>. The resulting <strong>vegetation</strong> is a mass of fibrin, platelets, and microorganisms that destroys valve tissue, produces emboli, and seeds distant sites. <strong>Viridans streptococci</strong> (oral flora) were historically the most common cause, but <strong>Staphylococcus aureus</strong> is now the leading pathogen overall (~30-40% of cases), reflecting the rise of healthcare-associated and injection-drug-use IE. <strong>Enterococci, coagulase-negative staphylococci</strong> (especially PVE), the <strong>HACEK group</strong> (Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella), and fungi (Candida in IV drug users or immunocompromised) are other causes.</p>
<p>The clinical presentation ranges from <strong>acute</strong> (S. aureus; high fever, rapid valve destruction, sepsis) to <strong>subacute</strong> (viridans streptococci; insidious weeks-months of low-grade fever, weight loss, anemia). The classic <strong>peripheral stigmata</strong> -- <strong>Osler nodes</strong> (painful nodules on finger pads), <strong>Janeway lesions</strong> (painless macules on palms/soles), <strong>splinter hemorrhages</strong>, <strong>Roth spots</strong> (retinal hemorrhages with pale centers), and <strong>petechiae</strong> -- are now uncommon (~5-15%) due to earlier diagnosis, but are specific when present. Diagnosis uses the <strong>modified Duke criteria</strong>, integrating blood culture results, echocardiographic evidence of vegetation, and clinical features.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Diagnosis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Three sets of blood cultures</strong> from separate sites before antibiotics (the most important diagnostic step); typical pathogen isolation in 90-95% of cases</li>
<li><strong>Echocardiography</strong> -- <strong>transthoracic echo (TTE)</strong> first; <strong>transesophageal echo (TEE)</strong> if TTE negative or suspicion persists (TEE sensitivity ~90-100% vs TTE ~60%); shows vegetation, abscess, prosthetic involvement</li>
<li><strong>Modified Duke criteria</strong> -- major (typical organism in blood cultures, echo evidence) and minor (fever, vascular/immunologic phenomena, predisposing heart condition); 2 major OR 1 major + 3 minor OR 5 minor = definite</li>
<li><strong>PCR, serology</strong> for culture-negative endocarditis (Bartonella, Coxiella)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Empiric antibiotic therapy (start immediately after cultures):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Native valve, community-acquired</strong> -- <strong>vancomycin</strong> 15-20 mg/kg q8-12h + <strong>cefepime</strong> 2 g q8h; add <strong>gentamicin</strong> if enterococcus suspected</li>
<li><strong>Prosthetic valve (&lt;1 year)</strong> -- vancomycin + gentamicin + cefepime + rifampin (covers coag-neg staph, S. aureus, gram-negatives)</li>
<li><strong>Prosthetic valve (&gt;1 year)</strong> -- same as native valve</li>
<li><strong>Injection drug use</strong> -- vancomycin (covers MRSA) +/- gentamicin; right-sided often monotherapy acceptable</li>
<li>Adjust to organism and susceptibility once identified (targeted therapy 4-6 weeks typically)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Targeted therapy examples:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Viridans streptococci (penicillin-susceptible)</strong> -- penicillin G or ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily x 4 weeks; or 2 weeks with gentamicin for selected cases</li>
<li><strong>Methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA)</strong> -- nafcillin or oxacillin 2 g IV q4h x 6 weeks; add gentamicin for first 3-5 days for native valve (controversial)</li>
<li><strong>MRSA</strong> -- vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg q8-12h x 6 weeks; daptomycin alternative</li>
<li><strong>Enterococcus</strong> -- ampicillin + gentamicin (or ceftriaxone) x 4-6 weeks; linezolid or daptomycin for resistant strains</li>
<li><strong>HACEK</strong> -- ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily x 4 weeks</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Surgical indications (life-saving; ~50% of IE patients ultimately need surgery):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Heart failure</strong> from valvular dysfunction (the most common surgical indication)</li>
<li><strong>Uncontrolled infection</strong> (persistent bacteremia &gt;5-7 days on appropriate antibiotics, fungal or resistant organisms, perivalvular abscess, fistula)</li>
<li><strong>Prevention of embolism</strong> -- large (&gt;10 mm) mobile vegetations with prior embolic event, or very large (&gt;15 mm) vegetations</li>
<li><strong>Prosthetic valve endocarditis</strong> with dehiscence or obstruction</li>
<li>Timing: <strong>early surgery</strong> (during initial hospitalization) for the above; not all surgery can wait for antibiotic cure</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Management of complications:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Heart failure</strong> -- diuretics, afterload reduction, monitor for surgery</li>
<li><strong>Embolic stroke</strong> -- most occur before or within first 2 weeks of antibiotics; neuroimaging; withhold anticoagulation/antiplatelets; surgery after stroke if large hemorrhage, otherwise proceed when stable</li>
<li><strong>Renal failure</strong> -- immune complex glomerulonephritis or ATN; supportive; may need temporary dialysis</li>
<li><strong>Splenic abscess</strong> -- percutaneous drainage or splenectomy</li>
<li><strong>Mycotic aneurysm</strong> -- monitor, embolize, or surgically clip</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Antibiotic prophylaxis (for future procedures):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>High-risk patients only: <strong>prosthetic valves, prior IE, certain congenital heart disease, cardiac transplant with valvulopathy</strong></li>
<li><strong>Amoxicillin 2 g PO 30-60 min before dental procedures</strong> (or ampicillin IV / cephalexin / azithromycin if penicillin-allergic)</li>
<li><strong>No longer recommended</strong> for routine GI/GU procedures</li>
</ul>
