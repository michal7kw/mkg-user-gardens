<h1>Reverse T3</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Reverse T3 (rT3) is a biologically inactive isomer of triiodothyronine generated when the body deiodinates thyroxine (T4) at the <strong>inner (tyrosyl) ring</strong> (via type 3 deiodinase, D3) rather than the outer ring that yields active T3 (via type 1 or 2 deiodinase, D1/D2). Because rT3 cannot bind the nuclear thyroid hormone receptor to drive metabolism, rising rT3 represents a <strong>hormone-conservation response</strong> that lowers metabolic demand during stress, illness, or starvation.</p>
<p>Elevated rT3 is the metabolic signature of <strong>non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS, also called euthyroid sick syndrome)</strong>, in which acute or chronic illness, sepsis, surgery, trauma, starvation, or caloric restriction suppresses D1 (which produces active T3) while favoring D3 (which produces rT3). Chronic psychological stress, elevated cortisol, systemic inflammation, and <strong>selenium deficiency</strong> (selenium is a cofactor for deiodinase enzymes) can all increase rT3. The marker is therefore most useful as a context-dependent indicator of impaired peripheral thyroid hormone conversion and utilization rather than as a standalone diagnosis.</p>
<p>Interpretation should always be paired with <strong>TSH, Free T3, and Free T4</strong>. A common clinical pattern is normal or low-normal TSH with low Free T3 and elevated rT3 -- suggesting the body is preferentially inactivating thyroid hormone. Functional medicine practitioners use the <strong>Free T3 to Reverse T3 ratio</strong> (target &gt;0.20) as a tissue-level hypothyroidism indicator; mainstream endocrinology does not endorse this. Low rT3 is uncommon and generally not clinically significant, though it may accompany hyperthyroidism or vigorous T4-to-T3 conversion.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 3/5</strong> (well-validated mechanistically; functional-medicine use case; not in mainstream endocrine guidelines for routine practice).</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase rT3:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acute or chronic illness</strong> (non-thyroidal illness syndrome; the most common cause of elevated rT3 in hospitalized patients)</li>
<li><strong>Severe caloric restriction or fasting</strong> (adaptive response to conserve energy)</li>
<li><strong>High cortisol / chronic psychological stress</strong> (cortisol inhibits D1, favoring D3)</li>
<li><strong>Systemic inflammation</strong> (sepsis, RA, IBD)</li>
<li><strong>Surgery, trauma, burns</strong></li>
<li><strong>Selenium deficiency</strong> (D1 and D2 are selenium-dependent)</li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> amiodarone (inhibits T4-to-T3 conversion), beta-blockers (high-dose propranolol), glucocorticoids, propylthiouracil, dexamethasone</li>
<li><strong>Chronic kidney disease, liver cirrhosis</strong></li>
<li><strong>Aging</strong></li>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency</strong> (iron required for deiodinase function)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease rT3:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong> (T4-to-T3 conversion favored)</li>
<li><strong>Robust peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion</strong></li>
<li><strong>Recovery from acute illness</strong></li>
<li><strong>Selenium repletion</strong> (in deficient patients)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated rT3 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS):</strong> the most common context; elevated rT3 with low T3; typically resolves with recovery from underlying illness</li>
<li><strong>Chronic stress / HPA-axis dysregulation:</strong> functional medicine interprets elevated rT3 as a sign of &quot;tissue-level hypothyroidism&quot; -- the body cannot effectively utilize thyroid hormones despite normal TSH/FT4</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction, anorexia nervosa:</strong> adaptive response</li>
<li><strong>Severe illness, sepsis, post-surgical:</strong> poor prognosis when rT3 very elevated</li>
<li><strong>Selenium deficiency:</strong> impaired deiodinase function</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Functional medicine FT3/rT3 ratio:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Ratio &gt;0.20: normal tissue-level thyroid hormone action</li>
<li>Ratio &lt;0.20: &quot;tissue-level hypothyroidism&quot; (controversial; not in mainstream guidelines)</li>
<li>Some practitioners add liothyronine (T3) therapy for patients with low ratio despite normal TSH (off-label; not evidence-based)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Not in mainstream endocrine guidelines</strong> for routine thyroid testing</li>
<li><strong>Acute illness confounds</strong> in hospitalized patients</li>
<li><strong>Significant diurnal and inter-individual variability</strong></li>
<li><strong>Functional-medicine interpretation (FT3/rT3 ratio) not validated</strong> in large trials</li>
<li><strong>Assay variability</strong> across labs</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Functional medicine: comprehensive thyroid workup (with TSH, FT3, FT4)</li>
<li>Research: NTIS and stress response studies</li>
<li>Some clinicians: persistent hypothyroid symptoms despite normal TSH (controversial)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>Elevated rT3 usually reflects an underlying stressor; management targets the root cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat acute illness</strong> (the principal cause of elevated rT3 in hospitalized patients)</li>
<li><strong>Restore adequate caloric and protein intake</strong> (in caloric restriction / anorexia)</li>
<li><strong>Improve sleep</strong> and <strong>reduce chronic physiological and psychological stress</strong></li>
<li><strong>Correct selenium deficiency</strong> -- 200 mcg daily; supports D1 and D2 function</li>
<li><strong>Adaptogenic support</strong> -- Ashwagandha 300-600 mg daily (small studies show thyroid support); Rhodiola, Holy Basil (research-stage)</li>
<li><strong>Adequate iron status</strong> -- iron is required for deiodinase function; treat iron deficiency</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> rT3-elevating medications (amiodarone, high-dose beta-blockers, glucocorticoids) when possible</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying chronic inflammation</strong> (RA, IBD, infection)</li>
<li><strong>Some functional medicine practitioners add liothyronine (T3)</strong> for patients with low FT3/rT3 ratio and persistent hypothyroid symptoms despite normal TSH (off-label; not evidence-based; ATA does not recommend)</li>
<li><strong>Address comorbidities:</strong> CKD, cirrhosis, depression</li>
</ol>
