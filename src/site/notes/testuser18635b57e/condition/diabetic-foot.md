<h1>Diabetic Foot</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The diabetic foot is one of the most devastating complications of diabetes mellitus, resulting from the <strong>triad of peripheral neuropathy, peripheral artery disease (PAD), and infection</strong>. Approximately <strong>15-25% of patients with diabetes</strong> will develop a foot ulcer during their lifetime, and diabetes is the leading cause of <strong>non-traumatic lower extremity amputation</strong> worldwide (a lower extremity is lost to diabetes every 20 seconds globally).</p>
<p><strong>Pathophysiology -- the three-component model:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Peripheral neuropathy</strong> (~60% of diabetic foot ulcers):
<ul>
<li><strong>Sensory neuropathy:</strong> loss of protective sensation (LOPS); patients do not feel injury from footwear, stepping on objects, or thermal injury. This is assessed with the <strong>10g monofilament</strong> test (loss of sensation at &gt;=4 of 10 sites on the plantar surface = LOPS)</li>
<li><strong>Motor neuropathy:</strong> muscle imbalance causing foot deformities (claw toes, prominent metatarsal heads, Charcot foot)</li>
<li><strong>Autonomic neuropathy:</strong> loss of sweating -&gt; dry, cracked skin (entry point for infection); arteriovenous shunting</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Peripheral artery disease</strong> (~40% of diabetic foot ulcers):
<ul>
<li>Atherosclerosis of femoral/popliteal/tibial arteries</li>
<li>Medial arterial calcification (Mönckeberg sclerosis) makes ABI unreliable</li>
<li>Diminished blood flow impairs wound healing and increases amputation risk</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Infection:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Often polymicrobial (gram-positive + gram-negative + anaerobes)</li>
<li>Range from superficial cellulitis to deep abscess, osteomyelitis, and necrotizing fasciitis</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>IWGDF (International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot) risk classification (2019):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Category 0:</strong> no LOPS, no PAD, no deformity</li>
<li><strong>Category 1:</strong> LOPS or PAD (no deformity)</li>
<li><strong>Category 2:</strong> LOPS + deformity or PAD + deformity</li>
<li><strong>Category 3:</strong> previous ulcer or amputation</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p><strong>Symptoms (often minimal or absent due to neuropathy):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Foot ulcer (may be detected incidentally; patient may not feel pain)</li>
<li>Skin changes: dry, cracked, calluses, discoloration</li>
<li>Deformity (Charcot foot, claw toes)</li>
<li>Signs of infection: erythema, warmth, swelling, purulent discharge, foul odor</li>
<li>Ischemia: cold foot, absent pulses, hairless shiny skin, dependent rubor</li>
<li>Gangrene: black, necrotic tissue</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Probe-to-bone test:</strong> sterile blunt probe through ulcer to palpate bone; sensitivity ~87%, specificity ~83% for osteomyelitis</p>
<p><strong>Wagner classification:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Grade 0: pre- or post-ulcerative lesion</li>
<li>Grade 1: superficial ulcer</li>
<li>Grade 2: deep ulcer to tendon/bone</li>
<li>Grade 3: deep ulcer with abscess/osteomyelitis</li>
<li>Grade 4: localized gangrene (forefoot)</li>
<li>Grade 5: extensive gangrene (entire foot)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Multidisciplinary team approach is essential:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Offloading:</strong> total contact cast (TCC) or removable cast walker (for plantar ulcers; reduces pressure to allow healing)</li>
<li><strong>Debridement:</strong> surgical removal of necrotic tissue, callus, and infected bone; essential for wound healing</li>
<li><strong>Infection management:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Mild (cellulitis only): oral antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate, cephalexin, clindamycin) x 1-2 weeks</li>
<li>Moderate-severe: IV antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam, vancomycin + ceftriaxone + metronidazole) x 2-4 weeks</li>
<li><strong>Deep tissue culture</strong> (not superficial swab) guides therapy</li>
<li><strong>Osteomyelitis:</strong> 6+ weeks of antibiotics; consider surgical resection</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Revascularization</strong> (if PAD present): percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) or surgical bypass; <strong>urgently</strong> if critical limb ischemia (rest pain, gangrene, non-healing ulcer)</li>
<li><strong>Wound care:</strong> moist wound healing; hydrogels, alginates, silver dressings (infected); <strong>negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT)</strong> for post-debridement surgical wounds</li>
<li><strong>Adjunctive:</strong> hyperbaric oxygen therapy (selected cases); growth factors (becaplermin; limited evidence)</li>
<li><strong>Glycemic control:</strong> HbA1c target &lt;7-8% (individualized)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Amputation:</strong> reserved for uncontrolled infection/sepsis, extensive gangrene, or intractable pain; <strong>minor</strong> (toe/ray/transmetatarsal) or <strong>major</strong> (below-knee/above-knee)</p>
