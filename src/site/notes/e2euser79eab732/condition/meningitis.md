<h1>Meningitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges -- the dura mater, arachnoid, and pia mater that surround the brain and spinal cord. It is most commonly caused by infection, with <strong>bacterial meningitis</strong> representing a life-threatening medical emergency requiring immediate empiric antibiotic therapy. The classic clinical triad is <strong>fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status</strong>, though this triad is present in only ~44% of bacterial meningitis cases.</p>
<p><strong>Etiology by age and setting:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adults (community-acquired):</strong> <em>Streptococcus pneumoniae</em> (most common), <em>Neisseria meningitidis</em>, <em>Listeria monocytogenes</em> (age &gt;50, immunocompromised, pregnant)</li>
<li><strong>Children:</strong> <em>S. pneumoniae</em>, <em>N. meningitidis</em>, <em>Haemophilus influenzae</em> type b (in unvaccinated)</li>
<li><strong>Neonates:</strong> Group B Streptococcus, <em>E. coli</em>, <em>Listeria</em></li>
<li><strong>Viral (aseptic):</strong> Enteroviruses (most common), HSV-2, varicella, mumps</li>
<li><strong>Immunocompromised:</strong> <em>Cryptococcus neoformans</em>, <em>Toxoplasma</em>, CMV, TB</li>
<li><strong>Post-neurosurgical:</strong> <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em>, coagulase-negative staph, gram-negative bacilli</li>
</ul>
<p>Vaccination has dramatically reduced the incidence of bacterial meningitis in developed countries. The introduction of conjugate vaccines against <em>H. influenzae</em> type b, <em>S. pneumoniae</em>, and <em>N. meningitidis</em> has decreased bacterial meningitis incidence by &gt;90% in vaccinated populations.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p><strong>Classic triad</strong> (present in ~44% of bacterial meningitis):</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Fever</strong> (77-95%)</li>
<li><strong>Neck stiffness</strong> (nuchal rigidity; 70-87%)</li>
<li><strong>Altered mental status</strong> (69-82%)</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Other symptoms:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Severe headache</li>
<li>Photophobia</li>
<li>Nausea, vomiting</li>
<li><strong>Petechial or purpuric rash</strong> (meningococcal; pathognomonic when present)</li>
<li>Seizures (15-23%)</li>
<li>Focal neurological deficits</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Kernig sign</strong> (inability to fully extend knee with hip flexed) and <strong>Brudzinski sign</strong> (involuntary hip flexion with neck flexion) have low sensitivity (&lt;50%) but high specificity.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p><strong>Lumbar puncture (LP) is diagnostic</strong> -- but do NOT delay antibiotics if LP is delayed.</p>

<p><strong>Blood:</strong> blood cultures (positive in 50-75% of bacterial meningitis), lactate, CRP, CBC</p>
<p><strong>Imaging:</strong> CT head before LP if signs of increased ICP, focal deficits, immunocompromise, or seizures</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Empiric antibiotics (start within 1 hour of presentation):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adults (community-acquired):</strong> Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h + Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg q8-12h + Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h (if &gt;50, pregnant, or immunocompromised for Listeria)</li>
<li><strong>Add dexamethasone</strong> 10 mg IV q6h x 4 days (started before or with first antibiotic dose; reduces hearing loss and mortality in pneumococcal meningitis)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>De-escalate</strong> based on culture and sensitivity results; typical duration 7-21 days depending on organism.</p>
<p><strong>Post-exposure prophylaxis</strong> for close contacts of meningococcal or Hib meningitis:</p>
<ul>
<li>Rifampin 600 mg PO q12h x 4 doses, or</li>
<li>Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO single dose, or</li>
<li>Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM single dose</li>
</ul>
