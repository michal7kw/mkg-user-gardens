<h1>Resistant Starch</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Resistant starch (RS) is the portion of starch and starch-degradation products that resists digestion by amylases in the human small intestine and instead reaches the large intestine, where it is fermented by the gut microbiota. It is conventionally divided into four types: RS1 (physically inaccessible, e.g. whole grains and legumes), RS2 (native granular starch, e.g. raw potato, green banana), RS3 (retrograded starch formed when cooked starchy foods are cooled, e.g. cooked-and-cooled potatoes, rice, and pasta), and RS4 (chemically modified). Functionally it behaves like a fermentable dietary fiber.</p>
<h2>Nutrient Profile</h2>
<p>Resistant starch yields little digestible energy (~2 kcal/g) and produces a blunted postprandial glucose and insulin response relative to rapidly digestible starch. Its principal nutritional role is as a fermentation substrate rather than a macronutrient source; cooling cooked starchy foods increases their RS3 content.</p>
<h2>Health Effects</h2>
<p>RS is among the most butyrogenic fermentable substrates: colonic fermentation produces short-chain fatty acids with a comparatively high butyrate yield, supporting colonocyte energy metabolism, mucosal barrier function, and luminal acidification (Topping &amp; Clifton 2001). Human trials report improved insulin sensitivity and favorable microbiota shifts toward butyrate-producing taxa with regular intake, though responses are individual-dependent.</p>
