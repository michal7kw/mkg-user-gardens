<h1>Chromatin</h1>
<h2>Description</h2>
<p>Chromatin is the physical form in which the genome exists inside the eukaryotic nucleus: a dynamic complex of DNA wound around histone proteins. The fundamental repeating unit is the nucleosome, in which roughly 147 base pairs of DNA are wrapped around an octamer of core histones (two each of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4), with linker histone H1 organizing higher-order folding. This packaging compresses about two meters of DNA per cell into a nucleus a few microns across while keeping the genome accessible to the machinery that reads, copies, and repairs it.</p>
<h2>Function</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Genome compaction</strong>: Hierarchical folding from nucleosomes to chromatin fibers to chromosomes</li>
<li><strong>Transcriptional regulation</strong>: The balance between open, transcriptionally permissive euchromatin and condensed, silenced heterochromatin determines which genes are expressed</li>
<li><strong>Epigenetic memory</strong>: Covalent modifications — DNA methylation and histone acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination — encode heritable patterns of gene activity without changing the DNA sequence</li>
<li><strong>DNA replication and repair</strong>: Chromatin must be transiently disassembled and reassembled to permit replication and access for repair of damage</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Relevance</h2>
<p>Dysregulation of chromatin and its modifying enzymes is central to cancer, where aberrant DNA methylation and histone modification silence tumor-suppressor genes and activate oncogenes. Mutations in chromatin remodelers cause developmental disorders, and epigenetic drugs (DNA methyltransferase inhibitors, histone deacetylase inhibitors) are used in certain hematologic malignancies. Age-associated changes in chromatin organization are a proposed hallmark of cellular aging.</p>
