<h1>Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a chronic, progressive <strong>cholestatic liver disease</strong> characterized by <strong>inflammation, fibrosis, and multifocal stricturing of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts</strong>, ultimately producing <strong>biliary cirrhosis and end-stage liver disease</strong>. The disease affects approximately <strong>1 in 10,000</strong> people, with a <strong>2:1 male predominance</strong> and typical onset between ages 30 and 50. Its hallmark association is with <strong>inflammatory bowel disease</strong> -- <strong>~75% of PSC patients have IBD</strong> (predominantly ulcerative colitis), and conversely <strong>~2.5-7.5% of IBD patients develop PSC</strong>. The pathogenesis is unknown but thought to involve <strong>autoimmune mechanisms</strong> directed at biliary epithelium, possibly triggered by gut-derived antigens in the setting of genetic susceptibility (HLA-B<em>08, HLA-DRB1</em>03).</p>
<p>The natural history is one of <strong>slow, unpredictable progression</strong>. Median survival from diagnosis to death or liver transplantation is <strong>10-12 years untreated</strong>, though the course varies widely -- some patients remain stable for decades while others progress rapidly. Most patients are initially asymptomatic, detected when <strong>alkaline phosphatase is found elevated</strong> (typically 3-10x upper limit of normal) on routine labs or during IBD evaluation. Symptomatic presentation includes <strong>fatigue, pruritus, jaundice, and right upper quadrant pain</strong>; <strong>bacterial cholangitis</strong> (fever, chills, jaundice) may complicate dominant strictures.</p>
<p>The most feared complications are <strong>cholangiocarcinoma</strong> (bile duct cancer; lifetime risk <strong>10-15%</strong>, often diagnosed late and with poor prognosis) and <strong>colorectal cancer</strong> in PSC-IBD patients (risk <strong>5-10x</strong> the general population, requiring annual colonoscopy surveillance). Diagnosis is established by <strong>MRCP</strong> (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography), which shows the characteristic <strong>multifocal beading, strictures, and dilatation</strong> of the bile ducts. <strong>ERCP</strong> (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography) is reserved for therapeutic intervention (stricture dilation, stenting, brushing for cancer) given its higher complication rate.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>There is <strong>no proven disease-modifying medical therapy</strong> for PSC. Treatment focuses on managing complications, surveillance for malignancy, and liver transplantation for end-stage disease.</p>
<p><strong>Pharmacotherapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA; ursodiol)</strong> -- improves liver tests but <strong>does not improve survival or delay progression</strong>; low-to-moderate doses (15-23 mg/kg/day) may be used for symptom control and chemoprevention, but high doses (28-30 mg/kg/day) may be harmful</li>
<li><strong>Immunosuppression</strong> (corticosteroids, azathioprine, biologics) -- generally <strong>not effective</strong> for PSC alone; may help overlap syndromes (PSC-autoimmune hepatitis) or IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis</li>
<li>Emerging therapies under investigation: <strong>norursodeoxycholic acid</strong> (norUDCA), <strong>Cenicriviroc</strong>, <strong>NGM282</strong> (FGF19 analog), <strong>all-trans retinoic acid</strong>; none yet approved</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Symptom management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pruritus</strong> -- <strong>cholestyramine</strong> (4-8 g BID, first-line; bind bile acids in gut), <strong>rifampin</strong> (150-300 mg BID; second-line; induces CYP enzymes), <strong>naltrexone</strong> (50 mg/day; opioid antagonist), <strong>sertraline</strong> (off-label; SSRI); plasmapheresis for refractory</li>
<li><strong>Fatigue</strong> -- no effective therapy; address sleep, anemia, hypothyroidism</li>
<li><strong>Bacterial cholangitis</strong> -- broad-spectrum antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, piperacillin-tazobactam); after relief of obstruction via ERCP</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Endoscopic therapy (for dominant strictures):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ERCP with balloon dilation</strong> +/- <strong>short-term stenting</strong> for dominant strictures (common bile duct strictures &gt;1.5 mm)</li>
<li><strong>Biliary brushing</strong> and intraductal biopsy to evaluate for cholangiocarcinoma in any new or changing stricture</li>
<li>Prophylactic antibiotics before ERCP (risk of cholangitis from instrumentation)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cancer surveillance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Annual colonoscopy</strong> with biopsies for all PSC-IBD patients (colorectal cancer risk 5-10x; surveillance reduces mortality)</li>
<li><strong>Annual abdominal imaging</strong> (MRI/MRCP or ultrasound) for <strong>cholangiocarcinoma</strong> and <strong>gallbladder carcinoma</strong> surveillance; CA 19-9 trend (not diagnostic alone)</li>
<li><strong>Annual liver ultrasound +/- AFP</strong> for hepatocellular carcinoma once cirrhosis develops</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Manage complications of chronic cholestasis and cirrhosis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fat-soluble vitamin supplementation</strong> (A, D, E, K) -- monitor levels and supplement as needed</li>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis</strong> -- DEXA at diagnosis and every 2-3 years; calcium 1000-1200 mg/day, vitamin D 800-2000 IU/day, bisphosphonate if osteoporotic</li>
<li><strong>Portal hypertension</strong> -- non-selective beta-blocker or endoscopic band ligation for varices</li>
<li><strong>Ascites, encephalopathy</strong> -- standard cirrhosis management</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Liver transplantation:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Indicated for <strong>end-stage liver disease</strong> (MELD-based allocation), <strong>refractory recurrent bacterial cholangitis</strong>, or <strong>select cholangiocarcinoma</strong> (Mayo protocol: neoadjuvant chemoradiation + transplant)</li>
<li>5-year survival &gt;85%; <strong>recurrent PSC</strong> in 20-30% of recipients</li>
<li>PSC is a leading indication for liver transplant (~5-10% of transplants in the US)</li>
</ul>
