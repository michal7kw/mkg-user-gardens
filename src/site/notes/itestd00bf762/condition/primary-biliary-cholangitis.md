<h1>Primary Biliary Cholangitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC, formerly &quot;primary biliary cirrhosis&quot;) is a <strong>chronic, progressive cholestatic autoimmune liver disease</strong> characterized by the <strong>T-cell mediated destruction of small intrahepatic bile ducts</strong> (the so-called <strong>florid duct lesion</strong>), leading to cholestasis, bile accumulation, progressive fibrosis, and eventually <strong>cirrhosis and end-stage liver disease</strong>. PBC predominantly affects <strong>middle-aged women</strong> (9:1 ratio; typical onset 35-60) and is the most common cause of chronic cholestatic liver disease in this demographic. Prevalence is ~<strong>1 in 1000 women over age 40</strong>, with strong familial aggregation and associations with <strong>HLA-DRB1*08</strong> and <strong>IL12A/IL12RB2</strong> variants.</p>
<p>The diagnostic hallmark is the <strong>anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA)</strong>, positive in ~<strong>95%</strong> of cases (specificity ~95%; targets the E2 subunit of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, PDC-E2). The AMA positivity may precede symptoms and cholestasis by years, making incidental detection common. Other autoantibodies (<strong>anti-gp210, anti-sp100</strong>) are useful in AMA-negative cases and carry prognostic significance (worse outcomes). The classic biochemical signature is <strong>elevated alkaline phosphatase</strong> (typically 2-10x ULN), with <strong>mildly elevated bilirubin</strong> initially that rises as cirrhosis develops, and <strong>elevated cholesterol</strong> (often marked, but not clearly atherogenic in PBC). Diagnosis requires <strong>cholestatic enzymes, AMA positivity, and exclusion of extrahepatic obstruction</strong>; biopsy is reserved for atypical cases.</p>
<p>The clinical course is <strong>slowly progressive over decades</strong>, but variable. Most patients are initially <strong>asymptomatic</strong> (diagnosed on routine liver tests); symptomatic presentation includes <strong>fatigue</strong> (most common, ~80%, often disabling and without correlation with disease severity), <strong>pruritus</strong> (worse at night, can be severe), <strong>sicca syndrome</strong> (dry eyes and mouth; overlaps with Sjogren's), and <strong>right upper quadrant discomfort</strong>. <strong>Late-stage</strong> disease features <strong>jaundice, xanthomas</strong> (from hyperlipidemia), <strong>portal hypertension</strong>, and the complications of cirrhosis. The major causes of death are <strong>liver failure</strong> and <strong>hepatocellular carcinoma</strong> (HCC risk ~5-7% once cirrhosis develops).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>First-line disease-modifying therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA; ursodiol)</strong> 13-15 mg/kg/day in divided doses -- <strong>first-line for all PBC patients</strong>; the only therapy proven to <strong>improve transplant-free survival</strong>. ~<strong>60-70% of patients achieve biochemical response</strong> (ALP &lt;1.67x ULN and normal bilirubin at 1 year; Toronto or Paris-II criteria). Start at diagnosis regardless of symptoms. Safe in pregnancy</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring</strong> -- ALP, bilirubin, AST, albumin, platelet count every 3-6 months; <strong>GLOBE score</strong> or <strong>UK-PBC score</strong> to stratify risk and guide escalation</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Second-line for inadequate responders to UDCA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Obeticholic acid (OCA)</strong> 5 mg daily (titrate to 10 mg if tolerated after 6 months) -- <strong>farnesoid X receptor agonist</strong>; FDA-approved 2016 for PBC as monotherapy or add-on to UDCA. Reduces ALP and bilirubin; <strong>worsens pruritus</strong> (common; dose-related) and may worsen liver function in advanced cirrhosis (Child-Pugh B/C; boxed warning for decompensation in 2024). Start at 5 mg in Child-Pugh A; avoid in B/C</li>
<li><strong>Fibrates</strong> -- <strong>fenofibrate 160 mg/day</strong> or <strong>bezafibrate 400 mg/day</strong> as adjunct to UDCA; improves liver tests and pruritus; modest evidence but widely used. Monitor for rhabdomyolysis and renal function. <strong>Bezafibrate-UDCA combination</strong> shows promise for achieving biochemical remission</li>
<li><strong>Budesonide</strong> -- modest benefit in early-stage PBC with interface hepatitis; <strong>avoid in cirrhosis</strong> (portal vein thrombosis risk)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Symptom management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pruritus</strong> -- <strong>cholestyramine</strong> 4-8 g BID (first-line; give 1 hour before or 4-6 hours after UDCA), <strong>rifampin</strong> 150-300 mg BID (second-line; hepatotoxicity monitoring), <strong>naltrexone</strong> 50 mg/day (third-line; initial withdrawal reaction), <strong>sertraline</strong> 75-100 mg/day (off-label); <strong>plasmapheresis</strong> or <strong>MARS</strong> for refractory</li>
<li><strong>Fatigue</strong> -- no proven therapy; address sleep, depression, hypothyroidism, anemia; trial of <strong>modafinil</strong> (limited evidence)</li>
<li><strong>Sicca syndrome</strong> -- preservative-free artificial tears, saliva substitutes, pilocarpine, cevimeline</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Manage complications of chronic cholestasis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fat-soluble vitamin deficiency</strong> -- monitor levels; supplement A (10,000-25,000 IU/day), D (800-2000 IU/day), E (50-100 IU/kg/day), K (5-10 mg/day)</li>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis</strong> -- <strong>DEXA at diagnosis and every 2-3 years</strong>; calcium 1000-1200 mg/day, vitamin D 800-2000 IU/day; <strong>bisphosphonate</strong> (alendronate 70 mg/week) if osteoporotic. Cholestasis accelerates bone loss</li>
<li><strong>Hyperlipidemia</strong> -- often marked; statins are safe and effective; HCC risk not clearly atherogenic in PBC</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cancer surveillance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hepatocellular carcinoma</strong> -- <strong>ultrasound +/- AFP every 6 months</strong> once cirrhosis develops (Platelet &lt;150, AST &gt;2x ULN are risk markers for progression to cirrhosis in PBC)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Liver transplantation:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Indicated for <strong>end-stage liver disease</strong> (MELD-based allocation), <strong>intractable pruritus</strong>, or <strong>hepatic osteodystrophy</strong></li>
<li><strong>5-year survival &gt;85%</strong>; PBC recurs in 10-40% of recipients but rarely affects graft survival</li>
</ul>
