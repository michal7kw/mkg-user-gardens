<h1>Electrolyte Imbalance</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Electrolyte imbalance refers to a disturbance in the concentration of essential minerals dissolved in body fluids -- principally <strong>sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), calcium (Ca2+), magnesium (Mg2+), chloride (Cl-), phosphate (PO4), and bicarbonate (HCO3-)</strong>. These ions are gatekeepers of cellular excitability: they set the resting membrane potential, drive action potentials in nerve and muscle, regulate cardiac rhythm, maintain acid-base balance, and govern osmotic gradients that control water distribution. Even small deviations from normal ranges can produce symptoms; severe disturbances can be rapidly fatal, particularly through <strong>cardiac arrhythmia</strong> or <strong>seizure</strong>.</p>
<p>The most clinically dangerous disturbances are <strong>hyperkalemia</strong> and <strong>hypokalemia</strong> (peaked or flattened T waves, risk of ventricular fibrillation), <strong>hyponatremia</strong> (confusion, seizures, cerebral edema), <strong>hypocalcemia</strong> (tetany, Chvostek/Trousseau signs, prolonged QT), and <strong>hypomagnesemia</strong> (often coexists with hypokalemia and hypocalcemia, and must be corrected first or the others will not resolve). A key clinical principle is that <strong>the rate of change matters more than the absolute value</strong>: a sodium of 120 mEq/L reached over 48 hours causes severe cerebral edema, while 120 reached over weeks may be asymptomatic. Symptomatic or severe cases require inpatient management with cardiac monitoring.</p>
<p>Diagnosis combines the clinical scenario (vomiting, diarrhea, diuretic use, kidney disease, DKA), symptoms, and <strong>serum electrolyte panel</strong> with paired urine electrolytes and calculation of the <strong>anion gap</strong> (Na - [Cl + HCO3]; normal 8-12). Treatment is cause-specific and emphasizes <strong>gradual correction</strong>: overly rapid sodium correction in chronic hyponatremia causes <strong>osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis)</strong>, while overly rapid potassium correction can cause hyperkalemia and cardiac arrest.</p>
<h2>Common Patterns and Management</h2>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Electrolyte</th>
<th>Low (Hypo-)</th>
<th>High (Hyper-)</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Sodium</strong></td>
<td>Hyponatremia: confusion, seizures; correct &lt;8-10 mEq/L per 24h</td>
<td>Hypernatremia: thirst, lethargy; replace free water slowly</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Potassium</strong></td>
<td>Hypokalemia: weakness, U waves, arrhythmia; give KCl orally or IV</td>
<td>Hyperkalemia: peaked T waves; K+ &gt;6.5 needs calcium gluconate + insulin/glucose + Kayexalate or dialysis</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Calcium</strong></td>
<td>Hypocalcemia: tetany, Chvostek sign; IV calcium gluconate for severe</td>
<td>Hypercalcemia: &quot;stones, bones, groans, moans&quot;; saline + bisphosphonate</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Magnesium</strong></td>
<td>Hypomagnesemia: tremor, seizures, refractory hypokalemia/hypocalcemia; replete first</td>
<td>Hypermagnesemia: areflexia, hypotension, respiratory depression; calcium gluconate + dialysis</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Phosphate</strong></td>
<td>Hypophosphatemia: muscle weakness, rhabdomyolysis, respiratory failure</td>
<td>Hyperphosphatemia: usually CKD; phosphate binders</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p><strong>Key management principles:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Always correct <strong>magnesium first</strong> when both Mg and K are low (hypomagnesemia causes renal K wasting)</li>
<li>In hyponatremia: distinguish hypovolemic, euvolemic (SIADH), and hypervolemic; symptomatic cases need 3% hypertonic saline</li>
<li>Recheck electrolytes every 2-4 hours during active correction</li>
<li>Discontinue or dose-adjust offending drugs (diuretics, ACE inhibitors, lithium)</li>
</ul>
