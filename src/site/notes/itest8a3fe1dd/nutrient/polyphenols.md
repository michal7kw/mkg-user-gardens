<h1>Polyphenols</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Polyphenols are a diverse family of over 8,000 plant-derived compounds characterized by multiple phenol structural units. They are classified into four major groups: flavonoids (including flavonols, flavanols, anthocyanins, flavanones, isoflavones), phenolic acids (hydroxybenzoic and hydroxycinnamic acids), stilbenes (notably resveratrol), and lignans. These compounds serve as the plant's defense against UV radiation, pathogens, and oxidative stress.</p>
<p>Polyphenols exhibit potent antioxidant activity by scavenging free radicals, chelating pro-oxidant metals, and upregulating endogenous antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase) through Nrf2 pathway activation. However, their bioavailability is generally low (&lt;10%), and much of their health benefit derives from metabolites produced by gut microbiota. Colonic bacteria metabolize polyphenols into bioactive phenolic acids and urolithins that exert systemic anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects.</p>
<p>The health benefits of polyphenols are well-supported by epidemiological evidence linking high polyphenol intake (from fruits, vegetables, tea, coffee, dark chocolate, and olive oil) to reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, neurodegenerative diseases, and certain cancers. Polyphenols modulate inflammatory pathways (NF-kB, COX-2), improve endothelial function (via increased nitric oxide bioavailability), and may improve insulin sensitivity. Their effects are synergistic within whole-food matrices, supporting food-first approaches over isolated supplements.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Scavenge reactive oxygen and nitrogen species as direct antioxidants</li>
<li>Upregulate endogenous antioxidant defenses through Nrf2 pathway activation</li>
<li>Inhibit NF-kB and COX-2 inflammatory signaling pathways</li>
<li>Improve endothelial function and nitric oxide bioavailability</li>
<li>Serve as prebiotic substrates for beneficial gut bacteria</li>
<li>Modulate gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms (histone modification, DNA methylation)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Berries (blueberries, strawberries, acai, blackberries)</li>
<li>Dark chocolate and cacao (70%+ cacao content)</li>
<li>Green tea and matcha (catechins, especially EGCG)</li>
<li>Coffee (chlorogenic acids)</li>
<li>Red wine (resveratrol, anthocyanins)</li>
<li>Extra virgin olive oil (oleuropein, hydroxytyrosol)</li>
<li>Turmeric (curcumin)</li>
<li>Red onions, apples, and citrus fruits</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Polyphenols are not classified as essential nutrients, so clinical deficiency does not occur. However, diets low in polyphenol-rich foods are associated with increased oxidative stress, higher systemic inflammation (elevated CRP, IL-6), and greater risk of chronic diseases including cardiovascular disease and neurodegeneration. Populations consuming traditional polyphenol-rich diets (Mediterranean, Okinawan) consistently show lower disease burden.</p>
<p>Excessive polyphenol intake from concentrated supplements can cause gastrointestinal upset, nausea, and hepatotoxicity (reported with high-dose green tea extracts). Tannins and certain polyphenols inhibit non-heme iron absorption, which may exacerbate iron deficiency in vulnerable populations. Very high doses of isolated polyphenols may exhibit pro-oxidant activity, paradoxically increasing oxidative stress -- an effect not observed with polyphenols consumed in whole-food form.</p>
