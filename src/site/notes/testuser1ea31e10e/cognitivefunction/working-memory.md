<h1>Working Memory</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Working memory is the cognitive system that temporarily holds and manipulates information needed for ongoing cognitive tasks such as reasoning, comprehension, planning, and learning. Unlike passive short-term storage, working memory actively maintains and operates on information — for example, keeping a phone number in mind while dialing, or holding intermediate results during mental arithmetic. It is one of the three core executive functions, alongside inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility.</p>
<p>In Baddeley's influential multicomponent model, working memory comprises a central executive that allocates attention, two modality-specific buffers (the phonological loop for verbal material and the visuospatial sketchpad for visual/spatial material), and an episodic buffer that integrates information into coherent representations. Capacity is sharply limited — typically only a few items can be maintained at once — and this limit is closely tied to broader measures of fluid intelligence and attentional control.</p>
<h2>Neural Substrates</h2>
<p>Working memory depends primarily on the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex acting in concert with the posterior parietal cortex; sustained, recurrent neural activity in these regions is thought to maintain information across delays. Frontoparietal network connectivity, basal ganglia gating, and thalamic input all contribute to encoding, maintenance, and updating of working-memory contents.</p>
<h2>Neurotransmitter Modulation</h2>
<p>Prefrontal working memory follows an inverted-U dose-response for catecholamines. Optimal levels of dopamine (acting at D1 receptors) and norepinephrine (acting at alpha-2A adrenergic receptors) strengthen the persistent neural activity that sustains information, whereas both too little (as in ADHD or fatigue) and too much (as in acute stress) degrade performance. NMDA-receptor-mediated glutamatergic transmission supports the recurrent excitation underlying maintenance.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Working-memory deficits are a hallmark of ADHD, schizophrenia (where they correlate with functional outcome and dorsolateral prefrontal hypoactivation), and many forms of cognitive impairment and aging. Working-memory capacity is a strong predictor of academic and occupational performance, and is a frequent target of cognitive training, exercise, and pharmacological interventions, though transfer of trained gains to untrained tasks remains debated.</p>
