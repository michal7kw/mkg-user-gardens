<h1>Acute Ischemic Stroke</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is the sudden onset of a focal neurological deficit caused by occlusion of a cerebral artery, which cuts off blood flow to a region of brain tissue. Without adequate perfusion, neurons in the infarct core die within minutes, while a surrounding zone of hypoperfused but still-salvageable tissue (the ischemic penumbra) can survive for a limited time if flow is restored. AIS accounts for roughly 85% of all strokes and is among the leading global causes of death and acquired long-term disability.</p>
<p>The underlying mechanisms include large-artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism (most often from atrial fibrillation), small-vessel (lacunar) disease, and less common causes such as dissection or hypercoagulable states. Because the volume of irreversibly damaged brain expands rapidly over time (&quot;time is brain&quot;), the entire clinical pathway is built around speed: rapid recognition, emergent neuroimaging to exclude hemorrhage, and reperfusion therapy as fast as possible.</p>
<p>Clinically, AIS matters because timely treatment dramatically alters outcome. Intravenous thrombolysis and, for large-vessel occlusions, endovascular thrombectomy can restore perfusion and rescue the penumbra, substantially reducing disability. After the acute phase, aggressive secondary prevention targeting the underlying vascular risk factors is essential to prevent recurrence.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Symptoms appear abruptly and reflect the vascular territory involved. Anterior-circulation strokes typically cause contralateral face/arm/leg weakness, sensory loss, aphasia (dominant hemisphere), or neglect (non-dominant). Posterior-circulation strokes cause vertigo, diplopia, ataxia, and crossed sensorimotor findings. The FAST mnemonic (Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call emergency services) captures the most common presentations and drives public recognition.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is clinical, confirmed and refined by imaging. Non-contrast CT is performed first to exclude intracranial hemorrhage, which is an absolute contraindication to thrombolysis. CT or MR angiography identifies a large-vessel occlusion that may be amenable to thrombectomy, and diffusion-weighted MRI is the most sensitive test for early infarction. Perfusion imaging can estimate the salvageable penumbra and extend treatment windows in selected patients.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant modifiable risk factor is hypertension; others include atrial fibrillation, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, carotid stenosis, and physical inactivity. A prior transient ischemic attack or stroke markedly raises recurrence risk. Non-modifiable factors include older age, male sex, and family history.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Reperfusion is the centerpiece of acute care: intravenous thrombolysis (alteplase or tenecteplase) within the approved window and mechanical thrombectomy for proximal large-vessel occlusions. Care occurs in dedicated stroke units, which independently improve outcomes. Early antiplatelet therapy, blood pressure management, and prevention of complications follow. Secondary prevention is tailored to mechanism: antiplatelets and statins for atherothrombotic stroke, anticoagulation for cardioembolic stroke, and carotid revascularization for high-grade symptomatic stenosis.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Major complications include hemorrhagic transformation, malignant cerebral edema with herniation in large infarcts, aspiration pneumonia, venous thromboembolism, and post-stroke seizures. Long-term sequelae include hemiparesis, spasticity, communication impairment, and post-stroke depression.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Outcomes depend heavily on infarct size, location, and the speed of reperfusion. Rapid treatment of large-vessel occlusion can yield functional independence in a substantial fraction of patients who would otherwise be severely disabled, whereas delayed presentation worsens prognosis significantly.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Stroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, with ischemic stroke comprising the large majority of cases. Incidence rises steeply with age, and the global burden continues to grow with aging populations and the prevalence of hypertension and atrial fibrillation.</p>
