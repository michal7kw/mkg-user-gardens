<h1>Free T3</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Free triiodothyronine (Free T3, FT3) is the unbound, biologically active form of triiodothyronine (T3), representing approximately 0.3% of total T3 in circulation (the remaining 99.7% is bound to thyroxine-binding globulin, transthyretin, and albumin). T3 is the most metabolically potent thyroid hormone, being <strong>3-5 times more active than T4 (thyroxine)</strong> at the cellular level. While the thyroid gland secretes T4 as its principal output (~90% of thyroid hormone production), approximately 80% of circulating T3 is generated peripherally by <strong>deiodination of T4</strong> via the type 1 (D1) and type 2 (D2) iodothyronine deiodinases -- selenium-containing enzymes expressed primarily in the liver, kidneys, skeletal muscle, brain, and pituitary.</p>
<p>T3 exerts its effects by binding to <strong>nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (TR-alpha, TR-beta)</strong>, which regulate the transcription of genes controlling basal metabolic rate, thermogenesis, heart rate, myocardial contractility, protein synthesis, lipid metabolism, and neurodevelopment. The conversion of T4 to T3 is regulated by deiodinase activity, which is influenced by selenium status, caloric intake, illness, and medications.</p>
<p>Measurement of free T3 is clinically important in several scenarios: (1) <strong>confirming hyperthyroidism</strong> when TSH is suppressed (especially &quot;T3 thyrotoxicosis&quot; -- elevated FT3 with normal FT4, which can be the earliest sign of Graves disease relapse); (2) <strong>assessing T4-to-T3 conversion</strong> in patients on levothyroxine therapy who have persistent hypothyroid symptoms despite normal TSH (some practitioners add liothyronine / T3); (3) <strong>evaluating euthyroid sick syndrome (non-thyroidal illness)</strong> in critically ill patients where FT3 falls early; (4) <strong>combination T4/T3 therapy monitoring</strong>. However, FT3 is not recommended for routine thyroid screening (TSH and FT4 are preferred); FT3 is reserved for specific clinical situations.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational thyroid biomarker; ATA/ETA guideline-endorsed for specific indications).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range is assay-specific (immunoassay vs LC-MS/MS vs equilibrium dialysis)</li>
<li><strong>Significant diurnal variation</strong> (peak in early morning; nadir in late afternoon)</li>
<li><strong>FT3 falls in non-thyroidal illness</strong> (euthyroid sick syndrome); not a reliable marker in hospitalized patients</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> FT3 falls slightly in second and third trimesters</li>
<li><strong>Biotin supplementation</strong> can falsely elevate FT3 in streptavidin-biotin immunoassays (stop biotin 48 h before testing)</li>
<li><strong>Normal range varies with age</strong> -- elderly have slightly lower FT3 (physiologic)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase FT3 (hypertriiodothyroninemia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Graves disease</strong> (most common cause of overt hyperthyroidism)</li>
<li><strong>Toxic multinodular goiter</strong></li>
<li><strong>Toxic adenoma</strong> (Plummer disease)</li>
<li><strong>T3 thyrotoxicosis</strong> (elevated FT3 with normal FT4 -- can be earliest sign of Graves relapse)</li>
<li><strong>Exogenous T3 supplementation</strong> (liothyronine / cytomel; desiccated thyroid / Armour)</li>
<li><strong>Early or mild hyperthyroidism</strong></li>
<li><strong>Thyroiditis</strong> (subacute, postpartum, painless, amiodarone-induced -- transient elevation)</li>
<li><strong>Iodine-induced hyperthyroidism</strong> (Jod-Basedow)</li>
<li><strong>Struma ovarii</strong> (ovarian teratoma with thyroid tissue)</li>
<li><strong>Biotin interference</strong> (falsely elevated in immunoassays)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease FT3 (hypotriiodothyroninemia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong> (primary or central)</li>
<li><strong>Euthyroid sick syndrome / non-thyroidal illness</strong> (the most common cause of low FT3 in hospitalized patients; caloric restriction, sepsis, post-surgical, malignancy)</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction, fasting, eating disorders</strong> (reduced T4-to-T3 conversion)</li>
<li><strong>Selenium deficiency</strong> (impaired deiodinase function)</li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> amiodarone (inhibits deiodinase), propranolol (high dose), corticosteroids, propylthiouracil, lithium, contrast agents (iopanoic acid)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic kidney disease, liver cirrhosis</strong> (impaired deiodination)</li>
<li><strong>Aging</strong> (mild physiological decline)</li>
<li><strong>Poor T4-to-T3 conversion</strong> despite adequate levothyroxine dosing</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated FT3 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism (T3 thyrotoxicosis):</strong> elevated FT3 with suppressed TSH and normal FT4 -- often the earliest sign of recurrent Graves disease</li>
<li><strong>Overt hyperthyroidism:</strong> elevated FT3 and FT4 with suppressed TSH</li>
<li><strong>Thyroid storm:</strong> markedly elevated FT3/FT4 with severe clinical features (hyperpyrexia, tachycardia, altered mental status)</li>
<li><strong>Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (type 1):</strong> elevated FT3, FT4 with suppressed TSH</li>
<li><strong>Exogenous thyroid hormone</strong> (factitious hyperthyroidism; liothyronine or desiccated thyroid)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Low FT3 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Euthyroid sick syndrome (non-thyroidal illness):</strong> the most common cause in hospitalized patients; low FT3 with normal TSH; usually self-resolves when underlying illness resolves</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction, anorexia nervosa:</strong> reduced T4-to-T3 conversion (adaptive mechanism to conserve energy)</li>
<li><strong>Advanced age:</strong> mild physiologic decline</li>
<li><strong>Severe selenium deficiency:</strong> impaired deiodinase function</li>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong> (less commonly low than low FT4)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Not a screening test</strong> -- TSH is preferred for routine thyroid screening; FT3 reserved for specific indications</li>
<li><strong>Euthyroid sick syndrome confounds</strong> in hospitalized patients</li>
<li><strong>Significant diurnal variation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Biotin interference</strong> in immunoassays</li>
<li><strong>Reference range varies by lab and method</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Hyperthyroidism diagnosis (with TSH, FT4)</li>
<li>T3 thyrotoxicosis diagnosis (FT3 elevated with normal FT4)</li>
<li>Monitoring antithyroid drug therapy (methimazole, PTU)</li>
<li>Liothyronine (T3) therapy monitoring</li>
<li>Combination T4/T3 therapy monitoring (controversial)</li>
<li>Euthyroid sick syndrome evaluation (research)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>FT3 reflects thyroid hormone action; management targets the underlying thyroid state:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For elevated FT3 (hyperthyroidism):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Graves disease:</strong> methimazole (preferred over PTU except in 1st trimester pregnancy); radioactive iodine ablation; thyroidectomy</li>
<li><strong>Toxic multinodular goiter / adenoma:</strong> radioactive iodine or surgery</li>
<li><strong>Beta-blocker</strong> (propranolol) for symptomatic control (also inhibits peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion at high doses)</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> exogenous thyroid hormone</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For low FT3 (hypothyroidism or non-thyroidal illness):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism:</strong> levothyroxine (T4) replacement; dose-adjusted to normalize TSH</li>
<li><strong>Combination T4/T3 therapy:</strong> controversial; some clinicians add liothyronine (T3) 5-10 mcg daily for patients with persistent symptoms despite normal TSH on levothyroxine; ATA does not recommend routine use</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying illness</strong> (euthyroid sick syndrome resolves with recovery)</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional support</strong> for caloric restriction / anorexia</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Selenium</strong> 200 mcg daily in selenium-deficient patients -- cofactor for deiodinase enzymes; modest FT3 improvement in mild hypothyroidism (controversial)</li>
<li><strong>Zinc</strong> 15-30 mg daily -- cofactor for thyroid hormone receptor function; modest effect in hypothyroid patients</li>
<li><strong>Adequate iodine intake</strong> (150 mcg/day) -- essential substrate for thyroid hormone synthesis</li>
<li><strong>Reduce goitrogens</strong> (raw cruciferous vegetables, soy) in susceptible individuals</li>
<li><strong>Avoid excessive iodine</strong> (kelp, contrast agents) in autoimmune thyroid disease</li>
</ol>
