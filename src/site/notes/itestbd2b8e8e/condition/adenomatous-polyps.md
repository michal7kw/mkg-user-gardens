<h1>Adenomatous Polyps</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Adenomatous polyps, or colorectal adenomas, are benign but neoplastic outgrowths of the large-bowel mucosa composed of dysplastic glandular epithelium. They are extremely common — present in a substantial fraction of adults over 50 — and are clinically important because they are the dominant precursor of colorectal cancer. The transformation from normal mucosa through adenoma to invasive carcinoma, the &quot;adenoma-carcinoma sequence,&quot; is driven by a stepwise accumulation of genetic alterations (notably loss of APC, followed by KRAS activation and TP53 inactivation) and typically unfolds over many years, which provides the long window in which screening colonoscopy can interrupt the process.</p>
<p>Adenomas are categorized histologically as tubular, tubulovillous, or villous, with increasing villous content correlating with greater malignant potential. Additional features that raise risk include larger size (≥10 mm), high-grade dysplasia, and the presence of multiple adenomas. A parallel pathway involving sessile serrated lesions accounts for a meaningful minority of colorectal cancers and is now recognized as a distinct precursor with its own surveillance considerations. Because most adenomas remain asymptomatic, their identification depends almost entirely on screening rather than on symptoms.</p>
<p>The public-health significance of adenomatous polyps is enormous: detecting and removing them lowers both the incidence of and mortality from colorectal cancer. This makes high-quality colonoscopy with complete polypectomy, supported by stool-based tests such as the fecal occult blood/immunochemical test for screening, one of the most effective cancer-prevention strategies in modern medicine.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The great majority of adenomatous polyps are asymptomatic and discovered during screening or evaluation for unrelated complaints. Larger lesions may bleed, producing occult blood detectable on stool testing or, less often, visible rectal bleeding and iron-deficiency anemia. Symptomatic presentation generally signals a large or advanced lesion.</p>

<p>Polyps are classified by architecture (tubular, tubulovillous, villous), size, and dysplasia grade, all of which inform malignant risk and surveillance intervals.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Risk rises with age and is increased by a personal or family history of adenomas or colorectal cancer, hereditary syndromes such as familial adenomatous polyposis and Lynch syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, obesity and metabolic syndrome, diets high in red and processed meat and low in fiber, smoking, and heavy alcohol use.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Definitive management is complete endoscopic removal of all detected polyps, with endoscopic mucosal resection used for larger or flat lesions. Resected tissue is examined histologically; if a polyp contains invasive cancer beyond what endoscopy can clear, surgical resection follows. After polypectomy, patients enter a risk-stratified surveillance program, with colonoscopy intervals determined by the number, size, and histology of polyps found. Lifestyle modification and, in selected high-risk individuals, chemoprevention with aspirin may reduce recurrence.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The principal complication is malignant transformation to colorectal adenocarcinoma. Polyps may also bleed, and polypectomy itself carries small risks of bleeding and perforation. Patients with adenomas remain at elevated risk for recurrent and metachronous lesions, underscoring the need for surveillance.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>The prognosis of an isolated, completely removed low-risk adenoma is excellent, and removal interrupts progression to cancer. Outlook worsens with advanced adenomas (large size, villous histology, high-grade dysplasia) and in hereditary polyposis syndromes, where lifelong surveillance and sometimes prophylactic surgery are required.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Colorectal adenomas are very common in middle-aged and older adults, and their prevalence increases steadily with age. Because colorectal cancer is one of the most frequently diagnosed cancers worldwide, the population burden of its adenomatous precursors is correspondingly large, and organized screening programs target this precursor stage directly.</p>
