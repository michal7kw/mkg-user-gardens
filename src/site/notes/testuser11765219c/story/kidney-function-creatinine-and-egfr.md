<h1>Kidney Function, Creatinine and eGFR</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p><a href="/garden/testuser11765219c/labtest/creatinine/" class="internal-link">Creatinine</a> is a waste product of normal muscle metabolism that your kidneys filter out of the blood at a steady rate. Because production is fairly constant, the blood creatinine level is a convenient inverse gauge of kidney filtration: as filtration falls, creatinine rises. From it, labs compute the <strong>estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)</strong> — the single most useful number for tracking kidney function over time. It is not an &quot;optimization&quot; lever so much as a <strong>safety net</strong>: it tells you the organ that clears drugs, balances electrolytes, and regulates blood pressure is keeping up.</p>
<h2>The Story</h2>
<p>Every day your kidneys filter around 180 liters of blood, reclaiming water, salts, and nutrients while excreting waste. The glomerular filtration rate measures that filtering capacity, but it is impractical to measure directly, so it is estimated from creatinine plus age and sex using validated equations (the CKD-EPI family; PMID: 19414839). These equations have been refined over time — the 2021 update removed the long-standing race coefficient to make estimates fairer and more accurate (PMID: 34554658).</p>
<p>Interpretation hinges on context. Creatinine scales with muscle mass, so a very muscular person can have a &quot;high&quot; creatinine with perfectly healthy kidneys, while someone with low muscle can have a falsely reassuring value. Dehydration, a large protein or creatine-supplement load, and some medications transiently shift it. This is why a single value matters less than the <strong>trend</strong> and the eGFR read alongside a urine test for protein (albumin) — the earliest sign of kidney stress.</p>
<p>The bigger picture: chronic kidney disease is common, often silent until advanced, and driven largely by the same forces behind the rest of a longevity panel — high blood pressure, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome (<a href="/garden/testuser11765219c/clinicalpattern/metabolic-syndrome/" class="internal-link">Metabolic Syndrome</a>) (PMID: 32061315). High <a href="/garden/testuser11765219c/labtest/uric-acid/" class="internal-link">Uric Acid</a> and anemia (falling <a href="/garden/testuser11765219c/labtest/hemoglobin/" class="internal-link">Hemoglobin</a>) also travel with declining kidney function. So protecting eGFR is less about a kidney-specific supplement and more about controlling glucose, blood pressure, and weight — and avoiding nephrotoxic insults (NSAID overuse, dehydration, contrast without precautions).</p>
<h2>What This Means For You</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Read the trend, not one value.</strong> A stable eGFR over years is reassuring; a steady decline is the signal, even within the &quot;normal&quot; range.</li>
<li><strong>Context matters:</strong> high muscle mass or a creatine supplement can raise creatinine without any kidney problem; low muscle can hide a real decline. Pair eGFR with a urine albumin test.</li>
<li><strong>What protects it:</strong> controlling blood pressure, glucose, and weight; staying hydrated; and avoiding routine high-dose NSAIDs. There is no &quot;kidney supplement&quot; that substitutes for these.</li>
<li><strong>What strains it:</strong> poorly controlled diabetes and hypertension, chronic dehydration, high <a href="/garden/testuser11765219c/labtest/uric-acid/" class="internal-link">Uric Acid</a>, and nephrotoxic drug exposure.</li>
<li><strong>It changes dosing.</strong> A low eGFR means many drugs and supplements need dose adjustment — always flag reduced kidney function to a clinician and pharmacist.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>Creatinine is a steady muscle-waste product; blood levels inversely track kidney filtration, and eGFR is estimated from it.</li>
<li>Interpret with context — muscle mass, hydration, creatine intake, and some drugs shift creatinine independent of kidney health.</li>
<li>The trend in eGFR (plus a urine albumin test) matters more than any single value.</li>
<li>Chronic kidney disease is common and silent, driven by blood pressure, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.</li>
<li>Protect kidneys by controlling glucose/BP/weight and avoiding nephrotoxic insults; a low eGFR changes drug dosing.</li>
</ul>
