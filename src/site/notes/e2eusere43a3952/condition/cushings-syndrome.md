<h1>Cushings Syndrome</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cushing syndrome encompasses all causes of <strong>chronic cortisol excess</strong> (hypercortisolism), regardless of source. The most common cause overall is <strong>iatrogenic</strong> -- <strong>exogenous glucocorticoid therapy</strong> (prednisone, dexamethasone) prescribed for inflammatory or autoimmune disease. <strong>Endogenous</strong> Cushing syndrome is classified as <strong>ACTH-dependent</strong> (~80%) or <strong>ACTH-independent</strong> (~20%). The most common endogenous cause is <strong>Cushing disease</strong> -- an <strong>ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma</strong> (~70% of endogenous cases; typically a microadenoma). Other ACTH-dependent causes include <strong>ectopic ACTH secretion</strong> from tumors (small cell lung cancer, bronchial carcinoid, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, pheochromocytoma; ~10%). <strong>ACTH-independent</strong> causes include <strong>adrenal adenoma</strong> (~10%), <strong>adrenal carcinoma</strong> (rare; usually very high cortisol and virilization), and <strong>bilateral adrenal hyperplasia</strong> (rare).</p>
<p>The clinical presentation reflects the widespread catabolic and metabolic effects of cortisol on virtually every tissue. The <strong>classic phenotype</strong> includes <strong>central (truncal) obesity with fat redistribution</strong> (moon face, buffalo hump, supraclavicular fat pads), <strong>proximal muscle wasting</strong> (difficulty rising from a chair or climbing stairs), <strong>wide purple striae</strong> (&gt;1 cm; from skin thinning and rapid weight gain), <strong>easy bruising</strong> (capillary fragility), <strong>thin atrophic skin</strong>, and <strong>facial plethora</strong> (from vascular fragility and polycythemia). <strong>Metabolic</strong> effects include <strong>hypertension</strong> (~80%), <strong>insulin resistance and new-onset diabetes</strong> (~30-40%), <strong>dyslipidemia</strong>, and <strong>hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis</strong> (especially in ectopic ACTH, where cortisol overwhelms 11beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2, mimicking aldosterone). <strong>Bone</strong> effects include <strong>osteoporosis</strong> (~50%; vertebral compression fractures) and <strong>avascular necrosis</strong> (femoral head). <strong>Psychiatric</strong> manifestations range from irritability to severe depression, anxiety, and psychosis. <strong>Immune</strong> effects increase susceptibility to <strong>infection</strong> (a leading cause of death). Untreated, Cushing syndrome has a <strong>5-year mortality approaching 50%</strong> from cardiovascular disease, infection, and suicide.</p>
<p><strong>Diagnosis</strong> requires a <strong>two-step approach</strong>: (1) confirm hypercortisolism (any of <strong>24-hour urine free cortisol</strong>, <strong>late-night salivary cortisol</strong>, or <strong>1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test</strong>), and (2) determine the cause (plasma ACTH; if low -&gt; ACTH-independent -&gt; adrenal imaging; if high -&gt; ACTH-dependent -&gt; pituitary MRI, and inferior petrosal sinus sampling if needed to distinguish pituitary from ectopic). <strong>Pseudo-Cushing</strong> (alcohol excess, depression, severe obesity) can mimic true Cushing and must be excluded.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Identify and treat the underlying cause (the only curative approach):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Iatrogenic (exogenous glucocorticoids) -- the most common cause:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Taper and discontinue glucocorticoids</strong> when possible</li>
<li>Use <strong>steroid-sparing agents</strong> (methotrexate, biologics, calcineurin inhibitors) for the underlying disease</li>
<li>Gradual taper (over weeks to months) to allow HPA axis recovery; abrupt withdrawal causes adrenal crisis</li>
<li>For inhaled steroids, use lowest effective dose; rinse mouth to reduce systemic absorption</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cushing disease (pituitary adenoma):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Transsphenoidal surgery</strong> -- first-line; remission in <strong>65-90% of microadenomas</strong>, 40-65% of macroadenomas. Selective adenomectomy by experienced neurosurgeon</li>
<li><strong>Reoperation</strong> for residual or recurrent tumor</li>
<li><strong>Stereotactic radiosurgery</strong> (gamma knife) -- for residual tumor, recurrence, or surgical contraindication; takes 1-3 years to work</li>
<li><strong>Bilateral adrenalectomy</strong> -- last resort; cures hypercortisolism immediately but creates Addison's disease and risks <strong>Nelson syndrome</strong> (rapid pituitary tumor growth after loss of cortisol feedback)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adrenal causes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adrenal adenoma</strong> -- <strong>unilateral adrenalectomy</strong> (laparoscopic); curative</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal carcinoma</strong> -- <strong>open surgical resection</strong> (more aggressive); <strong>mitotane</strong> adjuvant; poor prognosis</li>
<li><strong>Bilateral macronodular adrenal hyperplasia</strong> -- <strong>bilateral adrenalectomy</strong> or unilateral with medical therapy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Ectopic ACTH:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Resect the tumor</strong> when possible (curative)</li>
<li>For unresectable tumors: <strong>medical therapy</strong> (below) or <strong>bilateral adrenalectomy</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Medical therapy (for patients awaiting surgery, surgical failures, or occult ectopic ACTH):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Steroidogenesis inhibitors (reduce cortisol production):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Osilodrostat</strong> (11beta-hydroxylase inhibitor; 2-30 mg BID) -- FDA-approved 2020 for Cushing disease; potent; monitor cortisol (risk of adrenal insufficiency) and QT</li>
<li><strong>Ketoconazole</strong> (200-600 mg TID; off-label) -- inhibits multiple steroidogenic enzymes; hepatotoxicity monitoring; alternative to surgery</li>
<li><strong>Metyrapone</strong> (250-750 mg QID; off-label) -- 11beta-hydroxylase inhibitor; available in Europe; hyperandrogenism in women</li>
<li><strong>Mitotane</strong> (adrenolytic; for adrenal carcinoma) -- destroys adrenal tissue; slow onset; neurological and GI toxicity</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pituitary-directed therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pasireotide</strong> (somatostatin analog; 600-900 mcg SC BID or monthly IM) -- reduces ACTH secretion in Cushing disease; hyperglycemia common</li>
<li><strong>Cabergoline</strong> (dopamine agonist; 1-7 mg/week) -- modest efficacy; useful in subset</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Glucocorticoid receptor antagonist:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mifepristone</strong> (300-1200 mg/day) -- blocks cortisol receptor; FDA-approved for Cushing syndrome with hyperglycemia; does NOT lower cortisol levels (so no adrenal insufficiency; but hypokalemia and endometrial thickening)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Manage complications (regardless of cause):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis</strong> -- DEXA; calcium 1000-1200 mg/day; <strong>vitamin D</strong> 800-2000 IU/day; <strong>bisphosphonate</strong> (alendronate, zoledronate) or denosumab if osteoporotic; <strong>teriparatide</strong> for severe osteoporosis (PTH analog; particularly effective)</li>
<li><strong>Hypertension</strong> -- ACE inhibitor or ARB; calcium channel blocker; mineralocorticoid effects of cortisol may respond to spironolactone</li>
<li><strong>Diabetes</strong> -- metformin first-line; GLP-1 agonist or insulin if needed; improves with cortisol reduction</li>
<li><strong>Dyslipidemia</strong> -- statin</li>
<li><strong>VTE prophylaxis</strong> -- for hospitalized patients; high VTE risk</li>
<li><strong>Infections</strong> -- high index of suspicion; treat aggressively; consider <em>Pneumocystis</em> prophylaxis if high-dose steroids</li>
<li><strong>Depression and psychiatric symptoms</strong> -- SSRI/SNRI; psychiatric referral for psychosis</li>
<li><strong>Hypogonadism</strong> -- testosterone replacement in men (after cure); estrogen/progestin in women</li>
<li><strong>Muscle weakness</strong> -- physical therapy; protein intake</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Post-treatment monitoring:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>After curative surgery, the <strong>contralateral normal adrenal and HPA axis are suppressed</strong> -- patients need <strong>glucocorticoid replacement</strong> (hydrocortisone 15-25 mg/day) for <strong>6-18 months</strong> until HPA recovery; taper slowly with ACTH stimulation testing</li>
<li>Monitor for <strong>recurrence</strong> (24-hour urine cortisol, late-night salivary cortisol, dexamethasone suppression annually)</li>
<li>Lifelong surveillance for <strong>Nelson syndrome</strong> (pituitary MRI annually) after bilateral adrenalectomy</li>
</ul>
