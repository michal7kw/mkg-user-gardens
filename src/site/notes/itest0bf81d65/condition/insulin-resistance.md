<h1>Insulin Resistance</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Insulin resistance is a metabolic state in which the body's tissues — principally skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue — respond inadequately to insulin, so that a given amount of insulin produces a smaller-than-normal effect on glucose disposal and hepatic glucose output. To compensate, the pancreatic beta cells secrete more insulin, producing hyperinsulinemia that can keep blood glucose normal for years. Insulin resistance is therefore the foundational defect that precedes and drives much of cardiometabolic disease.</p>
<p>Mechanistically, insulin resistance arises largely from the accumulation of excess fat in places it does not belong: visceral adipose tissue and ectopic lipid in the liver and muscle. These lipids and their intermediates (such as diacylglycerols and ceramides) interfere with the intracellular insulin signaling cascade, while inflammatory cytokines released from expanded adipose tissue further blunt insulin action. Elevated circulating free fatty acids impair muscle glucose uptake and promote hepatic gluconeogenesis, creating a self-reinforcing loop.</p>
<p>The clinical importance of insulin resistance lies in what it leads to. It is the unifying abnormality behind prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, polycystic ovary syndrome, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and it independently raises cardiovascular risk. Because it is driven heavily by modifiable factors, insulin resistance is also one of the most reversible: weight loss, exercise, and dietary change can substantially restore insulin sensitivity.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Insulin resistance is often silent, identified through laboratory findings rather than symptoms. When physical signs appear, they reflect chronic hyperinsulinemia: acanthosis nigricans (a velvety darkening of skin folds), skin tags, and central abdominal obesity. In women it commonly manifests as features of polycystic ovary syndrome. Some individuals report post-prandial fatigue, carbohydrate cravings, or reactive hypoglycemia.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>There is no single routine clinical test; resistance is inferred from surrogates. Fasting insulin is typically elevated, and the HOMA-IR index (derived from fasting glucose and fasting insulin) provides a practical estimate. An elevated triglyceride-to-HDL ratio is a useful, inexpensive surrogate. The research gold standard is the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, which directly quantifies glucose disposal but is impractical for everyday care.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant drivers are excess visceral and ectopic fat, physical inactivity, and an energy-dense, high-glycemic diet. Chronic low-grade inflammation, elevated free fatty acids, poor sleep, chronic stress, and certain medications (notably glucocorticoids) all worsen insulin action. Genetic susceptibility, reflected in family history of type 2 diabetes, modulates individual risk.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management targets the underlying surplus of energy and fat. The most effective interventions are weight loss with reduction of visceral adiposity, and regular physical activity — both aerobic exercise and resistance training increase insulin-independent and insulin-dependent glucose uptake by muscle. Dietary strategies that lower glycemic load, such as Mediterranean and lower-carbohydrate patterns, improve sensitivity. Pharmacologically, metformin reduces hepatic glucose output and modestly improves sensitivity, and insulin-sensitizing supplements such as berberine and inositol can serve as adjuncts.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Untreated insulin resistance progresses along a continuum to prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. It is central to metabolic syndrome and atherogenic dyslipidemia, promotes non-alcoholic fatty liver disease through hepatic lipid accumulation, and independently increases the risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease. In reproductive-age women it contributes to polycystic ovary syndrome.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Because insulin resistance is highly responsive to lifestyle change, prognosis is favorable when it is recognized early and addressed with sustained weight management and activity. Failure to intervene leads to progressive beta-cell stress, eventual beta-cell failure, and the cascade of cardiometabolic complications.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Insulin resistance is extraordinarily prevalent in modern populations, tracking closely with rising obesity. A large fraction of adults in industrialized countries are estimated to have some degree of insulin resistance, frequently undiagnosed, and its prevalence increases with age, adiposity, and sedentary lifestyle.</p>
