<h1>Adenomatous Polyposis Coli</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is an <strong>autosomal dominant</strong> inherited cancer syndrome caused by germline mutations in the <strong>APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) tumor suppressor gene</strong> on chromosome 5q21-q22. The APC protein is a negative regulator of the Wnt signaling pathway (it binds beta-catenin, targeting it for degradation). Loss of APC function leads to uncontrolled beta-catenin -&gt; constitutive Wnt signaling -&gt; cellular proliferation -&gt; adenoma formation.</p>
<p><strong>Clinical features:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hundreds to thousands of adenomatous polyps</strong> develop throughout the colon and rectum, beginning in adolescence (average age of polyp onset: <strong>16 years</strong>)</li>
<li><strong>Colorectal cancer is virtually inevitable</strong> if untreated, with average age of cancer diagnosis <strong>39 years</strong> (range 34-43)</li>
<li>Account for <strong>&lt;1%</strong> of all colorectal cancers but are clinically critical because cancer is completely preventable with prophylactic colectomy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Variants:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Classic FAP:</strong> &gt;1000 polyps; early CRC onset</li>
<li><strong>Attenuated FAP (AFAP):</strong> 10-100 polyps; later CRC onset (~50s); APC mutations at 5' or 3' ends of gene</li>
<li><strong>Gardner syndrome:</strong> FAP + osteomas, epidermoid cysts, desmoid tumors, dental abnormalities</li>
<li><strong>Turcot syndrome:</strong> FAP + medulloblastoma (APC) or glioblastoma (Lynch syndrome)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Genetic testing:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>APC mutation detection in ~80-90% of classic FAP; genetic testing recommended for:
<ul>
<li>All first-degree relatives of known mutation carriers (starting age 10-12)</li>
<li>Individuals with &gt;10 adenomatous polyps cumulative lifetime</li>
<li>Individuals with CRC before age 40</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If a specific APC mutation is identified in a family, predictive testing of relatives is straightforward and highly informative</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Prophylactic colectomy (the cornerstone of FAP management):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Timing:</strong> age 15-25 (after polyps appear; before cancer develops)</li>
<li><strong>Surgical options:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Total proctocolectomy with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (IPAA):</strong> removes all colon and rectum; ileal &quot;J-pouch&quot; creates a reservoir; preferred for most patients; eliminates CRC risk; avoids permanent ileostomy</li>
<li><strong>Total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis (IRA):</strong> preserves rectum (fewer complications; better function); suitable for AFAP or few rectal polyps; requires lifelong rectal surveillance</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Timing individualized:</strong> based on polyp number, size, histology, and patient/family preference</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Upper GI surveillance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Upper endoscopy with duodenoscopy</strong> starting age 25-30; every 1-3 years depending on Spigelman stage (duodenal polyposis classification)</li>
<li><strong>Duodenal adenomas:</strong> endoscopic resection, ablative therapy, or surgical resection (pancreaticoduodenectomy) for advanced Spigelman stage</li>
<li><strong>Ampullary cancer</strong> is a leading cause of death after colectomy in FAP</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Medical therapy (adjunct, not substitute for surgery):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Celecoxib</strong> 400 mg BID (COX-2 inhibitor): reduces polyp number by ~28%; approved by FDA as adjunct; significant cardiovascular concerns</li>
<li><strong>Sulindac</strong> 150-200 mg BID: NSAID; reduces polyp number and size; may prevent duodenal adenoma progression</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Desmoid tumors:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Intra-abdominal fibromatosis; occur in 10-30% of FAP patients (especially after surgery; associated with APC mutations 3' to codon 1444)</li>
<li><strong>Not malignant</strong> but locally invasive; can cause bowel obstruction, ureteral compression</li>
<li>Treatment: sulindac + estrogen-blocker (tamoxifen or raloxifene); chemotherapy (doxorubicin + dacarbazine) for aggressive disease; surgery often not curative</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Other surveillance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Annual thyroid exam and ultrasound (papillary thyroid cancer risk)</li>
<li>Annual abdominal exam (desmoid tumors)</li>
<li>CHRPE (congenital hypertrophy of retinal pigment epithelium): a clinical marker present in ~70% of FAP families; detected on routine ophthalmoscopy</li>
</ul>
