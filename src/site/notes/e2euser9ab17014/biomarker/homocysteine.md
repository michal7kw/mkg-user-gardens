<h1>Homocysteine</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Homocysteine (Hcy) is a non-proteinogenic, sulfur-containing amino acid generated as an obligate intermediate of methionine metabolism within the one-carbon (methylation) cycle. When the universal methyl donor S-adenosylmethionine donates a methyl group, it is converted to S-adenosylhomocysteine, which is then hydrolyzed to homocysteine. Homocysteine sits at a metabolic crossroads with three fates: it can be re-methylated back to methionine (using folate-derived 5-methyltetrahydrofolate and vitamin B12 as a cofactor for methionine synthase, or via betaine/choline), it can be irreversibly catabolized through the transsulfuration pathway to cystathionine and ultimately cysteine and glutathione (a vitamin B6-dependent step), or it can be exported into the circulation.</p>
<p>Because homocysteine disposal depends on adequate folate, vitamin B12, and vitamin B6, plasma homocysteine functions as a sensitive functional marker of B-vitamin status and overall methylation capacity. Levels rise when these vitamins are deficient, when renal clearance is impaired, in genetic variants of one-carbon enzymes (notably the MTHFR C677T polymorphism, which reduces 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase activity), and with advancing age, hypothyroidism, smoking, and certain drugs. The amino acid itself is thought to promote vascular and neuronal injury through endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, pro-thrombotic effects on platelets and coagulation, and excitotoxicity.</p>
<p>Clinically, fasting total plasma homocysteine is ordered to evaluate unexplained or premature atherothrombotic disease, recurrent venous thromboembolism, suspected B-vitamin deficiency, and as one input in cognitive and dementia risk assessment. While homocysteine is a robust and reproducible <em>risk marker</em>, large trials show that lowering it pharmacologically with B vitamins reliably reduces the level but yields more modest and inconsistent reductions in hard clinical events, so it is interpreted as a marker and a modifiable target rather than a guaranteed causal lever in every patient.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Values are for fasting total plasma homocysteine; non-fasting and methionine-load testing exist but are less standardized.</li>
<li>Reference intervals vary modestly by assay, age, and sex (levels are generally higher in men and rise with age).</li>
<li>Levels increase in chronic kidney disease due to reduced clearance, independent of B-vitamin status.</li>
<li>Improper specimen handling (delayed separation of plasma from cells) falsely elevates results because red cells continue to release homocysteine.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Homocysteine:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Folate, vitamin B12, or vitamin B6 deficiency (insufficient cofactors for re-methylation and transsulfuration)</li>
<li>MTHFR C677T (and related) polymorphisms (reduced enzyme activity)</li>
<li>Chronic kidney disease (reduced clearance)</li>
<li>Advancing age and male sex</li>
<li>Hypothyroidism</li>
<li>Smoking and excessive coffee or alcohol intake</li>
<li>Certain medications (methotrexate, fibrates, metformin, some antiepileptics, nitrous oxide)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Homocysteine:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Adequate intake or supplementation with folate, vitamin B12, and vitamin B6</li>
<li>Betaine (trimethylglycine) supplementation (provides an alternative re-methylation pathway)</li>
<li>Diets rich in leafy greens, legumes, and folate-fortified foods</li>
<li>Reduced consumption of homocysteine-raising substances (smoking cessation)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular Disease</strong> -- hyperhomocysteinemia is an independent, dose-related risk marker for coronary and atherothrombotic disease via endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and pro-thrombotic effects (PMID: 41149254).</li>
<li><strong>Stroke</strong> -- elevated homocysteine is associated with increased ischemic stroke risk, and homocysteine-lowering shows its most consistent (though modest) benefit for stroke prevention.</li>
<li><strong>Venous Thromboembolism</strong> -- hyperhomocysteinemia is linked to increased venous and cerebral venous thrombotic risk (PMID: 42078605).</li>
<li><strong>Cognitive decline and dementia</strong> -- raised homocysteine is associated with vascular dementia and Alzheimer-type cognitive impairment, plausibly through cerebrovascular injury and neurotoxicity (PMID: 36359260).</li>
<li><strong>Mild Cognitive Impairment</strong> -- elevated levels are over-represented in MCI and may predict progression.</li>
<li><strong>Chronic Kidney Disease</strong> -- both a consequence (reduced clearance) and a potential contributor to cardiovascular risk in CKD.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Adequate B-vitamin status and good methylation capacity (a generally favorable finding).</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Confirming and tracking response to B-vitamin repletion (recheck after 6-12 weeks).</li>
<li>Evaluating unexplained premature atherosclerosis or recurrent thrombosis.</li>
<li>Adjunct in cognitive and dementia risk stratification, particularly in older adults with low folate/B12.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Folate (folic acid or 5-MTHF)</strong> -- 400-800 mcg/day is the single most effective intervention for lowering homocysteine; 5-methyltetrahydrofolate is preferred in individuals with MTHFR variants (PMID: 41194300).</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)</strong> -- 250-1000 mcg/day, especially in older adults, vegetarians/vegans, and those on metformin or acid-suppressing drugs; B12 is required for methionine synthase.</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)</strong> -- supports the transsulfuration arm; commonly combined with folate and B12 in B-complex regimens.</li>
<li><strong>B-complex combination therapy</strong> -- combined folate + B12 + B6 produces the largest and most reliable homocysteine reductions and is the standard approach in hyperhomocysteinemia (PMID: 41615824).</li>
<li><strong>Betaine (trimethylglycine)</strong> -- considered when homocysteine remains elevated despite B-vitamin repletion or in CBS-related homocystinuria; provides an alternative re-methylation route.</li>
<li><strong>Dietary pattern</strong> -- emphasize folate-rich foods (leafy greens, legumes, citrus) and a Mediterranean-style diet; address smoking and excessive alcohol/coffee.</li>
<li><strong>Treat secondary causes</strong> -- correct hypothyroidism and optimize renal function where possible.</li>
</ol>
