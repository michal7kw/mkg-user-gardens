<h1>Malnutrition and Malabsorption</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Malnutrition encompasses <strong>both undernutrition</strong> (inadequate caloric or nutrient intake) and <strong>malabsorption</strong> (inability to absorb ingested nutrients). Together they represent a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide -- in <strong>hospitals and intensive care units</strong>, malnutrition affects <strong>20-50% of patients</strong> and is an independent predictor of <strong>prolonged hospital stay, infection, poor wound healing, and mortality</strong>. In developing countries, <strong>protein-energy malnutrition</strong> (marasmus, kwashiorkor) remains a leading cause of child death. In developed countries, <strong>disease-related malnutrition</strong> (cancer cachexia, sarcopenia of aging, post-surgical catabolism, ICU-acquired weakness) dominates.</p>
<p>The body's response to starvation is <strong>phased</strong>: glycogen stores deplete within 24 hours, then <strong>gluconeogenesis</strong> from amino acids and glycerol sustains glucose for ~1-2 weeks, then <strong>ketogenesis</strong> from fat shifts metabolism to ketone bodies, sparing muscle protein. <strong>Protein-energy malnutrition</strong> preferentially spares vital organs but catabolizes skeletal muscle (sarcopenia), visceral protein (low albumin, transferrin), and immune cells (lymphopenia, impaired cellular immunity). The <strong>immunodeficiency of malnutrition</strong> increases susceptibility to infection -- the leading cause of death in severe malnutrition.</p>
<p><strong>Malabsorption</strong> results from <strong>maldigestion</strong> (pancreatic enzyme or bile deficiency) or <strong>mucosal disease</strong> (celiac, Crohn, short bowel, infections). The hallmark is <strong>steatorrhea</strong> -- greasy, foul-smelling stools that float -- from fat malabsorption, accompanied by deficiencies of <strong>fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)</strong> and <strong>vitamin B12</strong> (loss of ileal absorption or pancreatic protease). Specific clues suggest the cause: <strong>iron deficiency anemia</strong> in celiac disease, <strong>osteomalacia</strong> in vitamin D malabsorption, <strong>easy bruising</strong> in vitamin K deficiency, <strong>night blindness</strong> in vitamin A deficiency. <strong>Diagnosis</strong> integrates anthropometry (BMI, mid-arm circumference, grip strength), <strong>nutritional screening tools</strong> (MUST, NRS-2002), biochemical markers (albumin, prealbumin -- though these are also inflammation markers), and <strong>malabsorption workup</strong> (celiac serology, fecal elastase, fecal fat, hydrogen breath tests, endoscopy with biopsies).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Identify and treat the underlying cause:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Celiac disease</strong> -- strict gluten-free diet</li>
<li><strong>Inflammatory bowel disease</strong> -- anti-inflammatory therapy (5-ASA, steroids, biologics)</li>
<li><strong>Pancreatic insufficiency</strong> -- pancreatic enzyme replacement (PERT; pancrelipase with meals)</li>
<li><strong>Short bowel syndrome</strong> -- specialized enteral nutrition, octreotide for high output, GLP-2 (teduglutide) to promote adaptation</li>
<li><strong>SIBO</strong> -- rifaximin or metronidazole</li>
<li><strong>Eating disorder</strong> -- psychiatric treatment (CBT-E, family-based therapy for anorexia nervosa)</li>
<li><strong>Cancer cachexia</strong> -- nutritional counseling, anti-cancer therapy; consider appetite stimulants (megestrol) and anabolic agents</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Nutritional support (escalate as needed):</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Oral nutrition</strong> -- fortified diet, oral nutritional supplements (ONS; e.g., Ensure, Fortisip, Boost); 30-35 kcal/kg/day and 1.0-1.5 g/kg/day protein for most adults</li>
<li><strong>Enteral nutrition</strong> (tube feeding) -- if oral intake inadequate despite counseling; nasogastric for &lt;2-4 weeks, PEG/PEJ for longer</li>
<li><strong>Parenteral nutrition</strong> (PN) -- if GI tract non-functional (ileus, short bowel, severe mucositis); central venous access; higher risk of infection and metabolic complications</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Refeeding syndrome (CRITICAL -- prevent this):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Risk when feeding severely malnourished patients (BMI &lt;16, minimal intake &gt;10 days, rapid weight loss)</li>
<li><strong>Pathophysiology</strong> -- carbohydrate feeding triggers insulin surge -&gt; intracellular shift of phosphate, potassium, magnesium -&gt; arrhythmia, heart failure, death</li>
<li><strong>Prevention</strong> -- thiamine 200-300 mg before feeding; start at <strong>10-20 kcal/kg/day</strong> and escalate over 4-7 days; monitor and replace phosphate, potassium, magnesium aggressively</li>
<li><strong>High-risk patients</strong> (BMI &lt;14, minimal intake &gt;15 days) -- start at 5-10 kcal/kg/day; ICU monitoring</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Micronutrient repletion:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron</strong> -- oral ferrous sulfate 325 mg daily; IV iron if intolerant or rapid repletion needed (iron dextran, ferric carboxymaltose)</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12</strong> -- 1000 mcg IM daily x1 week, then weekly x4, then monthly; or 1000-2000 mcg oral daily</li>
<li><strong>Folate</strong> -- 1-5 mg oral daily</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> -- cholecalciferol 1000-2000 IU/day maintenance; 50,000 IU weekly x8 weeks if deficient</li>
<li><strong>Zinc</strong> -- 15-30 mg elemental zinc daily</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium</strong> -- oral or IV depending on severity</li>
<li><strong>Multivitamin</strong> in all patients with malabsorption or restricted intake</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Specialized situations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sarcopenia of aging</strong> -- protein 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day, resistance exercise 2-3x/week</li>
<li><strong>ICU</strong> -- early enteral nutrition (within 24-48 hours); permissive underfeeding in first week; tight glycemic control</li>
<li><strong>Post-bariatric surgery</strong> -- lifelong multivitamin, calcium, vitamin D, iron, B12; monitor annually</li>
<li><strong>Cancer cachexia</strong> -- nutritional counseling; megestrol acetate (appetite), corticosteroids (short-term), omega-3 fatty acids; emerging: ghrelin agonists, anti-inflammatory agents</li>
<li><strong>Anorexia nervosa</strong> -- specialist eating disorder team; refeeding precautions; psychiatric comanagement</li>
</ul>
