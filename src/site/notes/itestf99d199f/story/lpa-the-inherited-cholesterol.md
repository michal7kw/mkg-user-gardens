<h1>Lp(a) — The Inherited Cholesterol</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p><a href="/garden/itestf99d199f/biomarker/lpa/" class="internal-link">Lp(a)</a> (lipoprotein little-a) is an LDL-like particle with an extra protein, apolipoprotein(a), wrapped around it. Its concentration is set almost entirely by the genes you inherit — it barely moves with diet, exercise, or statins — and it is a strong, independent cause of cardiovascular disease and aortic valve stenosis. Because the level is stable for life, Lp(a) is the rare biomarker you generally <strong>test once</strong>, and a high result reshapes how aggressively you manage every other modifiable risk factor.</p>
<h2>The Story</h2>
<p>Structurally, Lp(a) is an <a href="/garden/itestf99d199f/labtest/ldl-cholesterol/" class="internal-link">LDL Cholesterol</a> particle (with its <a href="/garden/itestf99d199f/labtest/apolipoprotein-b/" class="internal-link">Apolipoprotein B</a> shell) plus apolipoprotein(a), a protein that resembles plasminogen and may promote clotting and inflammation within the artery wall. This dual &quot;atherogenic + thrombogenic&quot; character is why elevated Lp(a) adds risk on top of LDL. Mendelian studies — using inherited genetic variants as a natural experiment — show that genetically higher Lp(a) causes more myocardial infarction, which is strong evidence for causality rather than mere association (PMID: 19509380). European and American expert panels now recommend measuring Lp(a) at least once in adults to refine risk (PMID: 20965889, PMID: 28183512).</p>
<p>Roughly one in five people carry a clinically meaningful elevation, and most have no idea. The frustrating part historically is that lifestyle and statins move Lp(a) little (statins may even nudge it up slightly). That is changing — potent RNA-based therapies that lower Lp(a) dramatically are in late-stage trials — but as of today the practical response to a high Lp(a) is <strong>risk amplification</strong>: treat it as a reason to be more aggressive about the levers you <em>can</em> move (LDL/ApoB, blood pressure, smoking, glucose), not as a number to chase directly.</p>
<h2>What This Means For You</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Test once.</strong> Because it is genetically fixed, a single lifetime measurement is usually enough. There is no need to re-test it like a metabolic marker.</li>
<li><strong>A high value is an amplifier.</strong> It does not have its own quick fix; instead it lowers the threshold for aggressively managing <a href="/garden/itestf99d199f/labtest/ldl-cholesterol/" class="internal-link">LDL Cholesterol</a> / <a href="/garden/itestf99d199f/labtest/apolipoprotein-b/" class="internal-link">Apolipoprotein B</a>, blood pressure, and other risk factors — often a statin conversation with a clinician.</li>
<li><strong>Family matters.</strong> High Lp(a) is inherited, so a high result is a signal to encourage first-degree relatives to be tested, especially with a family history of early heart disease.</li>
<li><strong>Supportive nutrients are limited.</strong> <a href="/garden/itestf99d199f/supplement/niacin/" class="internal-link">Niacin</a> can lower Lp(a) modestly but has not improved outcomes and carries side effects; <a href="/garden/itestf99d199f/supplement/coenzyme-q10/" class="internal-link">CoQ10</a> and <a href="/garden/itestf99d199f/supplement/l-carnitine/" class="internal-link">L-Carnitine</a> are sometimes discussed but are not established Lp(a)-lowering levers. The real leverage is managing overall atherogenic burden.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>Lp(a) is an LDL-like particle whose level is set by genetics — it barely responds to diet, exercise, or statins.</li>
<li>It is an independent, causal risk factor for heart attack and aortic stenosis (shown by Mendelian genetic studies).</li>
<li>Test it once in a lifetime; a high value amplifies how aggressively you manage LDL/ApoB, blood pressure, and other modifiable risks.</li>
<li>High Lp(a) is inherited — a high result is a reason to have relatives tested.</li>
<li>Diet/supplements do little; potent Lp(a)-lowering drugs are in trials but not yet standard.</li>
</ul>
