<h1>Bones</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Bones are the rigid, mineralized organs that together form the skeleton, the structural framework of the body. The adult human skeleton comprises 206 bones classified by shape into long bones (e.g., femur, humerus), short bones (e.g., carpals), flat bones (e.g., skull, sternum, scapula), irregular bones (e.g., vertebrae), and sesamoid bones (e.g., patella). Beyond providing a scaffold, bones protect vital organs, anchor skeletal muscles to enable locomotion, house the hematopoietic marrow that produces blood cells, and act as the body's central reservoir of calcium and phosphate.</p>
<p>Far from being inert, bone is metabolically active connective tissue composed of an organic matrix (predominantly type I collagen, which provides tensile strength and flexibility) impregnated with an inorganic mineral phase of hydroxyapatite — a crystalline calcium phosphate that confers hardness and compressive strength. Three principal cell types govern bone biology: osteoblasts, which synthesize and mineralize new matrix; osteocytes, terminally differentiated osteoblasts embedded within the matrix that act as the primary mechanosensors directing remodeling; and osteoclasts, large multinucleated cells of hematopoietic origin that resorb bone. Macroscopically, bone is organized into dense outer cortical (compact) bone and inner trabecular (spongy) bone, the latter being more metabolically active and the first to show loss in osteoporosis.</p>
<p>Throughout life, bone undergoes continuous remodeling in which old or damaged tissue is resorbed by osteoclasts and replaced with new matrix by osteoblasts, a tightly coupled process orchestrated by the RANK/RANKL/OPG signaling axis, Wnt/sclerostin signaling, and systemic hormones. Peak bone mass is typically reached in the late twenties to early thirties, after which a gradual net loss begins, accelerating sharply in women after menopause due to estrogen withdrawal. The balance between resorption and formation is the central determinant of skeletal health and the principal target of interventions against bone loss.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Providing structural support and the mechanical framework that gives the body shape and resists gravitational and muscular loads</li>
<li>Protecting internal organs — the skull shields the brain, the rib cage protects the heart and lungs, and the vertebral column encases the spinal cord</li>
<li>Acting as levers for skeletal muscle attachment, converting muscle contraction into movement at joints</li>
<li>Producing blood cells via hematopoiesis in red bone marrow, generating erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelets</li>
<li>Storing and releasing minerals — bone holds roughly 99% of the body's calcium and about 85% of its phosphate, buffering serum levels under hormonal control</li>
<li>Functioning as an endocrine organ, secreting osteocalcin (which influences glucose metabolism and male fertility) and FGF23 (which regulates phosphate excretion)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Mineral Homeostasis and Hormonal Regulation</h2>
<p>Bone is integral to maintaining serum calcium within a narrow physiological range. When blood calcium falls, parathyroid hormone (PTH) stimulates osteoclastic bone resorption and renal calcium reabsorption while promoting renal activation of vitamin D to calcitriol, which in turn enhances intestinal calcium absorption. Calcitonin, released by thyroid C-cells when calcium is high, modestly opposes resorption. Vitamin D status is a key determinant of how efficiently dietary calcium is absorbed; deficiency leads to secondary hyperparathyroidism and accelerated bone loss. Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) is required for the carboxylation of osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein, directing calcium into bone matrix rather than soft tissue. Magnesium serves as a cofactor in vitamin D metabolism and influences PTH secretion, while phosphate, sex hormones (notably estrogen and testosterone), thyroid hormones, and glucocorticoids all materially affect remodeling balance.</p>
<h2>Associated Conditions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Osteoporosis — a systemic skeletal disorder of low bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration leading to fragility and increased fracture risk, most common in postmenopausal women and the elderly</li>
<li>Osteopenia — bone mineral density below normal but not yet meeting the diagnostic threshold for osteoporosis; an intermediate, at-risk state</li>
<li>Rickets and osteomalacia — defective mineralization of bone matrix in children and adults respectively, classically caused by severe vitamin D deficiency</li>
<li>Osteoarthritis — degenerative joint disease involving subchondral bone remodeling and osteophyte formation alongside cartilage loss</li>
<li>Rheumatoid arthritis — autoimmune inflammation that drives both local periarticular erosion and systemic bone loss via inflammatory cytokine activation of osteoclasts</li>
<li>Paget's disease of bone — focal disorganized, accelerated remodeling producing enlarged, structurally weak bone</li>
<li>Fractures — structural failure from trauma or, in fragility fractures, from low bone strength under modest load</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Skeletal health is assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which quantifies bone mineral density and underpins the diagnosis of osteopenia and osteoporosis. Biochemical bone turnover markers complement imaging: P1NP (procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide) reflects bone formation, while CTX and resorption markers reflect breakdown; osteocalcin indicates osteoblast activity, and alkaline phosphatase (bone isoform) tracks mineralization. Serum calcium, phosphate, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, and PTH are routinely measured to characterize the metabolic milieu. Foundational preventive strategies include adequate dietary calcium and vitamin D, sufficient protein intake, vitamin K2 and magnesium status, weight-bearing and resistance exercise to provide the mechanical loading that stimulates osteoblasts, avoidance of smoking and excess alcohol, and fall prevention. Pharmacological therapies for established osteoporosis include antiresorptives (bisphosphonates, denosumab) and anabolic agents (teriparatide, romosozumab).</p>
