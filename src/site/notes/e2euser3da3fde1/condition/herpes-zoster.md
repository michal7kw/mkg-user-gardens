<h1>Herpes Zoster</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Herpes zoster, commonly called shingles, is a painful skin and nerve condition caused by reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus (VZV). After a primary infection with chickenpox, the virus persists in a dormant state within sensory dorsal root and cranial nerve ganglia. When cell-mediated immunity to VZV declines — with advancing age, illness, or immunosuppression — the virus can reactivate, travel down a sensory nerve, and produce the characteristic eruption in the corresponding dermatome.</p>
<p>The hallmark of zoster is a unilateral, dermatomal vesicular rash that classically does not cross the midline, frequently preceded by a prodrome of pain, burning, or tingling in the affected area. Thoracic and trigeminal dermatomes are most often involved. Because the disease is fundamentally a viral neuritis, pain is a central feature and can be severe and disabling, both acutely and as a chronic sequela.</p>
<p>Herpes zoster matters clinically for two reasons: the acute illness can be intensely painful and, when it involves the eye (herpes zoster ophthalmicus) or facial nerve (Ramsay Hunt syndrome), can threaten vision and cause facial palsy; and a substantial fraction of patients — especially older adults — go on to develop post-herpetic neuralgia, a chronic neuropathic pain syndrome. Both prompt antiviral treatment and, increasingly, vaccination with the highly effective recombinant subunit vaccine have transformed prevention and management.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>A prodrome of localized pain, itching, or paresthesia in a single dermatome typically precedes the rash by one to several days. The eruption then appears as grouped erythematous papules that evolve into clear vesicles, pustules, and finally crusts over one to two weeks, confined to the dermatome and one side of the body. Pain is often neuropathic — burning, lancinating, with allodynia — and may be out of proportion to the visible lesions. Ophthalmic involvement is suggested by lesions on the tip of the nose (Hutchinson's sign).</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Herpes zoster is usually diagnosed clinically from the distinctive dermatomal vesicular rash. When confirmation is needed — atypical, disseminated, or immunocompromised cases — PCR detection of VZV DNA from a vesicle swab is the most sensitive test; direct fluorescent antibody testing is an alternative. The Tzanck smear is nonspecific and largely of historical interest.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The strongest risk factor is increasing age, reflecting the natural waning of VZV-specific cell-mediated immunity. Immunosuppression of any cause — HIV, hematologic and solid malignancies, transplantation, and immunosuppressive or biologic therapy — markedly increases both incidence and severity. A prior episode of chickenpox is a prerequisite, since it establishes the latent reservoir.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Antiviral therapy with acyclovir, valacyclovir, or famciclovir reduces the duration of viral replication, accelerates healing, and lessens acute pain; it is most effective when started within 72 hours of rash onset and is recommended for older and immunocompromised patients. Pain is managed with a stepwise approach that may include neuropathic agents. Ophthalmic and disseminated zoster require specialist involvement. The recombinant zoster vaccine is highly effective at preventing zoster and post-herpetic neuralgia and is recommended for eligible adults.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most common complication is post-herpetic neuralgia, chronic pain persisting after the rash resolves. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus can cause keratitis and vision loss; Ramsay Hunt syndrome produces facial paralysis with ear and oral lesions. Bacterial superinfection of skin lesions and, in immunocompromised hosts, disseminated cutaneous or visceral disease are additional risks.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>In immunocompetent patients the rash heals over two to four weeks, usually without sequelae. The principal long-term concern is post-herpetic neuralgia, whose risk rises sharply with age. Immunocompromised patients are at greater risk of severe, prolonged, or disseminated disease.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Most adults harbor latent VZV and are therefore at lifetime risk of zoster, with a lifetime incidence estimated near one in three. Incidence and complication rates climb steeply after age 50, which is why vaccination programs target older adults.</p>
