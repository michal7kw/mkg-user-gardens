<h1>Total Tau</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Total tau (t-tau) is the concentration of all tau protein isoforms in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), measured by ELISA or chemiluminescent immunoassay. Tau is an axonal microtubule-associated protein whose physiological role is to stabilize microtubules in neuronal axons. When axons are damaged -- by chronic neurodegeneration, acute trauma, ischemia, or prion disease -- tau leaks into the extracellular space and CSF, where it can be quantified as a sensitive but non-specific marker of neuronal injury burden. In the 2018 NIA-AA <strong>AT(N) framework</strong> for biologically defining Alzheimer's disease, t-tau constitutes the &quot;N&quot; (neurodegeneration, non-specific) category.</p>
<p>In Alzheimer's disease, CSF t-tau is typically elevated 2-3 fold above age-matched controls (300-600 pg/mL range), reflecting active neuronal loss and tangle-driven neurodegeneration. However, t-tau is elevated in essentially any condition involving neuronal injury, including acute stroke (transient peak within 1-2 weeks), moderate-to-severe traumatic brain injury, and especially Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (where CSF t-tau often exceeds 1200 pg/mL, making it a useful diagnostic marker for CJD vs other dementias). For AD specificity, t-tau is interpreted alongside Aβ42 (which decreases in AD) and p-tau (which increases specifically in AD) -- the classic &quot;AD CSF signature&quot; is low Aβ42, elevated t-tau, and elevated p-tau181.</p>
<p>CSF t-tau is one of the most well-validated AD biomarkers (FDA-cleared assays), but it is being progressively displaced by plasma p-tau217 (which has comparable diagnostic accuracy with a simple blood draw). CSF t-tau remains the gold standard when plasma biomarkers are equivocal or in research settings requiring high sensitivity.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (well-validated; guidelines-based; FDA-cleared assays).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range is age-dependent and assay-specific (always use lab-specific cutoffs)</li>
<li>Always interpret with Aβ42 and p-tau181 -- the AD &quot;signature&quot; pattern</li>
<li>Lumbar Puncture is required (limitation vs plasma biomarkers)</li>
<li>Polypropylene tubes mandatory (tau adsorbs to glass)</li>
<li>CSF should be aliquoted within 2 h of LP</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase t-tau:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Alzheimer's disease (moderate elevation, 2-3x normal)</li>
<li>Acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke (transient peak at 1-2 weeks)</li>
<li>Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (markedly elevated, often &gt;1200 pg/mL)</li>
<li>Moderate to severe traumatic brain injury (within 24-72 h)</li>
<li>Frontotemporal dementia, vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia (mild-moderate)</li>
<li>Any active neurodegenerative process with neuronal loss</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease t-tau:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Neuroprotective therapy response (research application)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Alzheimer's disease:</strong> when combined with low Aβ42 + high p-tau181, supports AD diagnosis with sensitivity/specificity &gt;85% (Hansson 2006)</li>
<li><strong>Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease:</strong> CSF t-tau &gt;1200 pg/mL is a diagnostic criterion (specificity ~95%; sensitivity ~80%) in the appropriate clinical context (rapidly progressive dementia, myoclonus, periodic EEG)</li>
<li><strong>Acute stroke:</strong> t-tau peaks 1-2 weeks post-stroke and correlates with infarct volume; useful in research</li>
<li><strong>Severe TBI:</strong> correlates with GCS, CT findings, and 6-month outcome</li>
<li><strong>Frontotemporal dementia</strong> and other non-AD dementias (less elevated than AD typically)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Non-specific for AD:</strong> elevated in any neuronal injury</li>
<li>For AD specificity, always interpret with Aβ42 and p-tau181</li>
<li>Requires lumbar puncture (invasive; risk of post-LP headache ~10%)</li>
<li>Plasma t-tau assays have historically been unreliable; plasma p-tau217 has largely replaced plasma t-tau</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Diagnostic workup of cognitive impairment (once per lifetime; not a serial monitoring marker)</li>
<li>Distinguish AD from CJD (massively elevated t-tau favors CJD)</li>
<li>Research: tracking disease-modifying therapy response in AD trials</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>t-tau reflects tissue injury and is not directly modifiable. Management targets the underlying cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Alzheimer's disease:</strong> integrate into the AT(N) framework; consider anti-amyloid therapy (lecanemab, donanemab) for early-stage amyloid-confirmed AD</li>
<li><strong>CJD:</strong> supportive care; prion surveillance reporting (mandatory in most countries)</li>
<li><strong>Stroke / TBI:</strong> standard neurocritical care and rehabilitation</li>
<li><strong>Serial CSF t-tau monitoring:</strong> reserved for clinical trials of disease-modifying therapies</li>
</ol>
