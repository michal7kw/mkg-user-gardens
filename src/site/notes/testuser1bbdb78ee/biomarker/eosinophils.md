<h1>Eosinophils</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Eosinophils are bilobed granulocytic white blood cells produced in the bone marrow under the influence of interleukin-5 (IL-5), IL-3, and GM-CSF. Named for the avid uptake of the acidic dye eosin by their cytoplasmic granules, they normally make up just 1-6% of circulating leukocytes, corresponding to an absolute count of roughly 0.0-0.5 x10^3/µL. Most eosinophils reside in peripheral tissues — particularly the gastrointestinal tract, skin, and respiratory mucosa — and circulate in blood only briefly during transit, so the blood count represents a small, dynamic fraction of the total body pool.</p>
<p>Functionally, eosinophils are central effectors of anti-parasite defense and Type-2 (allergic) inflammation. Upon activation they degranulate, releasing cytotoxic granule proteins such as major basic protein, eosinophil cationic protein, eosinophil peroxidase, and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin, which damage the cuticle of helminth parasites but can also injure host tissue in allergic and hypereosinophilic states. They additionally release leukotrienes, cytokines, and growth factors that amplify and sustain Type-2 immune responses.</p>
<p>Because both the absolute count and the percentage are reported, interpretation requires attention to the total WBC count: a &quot;normal&quot; percentage against a high total WBC can still represent an elevated absolute eosinophil count, which is the clinically preferred metric. In Polish laboratory reports, eosinophils are frequently grouped with basophils and monocytes under the differential heading &quot;Inne (Eo, Bazo, Mono)&quot; — the less abundant leukocyte fractions reported alongside neutrophils and lymphocytes.</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<h3>Factors that Increase Eosinophils (Eosinophilia)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Allergic disease:</strong> Asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, and food allergy drive IL-5-mediated eosinophil expansion.</li>
<li><strong>Drug reactions:</strong> Hypersensitivity reactions (including DRESS syndrome) commonly produce eosinophilia.</li>
<li><strong>Parasitic / helminth infection:</strong> Tissue-invasive helminths are a leading global cause of marked eosinophilia.</li>
<li><strong>Connective tissue / collagen vascular disease:</strong> Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis and other autoimmune conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal insufficiency:</strong> Loss of endogenous cortisol removes the normal suppressive signal, raising eosinophil counts.</li>
<li><strong>Pulmonary eosinophilia:</strong> Eosinophilic pneumonias and allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis.</li>
<li><strong>Neoplasia / hypereosinophilic syndrome:</strong> Clonal eosinophilia, lymphoma, and idiopathic hypereosinophilic syndrome.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Factors that Decrease Eosinophils (Eosinopenia)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acute physiological stress:</strong> Surgery, trauma, and acute illness raise endogenous cortisol and catecholamines, sequestering eosinophils.</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroid therapy:</strong> Exogenous glucocorticoids rapidly drive eosinophils out of circulation (apoptosis and margination).</li>
<li><strong>Acute bacterial or viral infection:</strong> Early acute infection is classically associated with eosinopenia.</li>
<li><strong>Cushing syndrome:</strong> Chronic cortisol excess suppresses circulating eosinophils.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Elevated levels (eosinophilia) are associated with:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><a href="/garden/testuser1bbdb78ee/condition/allergy/" class="internal-link">Allergy</a></strong>: Allergic and atopic disease is the most common cause of mild-to-moderate eosinophilia in developed regions.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/garden/testuser1bbdb78ee/condition/asthma/" class="internal-link">Asthma</a></strong>: Eosinophilic asthma is a distinct Type-2 phenotype, and the blood eosinophil count guides biologic therapy.</li>
<li><strong>Parasitic infection</strong>: The leading cause of marked eosinophilia worldwide, particularly tissue-invasive helminths.</li>
<li><strong>Hypereosinophilic syndrome / neoplasia</strong>: Persistent counts &gt; 1.5 x10^3/µL with end-organ damage require hematologic evaluation.</li>
</ul>
<p>A useful mnemonic for the causes of eosinophilia is <strong>&quot;AAACP&quot;</strong>: <strong>A</strong>llergy/<strong>A</strong>sthma, <strong>A</strong>drenal insufficiency, <strong>A</strong>llergic/parasitic disease, <strong>C</strong>onnective tissue / <strong>C</strong>ollagen vascular disease, and <strong>P</strong>arasites / <strong>P</strong>ulmonary eosinophilia — extended to include neoplasia (hypereosinophilic syndrome) and drug reactions.</p>
<p>Reduced levels (eosinopenia) are associated with:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acute stress and corticosteroid effect</strong>: The most common and clinically benign causes; an isolated low eosinophil count is rarely of independent diagnostic concern.</li>
</ul>
<p>Monitoring use cases:</p>
<ul>
<li>Tracking response to anti-IL-5 biologic therapy in eosinophilic asthma.</li>
<li>Surveillance of treatment response in parasitic infection and hypereosinophilic syndrome.</li>
<li>Supporting the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency when eosinophilia is unexplained.</li>
</ul>
