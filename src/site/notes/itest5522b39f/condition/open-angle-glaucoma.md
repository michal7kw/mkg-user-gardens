<h1>Open-Angle Glaucoma</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) is a chronic, progressive optic neuropathy characterized by the gradual loss of <strong>retinal ganglion cells</strong> and their axons, producing characteristic <strong>cupping of the optic disc</strong> and <strong>visual field loss</strong> that begins in the periphery and advances centrally if untreated. It is the <strong>most common form of glaucoma</strong> (70-90% of cases) and a leading cause of <strong>irreversible blindness worldwide</strong>, affecting ~<strong>60 million people</strong> with projections of 111 million by 2040. POAG is insidious: it is <strong>painless and asymptomatic until advanced</strong>, earning it the moniker &quot;the silent thief of sight&quot; -- by the time a patient notices vision loss, irreversible optic nerve damage has occurred.</p>
<p>The anatomic substrate is the <strong>anterior chamber angle</strong> -- the junction where the iris meets the cornea, through which aqueous humor drains via the <strong>trabecular meshwork</strong> and Schlemm's canal into the episcleral veins. In POAG this angle is <em>open</em> (hence the name) but the <strong>trabecular meshwork outflow resistance is increased</strong>, raising <strong>intraocular pressure (IOP)</strong>. Elevated IOP is the <strong>only modifiable risk factor</strong> and the target of all current therapy, although up to one-third of patients have <strong>normal-tension glaucoma</strong> (NTG), implicating vascular insufficiency and other mechanisms. The vascular hypothesis -- that nocturnal hypotension, vasospasm, and endothelial dysfunction reduce optic nerve perfusion -- explains why systemic conditions (diabetes, hypertension, sleep apnea) and biomarkers of vascular risk (homocysteine, CRP) are associated.</p>
<p>Diagnosis requires a comprehensive eye exam: <strong>tonometry</strong> (IOP; normal 10-21 mmHg), <strong>gonioscopy</strong> (confirms open angle), <strong>ophthalmoscopy</strong> (optic disc cupping, rim notching, nerve fiber layer defects), <strong>OCT</strong> (objective measurement of retinal nerve fiber layer thickness), and <strong>perimetry</strong> (visual field testing; characteristic arcuate scotoma, nasal step). Treatment aims to <strong>lower IOP by 20-50%</strong> and is effective in slowing disease progression, although vision already lost cannot be recovered.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Goal:</strong> reduce IOP by 20-50% from baseline; individualize target based on severity, age, and life expectancy. Reassess IOP, optic nerve, and visual field at regular intervals.</p>
<p><strong>First-line -- topical prostaglandin analogs:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Latanoprost 0.005%</strong> nightly, <strong>bimatoprost 0.01%</strong>, <strong>travoprost 0.004%</strong>, <strong>tafluprost 0.0015%</strong></li>
<li>Reduce IOP by 25-35% by increasing uveoscleral outflow; once-daily dosing; minimal systemic side effects</li>
<li>Side effects: conjunctival hyperemia, eyelash growth, iris and periocular pigmentation, macular edema (rare)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Second-line classes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Beta-blockers</strong> -- timolol 0.25-0.5% BID; reduce aqueous production. <strong>Contraindicated in asthma/COPD, bradycardia, heart block</strong></li>
<li><strong>Alpha-2 agonists</strong> -- brimonidine 0.1-0.2% TID; reduces production and increases uveoscleral outflow. Avoid in infants (apnea). Common allergy</li>
<li><strong>Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors</strong> -- dorzolamide 2% TID (topical) or acetazolamide (oral; reserve for short-term severe cases); reduce aqueous production. Oral causes paresthesias, metabolic acidosis, kidney stones</li>
<li><strong>Rho kinase inhibitors</strong> -- netarsudil, ripasudil; increase trabecular outflow</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Combination drops</strong> improve adherence (e.g., latanoprost + timolol, brimonidine + brinzolamide).</p>
<p><strong>Laser and surgical:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT)</strong> -- increasingly used as first-line (LiGHT trial showed SLT non-inferior to eye drops with better adherence); repeatable, low risk</li>
<li><strong>Minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS)</strong> -- iStent, Kahook Dual Blade, Xen gel stent; combined with cataract surgery for mild-moderate POAG</li>
<li><strong>Trabeculectomy</strong> with mitomycin C or <strong>tube shunt</strong> (Ahmed, Baerveldt) -- for refractory or advanced disease</li>
<li><strong>Sustained-release implants</strong> (bimatoprost implant, Durysta) reduce drop burden</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive / supportive:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Treat systemic vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, sleep apnea); avoid excessive nocturnal blood pressure dips</li>
<li>Nutritional support (omega-3, antioxidants, magnesium) for vascular health</li>
<li>Patient education on adherence -- drops must be taken daily for life</li>
</ul>
