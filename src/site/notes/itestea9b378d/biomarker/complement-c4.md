<h1>Complement C4</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Complement component C4 is a 200 kDa beta-globulin encoded by two tandem genes (C4A and C4B) within the MHC class III region on chromosome 6p21.3. C4 participates in both the <strong>classical</strong> (immune complex-triggered, via C1q-r-s) and <strong>lectin</strong> (mannose-binding lectin-triggered) complement activation pathways. When either pathway is activated, the C1 or MBL serine protease cleaves C4 into C4a (anaphylatoxin) and C4b (which opsonizes pathogens and forms the C3 convertase C4b2a). Because the alternative pathway bypasses C4, C4 consumption specifically indicates classical or lectin pathway activation.</p>
<p>Clinically, low serum C4 is the most useful single marker of classical/lectin pathway activation. The principal uses: (1) <strong>SLE disease activity monitoring</strong> -- C4 is often the first complement component to fall during SLE flares because it sits upstream in the classical pathway, closer to the point of immune complex activation. Falling C4 (with falling C3 and rising anti-dsDNA) precedes clinical flares, particularly lupus nephritis, by weeks to months; (2) <strong>Hereditary angioedema (HAE) screening</strong> -- persistently low C4 between attacks is the single best screening marker for C1-INH deficiency. A normal C4 essentially excludes HAE; (3) <strong>Cryoglobulinemia workup</strong> -- C4 is characteristically very low (often &lt;5 mg/dL) in mixed cryoglobulinemia (Types II/III), typically with normal or mildly low C3; (4) <strong>Glomerulonephritis workup</strong> -- low C4 helps differentiate classical pathway-mediated GN (lupus nephritis, cryoglobulinemic GN, post-strep GN late phase) from alternative pathway-mediated GN (C3 glomerulopathy).</p>
<p>Genetic <strong>C4 null alleles</strong> (especially C4A null) are common in the general population (~30-40%) and are an independent risk factor for SLE development. Patients with C4A null alleles have constitutively lower baseline C4 levels, complicating interpretation. Total C4 gene copy number varies from 0 to 8 in healthy individuals, contributing to baseline variability.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (ACR/EULAR classification criteria component; standard clinical use in SLE and HAE).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range is assay-specific</li>
<li>C4 is less of an acute-phase reactant than C3 -- more specific for complement consumption</li>
<li>Genetic C4A null alleles (common in SLE patients) cause constitutively low C4</li>
<li>C4 is more rapidly and profoundly suppressed than C3 in SLE flares (upstream in classical pathway)</li>
<li>Serial changes more informative than absolute values</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Decrease C4 (complement consumption or genetic deficiency):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Active SLE (classical pathway consumption)</li>
<li>Lupus nephritis (proliferative Class III/IV)</li>
<li>Cryoglobulinemia (mixed types II/III) -- characteristically very low</li>
<li>Hereditary angioedema (C1-INH deficiency) -- persistent low C4 between attacks</li>
<li>Serum sickness</li>
<li>Subacute bacterial endocarditis</li>
<li>Shunt nephritis</li>
<li>Urticarial vasculitis syndrome</li>
<li>Severe sepsis (massive complement consumption)</li>
<li>C4 null allele (genetic; constitutively low)</li>
<li>Complete C4 deficiency (very rare; causes SLE-like disease)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Increase C4 (acute-phase response):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute infection, inflammation</li>
<li>Pregnancy</li>
<li>Malignancy</li>
<li>Corticosteroid therapy (acute)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low C4 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Active SLE / lupus nephritis:</strong> often the first complement to fall; serial monitoring is the standard for SLE flare prediction; combined with C3 and anti-dsDNA, forms the classical &quot;SLE activity panel&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Hereditary angioedema (HAE):</strong> persistently low C4 between attacks is the single best screening marker for HAE; a normal C4 essentially excludes HAE. Confirm with C1-INH antigen and function</li>
<li><strong>Cryoglobulinemia (mixed):</strong> C4 characteristically very low (&lt;5 mg/dL); C3 may be normal or mildly low</li>
<li><strong>Urticarial vasculitis syndrome:</strong> low C4 with chronic urticaria suggests hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis (anti-C1q antibody)</li>
<li><strong>Post-streptococcal GN:</strong> low C3 with normal C4 typically; C4 falls later than C3</li>
<li><strong>Subacute bacterial endocarditis:</strong> immune complex consumption</li>
<li><strong>Genetic C4A null allele:</strong> common; constitutively low C4; risk factor for SLE development</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated C4 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute-phase response (infection, inflammation)</li>
<li>Pregnancy, estrogen therapy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Genetic variability in C4 gene copy number</strong> (0-8 copies) affects baseline; C4A null alleles common</li>
<li><strong>Acute-phase reactant</strong> -- concurrent inflammation can normalize despite consumption</li>
<li><strong>Reference range varies by lab</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>SLE diagnosis and disease activity monitoring (with C3, anti-dsDNA, CH50)</li>
<li>HAE screening (low C4 with low C1-INH function confirms HAE type I/II)</li>
<li>Cryoglobulinemia workup (very low C4 with normal/mildly low C3)</li>
<li>Differential of glomerulonephritis (classical vs alternative pathway)</li>
<li>Urticarial vasculitis workup (low C4 with anti-C1q antibody)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>C4 levels reflect underlying immune activation; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>SLE:</strong> hydroxychloroquine 200-400 mg daily; corticosteroids for acute flares; immunosuppressives (mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, azathioprine); biologics (belimumab, anifrolumab, rituximab)</li>
<li><strong>Lupus nephritis:</strong> mycophenolate + corticosteroids; voclosporin (FDA-approved 2021); rituximab or obinutuzumab for refractory</li>
<li><strong>Hereditary angioedema:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Acute attack: C1-INH concentrate (Berinert, Ruconest), icatibant (bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist), ecallantide (kallikrein inhibitor)</li>
<li>Prophylaxis: C1-INH (Cinryze), lanadelumab (kallikrein inhibitor), berotralstat (oral kallikrein inhibitor), oral attenuated androgens (danazol, stanozolol -- second-line)</li>
<li>Avoid ACE inhibitors (can trigger attacks); avoid estrogen-containing contraceptives</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Cryoglobulinemia:</strong> treat underlying cause (HCV with direct-acting antivirals; autoimmune with immunosuppression); rituximab for severe disease; plasmapheresis for life-threatening</li>
<li><strong>Sepsis / severe infection:</strong> supportive care; antibiotics; the complement consumption reflects the underlying disease</li>
<li><strong>Urticarial vasculitis:</strong> antihistamines; hydroxychloroquine; colchicine; rituximab or mycophenolate for severe</li>
</ol>
