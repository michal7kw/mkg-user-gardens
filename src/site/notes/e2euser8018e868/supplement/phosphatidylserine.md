<h1>Phosphatidylserine</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Phosphatidylserine (PS) is a negatively charged aminophospholipid that is a structural building block of every cell membrane and is especially enriched in nervous tissue, where it comprises an estimated 15% of the brain's total phospholipid pool. It is normally sequestered on the inner (cytoplasmic) leaflet of the plasma membrane by ATP-dependent flippase enzymes, a distribution that is essential for membrane integrity, signal transduction, and the survival of neurons. Endogenous PS is synthesized from phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine via base-exchange reactions, but synthesis efficiency and membrane PS content decline with age, providing the rationale for supplementation.</p>
<p>As a supplement, PS is best known for cognitive and stress-related applications. It is one of a small number of dietary ingredients granted an FDA-qualified health claim, which permits the carefully hedged statement that PS &quot;may reduce the risk of cognitive dysfunction and dementia in the elderly,&quot; albeit with the acknowledgment that the supporting evidence is limited and not conclusive. Clinical interest centers on two domains: age-associated memory impairment and mild cognitive decline, and the modulation of the cortisol stress response in both psychologically and physically stressed populations. Early positive trials used PS extracted from bovine cerebral cortex; because of bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) concerns, commercial PS is now manufactured from soy or sunflower lecithin, and a portion of the favorable cognitive literature reflects the older bovine source.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level:</strong> 4/5</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Phosphatidylserine acts through several complementary mechanisms rooted in its role as a membrane phospholipid:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Membrane fluidity and receptor function:</strong> By incorporating into neuronal membranes, PS preserves the fluid lipid environment required for the proper conformation and signaling of membrane-bound receptors, ion channels, and transporters, which tends to deteriorate with aging and oxidative stress.</li>
<li><strong>Neurotransmitter release:</strong> PS enhances the release of acetylcholine and dopamine and supports cholinergic neurotransmission, a pathway central to learning and memory and one that degrades in Alzheimer's-type dementia.</li>
<li><strong>Intracellular signaling:</strong> PS is an essential cofactor for protein kinase C (PKC) and other signaling enzymes, and it supports nerve growth factor (NGF) responsiveness, both of which contribute to synaptic plasticity and neuronal maintenance.</li>
<li><strong>HPA-axis and cortisol modulation:</strong> Supplemental PS blunts the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis response to acute stress, attenuating ACTH and cortisol release. This underlies its observed effects on exercise-induced and psychological stress.</li>
<li><strong>Cerebral glucose metabolism:</strong> PS has been associated with increased glucose utilization in cortical and hippocampal regions, consistent with improved neuronal energetics.</li>
<li><strong>Synaptic pruning signal:</strong> When externalized to the outer membrane leaflet, PS functions as an &quot;eat-me&quot; signal recognized by microglia, linking it to physiological synapse remodeling and to neuroinflammatory processes in the central nervous system.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Research Summary</h2>
<p>The strongest applications are cognitive support in aging and modulation of the stress response. In randomized and open-label trials, soybean-derived PS at 100-300 mg/day improved memory recognition, recall, and executive measures in elderly subjects with memory complaints, and Kato-Kataoka and colleagues reported memory benefits in elderly Japanese subjects, particularly those with comparatively low baseline scores. PS conjugated with omega-3 fatty acids (PS-DHA) has shown signals of benefit for memory in non-demented older adults. The aggregate cognitive evidence — chiefly the basis for the FDA qualified health claim — is positive in direction but is constrained by small sample sizes, heterogeneity, and reliance on older bovine-source data.</p>
<p>For stress and exercise, controlled trials show that PS attenuates the cortisol rise following moderate-to-intense exercise and reduces markers of exercise-induced oxidative stress, supporting its use before training to limit catabolic stress signaling. Emerging pediatric work, including trials of sunflower PS, has explored attention and behavioral outcomes in children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, with mixed but cautiously encouraging results. Overall the safety profile across trials is favorable.</p>
<h2>Administration Guide</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Typical dose:</strong> 100-300 mg/day of PS, usually divided into two or three doses. The 300 mg/day regimen is the most commonly studied for cognition; 100 mg/day is often used for maintenance.</li>
<li><strong>Stress/exercise dosing:</strong> 400-800 mg/day has been used acutely in exercise-cortisol studies, taken before training.</li>
<li><strong>Timing:</strong> Take with a fat-containing meal to aid absorption. Morning dosing is preferred for daytime cognitive benefits; pre-exercise dosing is used for cortisol modulation.</li>
<li><strong>Form selection:</strong> Sunflower-derived PS is preferred for individuals with soy allergy; soy-derived PS is the most widely available and most studied modern source. PS-DHA formulations combine PS with omega-3 for additive neuronal benefit.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<h3>Contraindications</h3>
<ul>
<li>Soy allergy when using soy-derived PS (choose sunflower-derived PS instead)</li>
<li>Concurrent anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy (use under medical supervision)</li>
</ul>
<h3>Drug Interactions</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Anticoagulants/antiplatelets:</strong> Theoretical additive bleeding risk at higher doses.</li>
<li><strong>Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (e.g., donepezil):</strong> May add to cholinergic activity.</li>
<li><strong>Anticholinergic medications:</strong> PS may counteract intended anticholinergic effects.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Side Effects</h3>
<p>PS is generally well tolerated. Reported adverse effects are mild and uncommon and include gastrointestinal upset and, rarely, insomnia at higher doses. No serious adverse events have been consistently reported in clinical trials of soy- or sunflower-derived PS.</p>
<h2>Quality Considerations</h2>
<ul>
<li>Prefer products that specify the PS source (soy or sunflower) and the actual elemental PS content per serving rather than the weight of the lecithin complex.</li>
<li>Choose supplements verified by third-party testing organizations (USP, NSF, ConsumerLab) for label accuracy.</li>
<li>Bovine-cortex PS is obsolete and should be avoided due to historical BSE transmission concerns.</li>
<li>PS-DHA products should disclose the DHA dose so omega-3 intake can be tracked.</li>
</ul>
