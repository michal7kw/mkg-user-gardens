<h1>Pentose Phosphate Pathway</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), also called the hexose monophosphate shunt or phosphogluconate pathway, is a cytoplasmic glucose-catabolizing route that runs in parallel to glycolysis. Rather than producing ATP, its principal purpose is to supply two metabolic &quot;currencies&quot; that growing and stressed cells cannot do without: <strong>NADPH</strong>, the cell's main source of reducing power for reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defense, and <strong>ribose-5-phosphate (R5P)</strong>, the five-carbon sugar backbone of nucleotides, RNA, DNA, and the cofactors ATP, NAD, FAD, and CoA. It branches from glycolysis at glucose-6-phosphate and can re-feed its carbon back into glycolysis as fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, which makes the pathway a flexible metabolic hub rather than a one-way conduit.</p>
<p>The PPP has two functionally distinct arms. The <strong>oxidative branch</strong> is irreversible and committed: glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) — the rate-limiting and most tightly regulated enzyme of the pathway — oxidizes glucose-6-phosphate, and together with 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase generates two molecules of NADPH per glucose along with ribulose-5-phosphate and one CO2. The <strong>non-oxidative branch</strong> is a reversible series of carbon-shuffling reactions catalyzed by transketolase (thiamine/vitamin B1-dependent) and transaldolase that interconvert three-, four-, five-, six-, and seven-carbon sugars, allowing the cell to balance its demand for NADPH versus ribose according to need. This architecture lets a quiescent cell make NADPH without accumulating excess ribose, and lets a rapidly proliferating cell make ribose for DNA synthesis even when NADPH demand is modest.</p>
<p>Clinically the PPP matters most through G6PD. G6PD deficiency is the <strong>most common human enzyme disorder, affecting an estimated 400 million people worldwide</strong>, and it is maintained at high frequency in malaria-endemic regions because it confers partial protection against severe malaria. Because red blood cells lack mitochondria and depend almost entirely on the PPP for the NADPH that keeps glutathione reduced, G6PD-deficient erythrocytes are uniquely vulnerable to oxidative stress, leading to acute hemolytic anemia after exposure to fava beans, certain drugs (e.g., primaquine, dapsone, rasburicase), or infection. At the other end of the spectrum, many tumors <strong>upregulate</strong> PPP flux to fuel nucleotide synthesis and to defend against the reactive oxygen species generated by rapid proliferation, making the pathway an active target in cancer metabolism research.</p>
<h2>Mechanism and Reactions</h2>
<p><strong>Oxidative phase (irreversible, NADPH-generating):</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)</strong> oxidizes glucose-6-phosphate to 6-phosphogluconolactone, reducing NADP+ to NADPH. This is the committed, rate-limiting step, controlled by the cytosolic NADP+/NADPH ratio.</li>
<li>6-Phosphogluconolactonase hydrolyzes the lactone to 6-phosphogluconate.</li>
<li><strong>6-Phosphogluconate dehydrogenase</strong> oxidatively decarboxylates 6-phosphogluconate to ribulose-5-phosphate, producing a second NADPH and releasing CO2.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Non-oxidative phase (reversible, ribose-balancing):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Ribose-5-phosphate isomerase converts ribulose-5-phosphate to ribose-5-phosphate (for nucleotide synthesis).</li>
<li>Ribulose-5-phosphate epimerase produces xylulose-5-phosphate.</li>
<li><strong>Transketolase</strong> (requires thiamine pyrophosphate) and <strong>transaldolase</strong> then reshuffle carbon among sugar phosphates (sedoheptulose-7-phosphate, erythrose-4-phosphate, fructose-6-phosphate, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate), linking the PPP back to glycolysis.</li>
</ul>
<p>Net yield from the oxidative branch per glucose-6-phosphate fully oxidized through the cycle: roughly 2 NADPH and the building blocks the cell requires, with carbon recycled into glycolytic intermediates as demand dictates.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Antioxidant defense:</strong> NADPH from the PPP regenerates reduced glutathione via glutathione reductase and supports the thioredoxin and catalase systems. Cells with high oxidative load — erythrocytes, the lens of the eye, phagocytes mounting a respiratory burst — are critically dependent on PPP flux.</li>
<li><strong>G6PD deficiency:</strong> An X-linked disorder causing episodic hemolysis triggered by oxidant drugs, fava beans (favism), or infection; it also causes neonatal jaundice. It is generally benign between triggers, and management centers on avoiding known oxidative triggers.</li>
<li><strong>Cancer metabolism:</strong> Proliferating tumor cells divert glucose into the PPP to supply ribose for nucleotides and NADPH for redox buffering and lipid synthesis; oncogenic signaling (e.g., via the PI3K/AKT axis and loss of p53) increases G6PD activity, making the pathway a candidate therapeutic target.</li>
<li><strong>Lipogenic and steroidogenic tissues:</strong> Liver, adipose tissue, lactating mammary gland, and the adrenal cortex run high PPP flux to supply NADPH for fatty acid and steroid biosynthesis.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Regulation</h2>
<p>Flux through the oxidative branch is governed chiefly by the <strong>NADP+/NADPH ratio</strong>: NADP+ availability activates G6PD, while accumulated NADPH inhibits it by product inhibition. Demand for ribose, biosynthetic load, and oxidative stress all raise oxidative-branch flux, whereas a high NADPH charge (a &quot;reduced&quot; cell) throttles it. The reversible non-oxidative branch responds to the relative cellular need for NADPH versus ribose-5-phosphate, allowing carbon to flow either toward nucleotide precursors or back into glycolysis. Cofactor adequacy matters too: transketolase requires thiamine (vitamin B1), and the NADP+/NADPH redox couple ultimately depends on niacin (NAD/NADP precursor) and riboflavin-dependent FAD enzymes that recycle glutathione.</p>
