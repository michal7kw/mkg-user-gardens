<h1>Gilberts Syndrome</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Gilbert syndrome (pronounced &quot;zheel-BAYR&quot;; also called Meulengracht syndrome or unconjugated benign bilirubinemia) is the <strong>most common inherited disorder of bilirubin metabolism</strong>, affecting approximately <strong>5-10% of the population</strong>. It is caused by a polymorphism in the <strong>TATAA box</strong> of the UGT1A1 gene promoter (UGT1A1*28; TA7 allele), leading to approximately <strong>30% reduction in hepatic UDP-glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 activity</strong> -- the enzyme responsible for conjugating bilirubin with glucuronic acid for biliary excretion.</p>
<p><strong>Clinical features:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia:</strong> total bilirubin typically 1-5 mg/dL (normal &lt;1.2); indirect (unconjugated) fraction &gt;85%</li>
<li><strong>Intermittent jaundice:</strong> triggered by fasting, stress, illness, sleep deprivation, alcohol, or menstruation</li>
<li><strong>Normal liver function tests:</strong> AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, albumin, INR all normal</li>
<li><strong>No hemolysis:</strong> normal hemoglobin, haptoglobin, LDH, reticulocyte count</li>
<li><strong>Entirely benign:</strong> no progression to liver disease; normal lifespan</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Diagnosis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Clinical diagnosis (rule out other causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia: hemolysis, medications, Crigler-Najjar, hypothyroidism)</li>
<li>No biopsy or genetic testing needed in typical presentation</li>
<li>Response to <strong>48-hour fast</strong> (bilirubin rises &gt;2x) or <strong>IV nicotinic acid</strong> test (historical; rarely used)</li>
<li>Genetic testing for UGT1A1*28 available but rarely necessary</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The &quot;cardioprotective&quot; paradox:</strong><br />
Mildly elevated bilirubin (as in Gilbert syndrome) is associated with a <strong>~50% reduction in cardiovascular disease risk</strong> in multiple epidemiological studies. Bilirubin is a potent <strong>endogenous antioxidant</strong> (more powerful than vitamin C or E in preventing LDL oxidation). Each 1 mg/dL increase in bilirubin is associated with a ~6-10% reduction in CVD risk. Gilbert syndrome is also associated with reduced risk of certain cancers, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome -- though the protective effects are modest and do not warrant inducing hyperbilirubinemia therapeutically.</p>
<p><strong>Pharmacogenomics significance:</strong><br />
UGT1A1 is involved in the metabolism of several drugs. In Gilbert syndrome:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Irinotecan (chemotherapy):</strong> UGT1A1*28 carriers have reduced metabolism of SN-38 (active metabolite) -&gt; increased risk of severe neutropenia and diarrhea. <strong>Dose reduction</strong> required.</li>
<li><strong>Atazanavir (HIV protease inhibitor):</strong> causes benign hyperbilirubinemia; more pronounced in UGT1A1*28 carriers.</li>
<li><strong>Nilotinib, pazopanib:</strong> increased bilirubin levels in carriers.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>No treatment is needed.</strong> The key management steps are:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Diagnose correctly</strong> (unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia with normal liver function and no hemolysis)</li>
<li><strong>Reassure the patient</strong> that the condition is benign, lifelong, and not associated with liver disease</li>
<li><strong>Avoid unnecessary investigations</strong> (repeated liver panels, abdominal imaging, liver biopsy)</li>
<li><strong>Counsel on triggers</strong> (fasting, illness, stress can precipitate visible jaundice but are not harmful)</li>
<li><strong>Maintain regular meals</strong> (avoid prolonged fasting)</li>
<li><strong>Pharmacogenomic flagging</strong> for irinotecan dosing if relevant</li>
</ol>
