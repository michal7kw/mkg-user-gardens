<h1>Influenza</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Influenza is an acute, highly contagious respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses, of which types A and B are responsible for the seasonal epidemics that recur each winter in temperate climates. Often called &quot;the flu,&quot; it is distinguished from the common cold by its abrupt onset and prominent systemic symptoms — high fever, severe myalgia, and incapacitating fatigue — superimposed on respiratory complaints. Seasonal influenza imposes an enormous global burden, causing on the order of hundreds of thousands of deaths and millions of severe illnesses worldwide each year.</p>
<p>Influenza viruses are RNA viruses whose surface glycoproteins, hemagglutinin and neuraminidase, are the targets of host immunity and the basis of subtyping (e.g., influenza A H1N1, H3N2). Their genomes evolve continuously through point mutation (antigenic drift), which erodes prior immunity and necessitates annual reformulation of the vaccine, and, for influenza A, through reassortment (antigenic shift), which can generate pandemic strains against which the population has little immunity. Transmission occurs mainly via respiratory droplets and aerosols, with a short incubation period of one to four days.</p>
<p>Clinically, influenza matters because, while most healthy adults recover within a week, it causes disproportionate severe disease and death in defined high-risk groups — the very young, the elderly, pregnant people, and those with chronic medical conditions — chiefly through pneumonia and decompensation of underlying illness. Because antivirals are most effective early and prevention is far more effective than treatment, annual vaccination of the population, and especially of high-risk groups, is the central public-health intervention.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The classic presentation is the sudden onset of high fever with chills, dry cough, sore throat, headache, diffuse myalgia and arthralgia, and pronounced malaise. Nasal congestion and rhinorrhea are common; gastrointestinal symptoms occur more often in children. Symptoms typically peak over the first two to three days and resolve over about a week, though cough and fatigue can linger. Warning signs of complicated disease include dyspnea, persistent or recurrent high fever, chest pain, and confusion.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>During recognized influenza season, the diagnosis is frequently made clinically in patients with influenza-like illness. When confirmation alters management — high-risk, hospitalized, or severe patients — rapid molecular assays or RT-PCR for influenza A and B offer the best sensitivity; rapid antigen tests are convenient but less sensitive. Viral culture and subtyping support public-health surveillance.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The groups at highest risk of severe influenza are children (especially under two years), adults over 65, pregnant people, and individuals with chronic cardiac, pulmonary, renal, hepatic, or metabolic disease, or immunosuppression. Lack of seasonal vaccination increases the likelihood and severity of infection across all groups.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Most healthy patients need only supportive care — rest, fluids, and antipyretics. Antiviral therapy with neuraminidase inhibitors (oseltamivir, zanamivir) or baloxavir is recommended for high-risk patients and for anyone with severe or progressive disease, and is most effective when started within 48 hours of symptom onset. Complicated influenza requires hospitalization, oxygen, and treatment of secondary bacterial infection. The cornerstone of management is prevention through annual vaccination, which is reformulated each year to match circulating strains.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Pneumonia — primary viral or secondary bacterial (often <em>Streptococcus pneumoniae</em> or <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em>) — is the most important complication. Influenza also precipitates exacerbations of chronic cardiopulmonary disease, can cause myocarditis and, rarely, neurologic complications, and is associated with a transiently increased risk of acute cardiovascular events. Severe disease may progress to sepsis and respiratory failure.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>The prognosis is excellent for healthy individuals, who usually recover within a week. Severe outcomes and deaths concentrate in the high-risk groups, driven mainly by pneumonia and decompensation of comorbid illness; timely antivirals and vaccination meaningfully reduce this risk.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Seasonal influenza produces annual winter epidemics in temperate regions and more variable, year-round activity in the tropics, infecting an estimated 5-15% of the population in a typical season. Antigenic drift necessitates yearly vaccine updates, while the pandemic potential of antigenic shift in influenza A remains a standing global health concern.</p>
