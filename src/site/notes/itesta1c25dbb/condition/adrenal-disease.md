<h1>Adrenal Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Adrenal disease encompasses disorders of the <strong>adrenal cortex</strong> (cortisol, aldosterone, androgen production) and <strong>adrenal medulla</strong> (catecholamine production). These disorders are classified as <strong>hormone deficiency</strong> or <strong>hormone excess</strong> syndromes:</p>
<p><strong>Adrenal cortex disorders:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>
<p><strong>Adrenal insufficiency (cortisol deficiency):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary (Addison disease):</strong> destruction of adrenal cortex; causes cortisol AND aldosterone deficiency; etiologies: autoimmune (~80% in developed countries), TB (most common globally), bilateral adrenalectomy, hemorrhage, metastasis, infections (HIV, fungal)</li>
<li><strong>Secondary (pituitary ACTH deficiency):</strong> pituitary tumor, Sheehan syndrome, exogenous steroid suppression; cortisol deficient but aldosterone preserved (RAAS intact)</li>
<li><strong>Presentation:</strong> fatigue, weight loss, hypotension, hyperpigmentation (primary only; from elevated ACTH/MSH), hyponatremia, hyperkalemia (primary only), hypoglycemia, salt craving</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal crisis:</strong> life-threatening emergency; severe hypotension, shock, hypoglycemia, altered mental status; triggered by stress (infection, surgery, trauma)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Cushing syndrome (cortisol excess):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ACTH-dependent:</strong> pituitary corticotroph adenoma (Cushing disease; ~70%); ectopic ACTH (small cell lung cancer, carcinoid)</li>
<li><strong>ACTH-independent:</strong> adrenal adenoma, adrenal carcinoma, exogenous glucocorticoids (most common cause overall)</li>
<li><strong>Presentation:</strong> central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, purple striae, proximal myopathy, hypertension, glucose intolerance, osteoporosis, easy bruising, mood changes</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Adrenal adenoma (aldosteronoma) or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia</li>
<li><strong>Presentation:</strong> hypertension (often resistant), hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis</li>
<li>One of the most common causes of secondary hypertension (~5-10%)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>21-hydroxylase deficiency (most common; 95% of cases)</li>
<li>Classic (salt-wasting or simple virilizing) and non-classic (late-onset)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Adrenal medulla disorders:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pheochromocytoma:</strong> catecholamine-secreting tumor; episodic hypertension, headache, sweating, tachycardia (&quot;triplet&quot;); 10% rule (10% bilateral, 10% malignant, 10% extra-adrenal, 10% familial)</li>
<li>Associated with MEN2, VHL, NF1</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Adrenal insufficiency:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hydrocortisone</strong> 15-25 mg/day (split: 10-15 mg AM, 5-10 mg early afternoon)</li>
<li><strong>Fludrocortisone</strong> 0.05-0.2 mg/day (primary AI only; aldosterone replacement)</li>
<li><strong>Stress-dose steroids:</strong> double/triple dose for illness; hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV for surgery/trauma</li>
<li><strong>Medical alert</strong> bracelet; emergency hydrocortisone injection kit</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal crisis:</strong> hydrocortisone 100 mg IV bolus then 50 mg q6h; aggressive IV fluids (normal saline + D5W); treat precipitating cause</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cushing syndrome:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Surgical resection</strong> of the tumor (transsphenoidal for pituitary; adrenalectomy for adrenal)</li>
<li><strong>Medical therapy:</strong> metyrapone, ketoconazole, osilodrostat (Cushing disease; before surgery or if not surgical candidate)</li>
<li><strong>Mifepristone</strong> (glucocorticoid receptor antagonist; for hyperglycemia)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Conn syndrome:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Spironolacne</strong> 50-200 mg/day (aldosterone antagonist; first-line for bilateral hyperplasia)</li>
<li><strong>Surgical adrenalectomy</strong> (for unilateral aldosteronoma; can cure hypertension)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pheochromocytoma:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Alpha-blockade first</strong> (phenoxybenzamine 10-20 mg BID-TID for 1-2 weeks before surgery; prevent hypertensive crisis during surgery)</li>
<li><strong>Beta-blocker</strong> (after alpha-blockade; for tachycardia/arrhythmia)</li>
<li><strong>Surgical resection</strong> (laparoscopic adrenalectomy)</li>
</ul>
