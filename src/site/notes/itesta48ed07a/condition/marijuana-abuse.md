<h1>Marijuana Abuse</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cannabis use disorder (CUD), formerly called marijuana abuse or cannabis dependence, is defined by the DSM-5-TR as a problematic pattern of cannabis use leading to clinically significant impairment. Approximately <strong>9% of adults</strong> who use cannabis will develop CUD; the risk rises to <strong>~17% if use begins in adolescence</strong> and to <strong>25-50% among daily users</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Key compounds:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>THC (delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol):</strong> primary psychoactive compound; partial agonist at <strong>CB1 receptors</strong> (abundant in the brain's reward, memory, and motor centers). Produces euphoria, altered perception, increased appetite, relaxation, and impaired short-term memory and motor coordination</li>
<li><strong>CBD (cannabidiol):</strong> non-intoxicating; anxiolytic and antipsychotic properties; does not activate CB1</li>
</ul>
<p>The average THC content of cannabis has increased dramatically from <strong>~3% in the 1990s to 15-30%</strong> in modern products, with concentrates (dabs, waxes) reaching <strong>60-90%</strong>. This rising potency is a major driver of the increasing CUD prevalence and psychiatric complications.</p>
<p><strong>Key clinical concerns:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Adolescent brain development:</strong> the prefrontal cortex (decision-making, impulse control) continues developing until ~25. Heavy adolescent cannabis use is associated with <strong>cognitive deficits</strong> (IQ decline of ~8 points in persistent heavy users), reduced educational attainment, and increased risk of <strong>schizophrenia</strong> (especially with COMT Val158Val genotype, early heavy use, and family history of psychosis)</li>
<li><strong>Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome (CHS):</strong> a recently characterized condition of cyclical severe vomiting in chronic heavy users, classically relieved by <strong>hot showers/baths</strong>; pathophysiology involves CB1 receptor dysregulation in the gut and hypothalamus; treated by <strong>cannabis cessation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Cannabis withdrawal syndrome:</strong> now recognized by DSM-5; onset 1-2 days after cessation; peaks day 2-6; resolves in 1-2 weeks; symptoms include irritability, anxiety, sleep disturbance, decreased appetite, restlessness, and depressed mood</li>
<li><strong>Gateway hypothesis:</strong> cannabis use is associated with increased risk of later use of harder substances, though causation vs shared vulnerability (common liability) is debated</li>
</ol>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Screening:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>CUDIT-R</strong> (Cannabis Use Disorders Identification Test-Revised): 8-item screening tool; score &gt;=8 suggests hazardous use</li>
<li><strong>DSM-5-TR criteria:</strong> &gt;=2 of 11 symptoms within 12 months (tolerance, withdrawal, using more/longer than intended, craving, failure to fulfill obligations, continued use despite problems, etc.)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Treatment:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Brief intervention:</strong> motivational interviewing; 1-2 sessions can reduce use</li>
<li><strong>Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT):</strong> identifies triggers, develops coping strategies; most evidence for psychosocial intervention</li>
<li><strong>Contingency management:</strong> voucher-based reinforcement of abstinence</li>
<li><strong>No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy</strong> for CUD</li>
<li><strong>Investigational pharmacotherapy:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>N-acetylcysteine (NAC)</strong> 1200-2400 mg/day (targets glutamate; reduces craving in adolescents)</li>
<li><strong>Gabapentin</strong> 1200 mg/day (reduces withdrawal symptoms)</li>
<li><strong>Dronabinol</strong> (synthetic THC; for withdrawal management)</li>
<li><strong>Buspirone, vilazodone</strong> (for co-occurring anxiety/depression)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cannabis withdrawal management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Supportive care</li>
<li>Symptomatic treatment: gabapentin (anxiety, sleep), hydroxyzine (anxiety)</li>
<li>Exercise, hydration, sleep hygiene</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cannabis cessation</strong> (the only definitive treatment)</li>
<li>Hot showers/baths (capsaicin receptor / TRPV1 mechanism)</li>
<li>IV fluids, antiemetics (haloperidol; often more effective than standard antiemetics)</li>
<li>Capsaicin cream applied to abdomen (TRPV1 activation; emerging evidence)</li>
</ul>
