<h1>Secretory IgA</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Secretory Immunoglobulin A (sIgA) is the predominant antibody isotype in mucosal secretions (saliva, tears, breast milk, gastrointestinal fluid, respiratory mucus, genitourinary secretions) and the principal mediator of mucosal immunity. Unlike serum IgA (a monomer), sIgA is a dimer joined by a 15 kDa J-chain and bound to an 80 kDa secretory component (a fragment of the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor added by epithelial cells during transcytosis). The secretory component makes sIgA remarkably resistant to proteolytic degradation, allowing it to function in the harsh protease-rich environment of the gut and respiratory tract.</p>
<p>sIgA functions as &quot;immune exclusion&quot; -- it binds pathogens, toxins, and commensal organisms in the mucus layer, preventing their adherence to and invasion of epithelial cells. Unlike IgG or IgM, sIgA does not activate complement or drive inflammation; it operates as a non-inflammatory first line of defense that &quot;neutralizes without escalating.&quot; Total daily sIgA production (~3-5 grams) exceeds the combined production of all other antibody isotypes, reflecting the massive antigenic load at mucosal surfaces.</p>
<p>Clinically, salivary and stool sIgA are measured as non-invasive surrogates of mucosal immune function. <strong>Low sIgA</strong> has been associated with: (1) increased susceptibility to upper respiratory tract infections and gastrointestinal infections; (2) food allergies and intolerances (failure of immune tolerance); (3) overtraining syndrome in endurance athletes (a key biomarker in sports medicine); (4) chronic psychological stress (HPA-axis activation suppresses sIgA); (5) primary selective IgA deficiency (the most common primary immunodeficiency, ~1 in 600 individuals of European descent); (6) celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease. <strong>Elevated sIgA</strong> can indicate active mucosal infection, autoimmune mucosal disease, or recovery from acute stress (paradoxical early rise).</p>
<p>Salivary sIgA is one of the most studied biomarkers in psychoneuroimmunology and sports medicine due to its non-invasive collection, sensitivity to stress, and rapid response to intervention. Levels fluctuate diurnally (peak in morning, nadir in afternoon) and respond within minutes to psychological stress via sympathetic nervous system activation.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 3/5</strong> (well-validated in research; not in mainstream clinical guidelines for most uses outside IgA deficiency workup).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Salivary sIgA has significant diurnal variation -- always collect morning fasting samples</li>
<li>Significant inter-individual variation; within-subject serial measurements are more useful than absolute values</li>
<li>Acute stress can either raise or lower sIgA depending on timing (early rise, late fall)</li>
<li>Reduced saliva flow (dehydration, Sjögren's, medications) lowers sIgA concentration independent of mucosal production</li>
<li>Assays vary across labs; standardize on one method for serial monitoring</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase sIgA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute mucosal infection (URTI, gastroenteritis)</li>
<li>Active inflammatory bowel disease</li>
<li>Celiac disease (active, untreated)</li>
<li>Recent vaccination (especially oral polio, rotavirus)</li>
<li>Acute psychological stress (short, brief; ~20-60 min post-stress)</li>
<li>Endurance exercise (immediately post-exercise; transient rise)</li>
<li>Chronic stress recovery phase (rebound)</li>
<li>Lactation (breast milk sIgA markedly elevated; provides neonatal immunity)</li>
<li>Some autoimmune diseases</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease sIgA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Selective IgA deficiency</strong> (most common primary immunodeficiency; ~1 in 600 Europeans; serum IgA &lt;7 mg/dL with normal IgG and IgM)</li>
<li><strong>Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID)</strong> -- sIgA and serum immunoglobulins low</li>
<li>Chronic psychological stress (sustained; HPA-axis activation)</li>
<li>Overtraining syndrome (endurance athletes; combines with elevated URTI risk)</li>
<li>Sleep deprivation</li>
<li>Malnutrition, anorexia nervosa</li>
<li>Aging (mucosal immune senescence)</li>
<li>Corticosteroid therapy</li>
<li>Immunosuppressive drugs (methotrexate, biologics)</li>
<li>Chemotherapy / radiation (especially gut-affecting)</li>
<li>Sjögren's syndrome (reduced saliva flow lowers sIgA concentration)</li>
<li>Atopic disease (paradoxically; some allergic individuals have low sIgA)</li>
<li>Smoking (chronic)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low sIgA is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Selective IgA deficiency:</strong> the most common primary immunodeficiency; many patients are asymptomatic, but increased risk of sinopulmonary infections, GI infections (especially Giardia), allergies, autoimmune diseases (celiac, SLE, RA)</li>
<li><strong>Recurrent mucosal infections:</strong> URTI, sinusitis, bronchitis, gastroenteritis -- especially in athletes with overtraining</li>
<li><strong>Food allergies and intolerances:</strong> failure of mucosal immune tolerance</li>
<li><strong>Overtraining syndrome</strong> in endurance athletes -- sIgA is the most consistent biomarker; low sIgA predicts URTI in elite athletes</li>
<li><strong>Chronic stress and burnout:</strong> HPA-axis activation suppresses sIgA</li>
<li><strong>Celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease:</strong> paradoxically low in some patients despite mucosal inflammation</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated sIgA is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Active mucosal infection</strong> -- the immune system is mounting a response</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune mucosal disease</strong> -- some lupus, Sjögren's, IBD patterns</li>
<li><strong>Recent mucosal vaccination</strong></li>
<li><strong>Recovery phase</strong> after acute stress (rebound)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Significant inter-individual variation</strong> limits absolute-value interpretation; within-subject serial measurement preferred</li>
<li><strong>Not in mainstream diagnostic algorithms</strong> for most diseases outside IgA deficiency workup</li>
<li><strong>Multiple sample types</strong> (saliva, stool, serum) measure different things; serum total IgA is distinct from secretory IgA</li>
<li><strong>Acute stress effects bidirectional</strong> -- interpretation requires context</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>IgA deficiency screening (with serum IgA, IgG, IgM levels)</li>
<li>Sports medicine: overtraining and infection risk monitoring</li>
<li>Psychoneuroimmunology research: stress biomarker</li>
<li>Functional medicine: gut immune function assessment</li>
<li>Research: mucosal vaccination response</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>sIgA levels respond to interventions within 1-4 weeks:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Probiotics</strong> (multi-strain, especially <em>Lactobacillus</em> and <em>Bifidobacterium</em> species) -- meta-analyses show modest sIgA increase (~15-30%); useful in athletes, immunocompromised, and IgA-deficient populations</li>
<li><strong>L-Glutamine</strong> 5-10 g/day -- preferred fuel for enterocytes and immune cells; modest sIgA increase in athletes and post-surgical patients</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin A</strong> (2000-5000 IU daily; or beta-carotene) -- essential for mucosal epithelial integrity and IgA production; sIgA increase in deficient individuals</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> (2000-5000 IU D3 daily) -- observational evidence links low D to low sIgA; supplementation may raise sIgA in deficient patients</li>
<li><strong>Zinc</strong> 15-30 mg daily -- co-factor for immune cell function; modest sIgA support</li>
<li><strong>Colostrum / bovine IgG</strong> supplements -- small studies suggest mucosal immune support</li>
<li><strong>Stress management</strong> -- mindfulness, meditation, breathwork, yoga; reduce HPA-axis activation</li>
<li><strong>Sleep optimization</strong> -- 7-9 h/night; address sleep apnea, insomnia</li>
<li><strong>Reduce overtraining</strong> -- periodize training; schedule rest weeks; monitor resting HR and HRV</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying gut dysbiosis</strong> -- targeted antimicrobial or probiotic therapy based on stool testing</li>
<li><strong>Avoid mucosal irritants</strong> -- smoking cessation, limit alcohol, address GERD</li>
<li><strong>For IgA deficiency:</strong> treat infections early; consider IVIG (only if concomitant IgG deficiency); avoid IgA-containing blood products (risk of anaphylaxis)</li>
</ol>
