<h1>Substance Withdrawal Syndrome</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Substance withdrawal syndrome is the constellation of <strong>physiological and psychological symptoms</strong> that emerge when a person with <strong>physiological dependence</strong> abruptly reduces or stops a substance. Dependence develops through <strong>neuroadaptation</strong>: the brain counter-regulates the acute effects of the substance, so removal unmasks this adaptation and produces a rebound in the opposite direction. For <strong>central nervous system depressants</strong> (alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates) withdrawal produces <strong>CNS hyperexcitability</strong> (tremor, seizures, delirium tremens) that can be <strong>life-threatening</strong>; for <strong>opioids</strong> withdrawal is intensely distressing but rarely fatal; for <strong>stimulants</strong> (cocaine, amphetamine) withdrawal is primarily psychological (depression, anhedonia, hypersomnia).</p>
<p>The clinical pattern is substance-specific. <strong>Alcohol withdrawal</strong> begins 6-24 hours after the last drink, peaks at 24-72 hours, and can progress through stages: mild autonomic symptoms (tremor, tachycardia, anxiety), then <strong>alcohol hallucinosis</strong>, then <strong>withdrawal seizures</strong> (generalized tonic-clonic, 24-48 hours), and finally <strong>delirium tremens</strong> (DTs: confusion, vivid hallucinations, severe autonomic instability; mortality 5-15% untreated) at 48-96 hours. The <strong>kindling effect</strong> -- each withdrawal episode lowers the seizure threshold and worsens subsequent withdrawals -- underscores the importance of treating each episode aggressively. <strong>Opioid withdrawal</strong> (methadone, heroin, prescription opioids) manifests at 8-24 hours (short-acting) to 30-72 hours (methadone) with <strong>rhinorrhea, lacrimation, myalgias, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, piloerection (&quot;cold turkey&quot;), and severe dysphoria</strong> -- clinically like severe influenza but not life-threatening in otherwise healthy adults.</p>
<p>The diagnostic principles are the same regardless of substance: confirm the substance (history, urine drug screen), assess severity with validated scales (<strong>CIWA-Ar</strong> for alcohol, <strong>COWS</strong> for opioids), screen for comorbid medical issues (infection, electrolyte derangements, Wernicke encephalopathy, intoxication with other substances), and determine the level of care (outpatient, inpatient, ICU for severe alcohol withdrawal). The <strong>principles of treatment</strong> are: substitute a long-acting agent of the same class, taper gradually, treat autonomic and psychological symptoms, prevent complications (seizures, Wernicke), and bridge to <strong>long-term relapse-prevention therapy</strong>.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>General approach:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Identify substance(s), dose, duration, and time of last use</li>
<li>Assess severity with validated scales: <strong>CIWA-Ar</strong> (alcohol), <strong>COWS</strong> (opioids)</li>
<li>Screen for medical complications (electrolytes, LFTs, CBC, ECG, infection)</li>
<li>Determine setting: outpatient (mild, stable support), inpatient (moderate-severe, comorbidity), ICU (severe DTs, respiratory compromise)</li>
<li>Always give <strong>thiamine 100 mg IV before any glucose</strong> in suspected alcohol withdrawal (prevents Wernicke encephalopathy)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal (life-threatening):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Benzodiazepines are the cornerstone</strong> -- long-acting (diazepam, chlordiazepoxide) preferred for self-taper; lorazepam/oxazepam if liver disease. <strong>Symptom-triggered dosing</strong> (CIWA-Ar-guided) is superior to fixed-schedule</li>
<li><strong>Phenobarbital</strong> -- alternative or adjunct for refractory withdrawal; long half-life</li>
<li><strong>CIWA-Ar-guided front-loading</strong> -- give diazepam 10-20 mg hourly until calm, then prn</li>
<li>Treat autonomic symptoms: <strong>beta-blockers</strong> (avoid masking severity), <strong>clonidine</strong>, <strong>dexmedetomidine</strong> for refractory autonomic instability</li>
<li>Seizures: IV lorazepam or diazepam</li>
<li><strong>Delirium tremens</strong>: ICU admission, high-dose benzodiazepines, supportive care</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Opioid withdrawal (distressing but rarely fatal):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Buprenorphine</strong> (partial mu-agonist) -- 2-8 mg sublingual; start when COWS &gt;=8 (to avoid precipitated withdrawal)</li>
<li><strong>Methadone</strong> (full mu-agonist) -- 10-20 mg initially; can only be prescribed by OTP-licensed clinicians in the US</li>
<li><strong>Alpha-2 agonists</strong> -- clonidine 0.1-0.3 mg q6h, lofexidine 0.54 mg q6h (FDA-approved) for autonomic symptoms</li>
<li><strong>Symptomatic</strong>: loperamide (diarrhea), ondansetron (nausea), NSAIDs (myalgias), hydroxyzine (anxiety)</li>
<li><strong>Bridge to maintenance therapy</strong>: buprenorphine or methadone maintenance reduces relapse and overdose mortality</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Stimulant withdrawal (cocaine, methamphetamine):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>No FDA-approved medication; supportive care</li>
<li>Treat depression and hypersomnia; consider mirtazapine, bupropion</li>
<li>Cognitive behavioral therapy and contingency management</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Nicotine withdrawal:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>NRT, varenicline, bupropion (see Tobacco Use Disorder)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Long-term management (after acute withdrawal):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Relapse-prevention pharmacotherapy</strong>: naltrexone, acamprosate (alcohol); buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone (opioids)</li>
<li><strong>Behavioral therapy</strong>: cognitive behavioral therapy, motivational interviewing, 12-step programs</li>
<li><strong>Treat comorbid psychiatric and medical conditions</strong></li>
<li><strong>Harm reduction</strong>: naloxone distribution, safe injection education, syringe services</li>
</ul>
