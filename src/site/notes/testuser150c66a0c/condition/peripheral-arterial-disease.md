<h1>Peripheral Arterial Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is an atherosclerotic occlusive disease of the lower-extremity arteries that reduces blood supply to the limbs. It affects <strong>&gt;230 million people worldwide</strong> and prevalence rises sharply with age (~10% at age 50, 15-20% over age 70). PAD is not merely a leg problem -- it is a sentinel marker of systemic atherosclerosis; patients with PAD face a <strong>3-5x higher risk of cardiovascular death</strong> (MI, stroke) and a 5-year mortality of <strong>~30%</strong>, comparable to many cancers. Approximately half of patients are asymptomatic, ~30% have atypical leg symptoms, and only ~10-15% have classic claudication, making the disease substantially under-diagnosed.</p>
<p>The pathophysiology is identical to coronary atherosclerosis: endothelial injury, lipid deposition, plaque formation, and luminal narrowing, complicated by thrombosis. <strong>Claudication</strong> (derived from <em>claudicare</em>, &quot;to limp&quot;) is reproducible leg pain, cramping, or fatigue that occurs with walking and is <strong>relieved within 10 minutes of rest</strong>. It reflects the supply-demand mismatch of working muscle. Disease progression leads to <strong>critical limb ischemia (CLI)</strong> -- rest pain, non-healing ulcers, or gangrene -- which threatens limb viability and requires urgent revascularization to prevent amputation.</p>
<p>The diagnostic cornerstone is the <strong>ankle-brachial index (ABI)</strong>: the ratio of ankle to brachial systolic blood pressure. <strong>ABI &lt;=0.90</strong> is diagnostic of PAD (sensitivity ~95%, specificity ~99%). Normal ABI is 1.00-1.40; values <strong>&gt;1.40</strong> indicate non-compressible (calcified) arteries common in diabetes or CKD, which falsely elevates ABI and necessitates toe-brachial index measurement.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Risk factor modification (all patients):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong> -- the single most effective intervention; reduces disease progression, CLI, and amputation risk</li>
<li><strong>Statins</strong> -- high-intensity (atorvastatin 40-80 mg or rosuvastatin 20-40 mg); target LDL &lt;70 mg/dL. Reduce CV events and may improve claudication</li>
<li><strong>Blood pressure control</strong> (&lt;130/80 mmHg)</li>
<li><strong>Diabetes management</strong> (HbA1c &lt;7%; any ACE inhibitor or ARB preferred)</li>
<li><strong>Antiplatelet therapy</strong> -- aspirin 75-100 mg daily OR clopidogrel 75 mg daily (CAPRIE trial favored clopidogrel in PAD)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Symptomatic therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Supervised exercise therapy</strong> -- 30-45 minutes of walking to near-maximal claudication, 3x/week for 12 weeks. First-line; improves peak walking time by &gt;150%, rivaling revascularization</li>
<li><strong>Cilostazol</strong> (100 mg BID) -- PDE-3 inhibitor; improves claudication distance. <strong>Contraindicated in heart failure</strong> (all PDE-3 inhibitors increase mortality in HF)</li>
<li><strong>Pentoxifylline</strong> -- weak evidence; rarely used</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Revascularization (endovascular or surgical) -- indications:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Critical limb ischemia (rest pain, ulceration, gangrene) -- urgent</li>
<li>Disabling claudication that fails medical therapy</li>
<li>Endovascular (angioplasty +/- stent) preferred for short lesions and high operative-risk patients</li>
<li>Surgical bypass for complex multi-segment disease and good vein conduit</li>
</ul>
