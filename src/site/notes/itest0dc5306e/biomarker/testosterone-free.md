<h1>Testosterone Free</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Free testosterone is the fraction of total circulating testosterone that is <strong>not bound to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) or albumin</strong> and is therefore immediately available to diffuse into target cells and exert androgenic effects. Although it represents only roughly <strong>1-2% of total testosterone</strong>, this unbound pool -- together with the loosely albumin-bound fraction (the &quot;bioavailable&quot; testosterone, ~30-40%) -- is the most physiologically meaningful measure of androgen action.</p>
<p>Free testosterone is especially informative when <strong>SHBG concentrations are abnormal</strong>. Conditions that lower SHBG (insulin resistance, obesity, hypothyroidism) raise free testosterone despite normal or low total; conditions that raise SHBG (aging, hyperthyroidism, estrogen excess, liver disease) lower free testosterone despite normal total. For this reason, <strong>Endocrine Society guidelines recommend assessing free or calculated free testosterone</strong> when total values are borderline or when clinical suspicion of hypogonadism persists despite a &quot;normal&quot; total.</p>
<p>Measurement methodology matters: <strong>equilibrium dialysis is the reference standard</strong>, while <strong>calculated free testosterone</strong> (derived from total testosterone, SHBG, and albumin via the Vermeulen equation) is widely used and generally reliable. <strong>Direct analog immunoassays are considered less accurate</strong> and are not recommended. Because of significant diurnal variation, samples should be collected in the morning (before 10 AM), and abnormal results should be confirmed on a repeat fasting morning draw.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (Endocrine Society guideline-endorsed for hypogonadism/PCOS workup).</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase free testosterone:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PCOS and other hyperandrogenic states</strong> (in women)</li>
<li><strong>Anabolic-androgenic steroid use</strong> (exogenous testosterone, nandrolone, oxandrolone)</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal or testicular tumors</strong> (testosterone-secreting)</li>
<li><strong>Congenital adrenal hyperplasia</strong> (21-hydroxylase deficiency)</li>
<li><strong>Low SHBG states</strong> -- insulin resistance, obesity, hypothyroidism, glucocorticoid excess, nephrotic syndrome</li>
<li><strong>Androgen deprivation therapy rebound</strong> (rare)</li>
<li><strong>Clomiphene citrate</strong> (selective estrogen receptor modulator; increases LH and endogenous testosterone)</li>
<li><strong>hCG therapy</strong> (stimulates Leydig cell testosterone production)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease free testosterone:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hypogonadism</strong> -- Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), cryptorchidism, orchitis, chemotherapy/radiation</li>
<li><strong>Secondary / central hypogonadism</strong> -- pituitary tumors, Kallmann syndrome, hemochromatosis, hyperprolactinemia</li>
<li><strong>Late-onset hypogonadism (LOH)</strong> -- age-related decline in testosterone</li>
<li><strong>Chronic illness</strong> -- CKD, HIV, COPD, cirrhosis, type 2 diabetes, obesity</li>
<li><strong>Opioids</strong> (chronic; suppress HPG axis)</li>
<li><strong>Glucocorticoids</strong> (chronic; suppress HPG axis)</li>
<li><strong>Anorexia nervosa, severe weight loss</strong> (hypothalamic suppression)</li>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency, severe illness</strong></li>
<li><strong>High SHBG states</strong> -- hyperthyroidism, estrogen therapy, liver disease, aging, anticonvulsants (raises bound fraction; lowers free fraction)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated free testosterone is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Men:</strong> anabolic steroid use (most common); testicular tumors; adrenal tumors; congenital adrenal hyperplasia</li>
<li><strong>Women:</strong> PCOS (the most common cause); congenital adrenal hyperplasia; androgen-secreting tumors (ovarian, adrenal)</li>
<li><strong>Both sexes:</strong> hirsutism, acne, virilization (voice deepening, clitoromegaly in women; gynecomastia, low LH/FSH in men from exogenous steroids)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced free testosterone is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Men: hypogonadism symptoms:</strong> low libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue, reduced muscle mass, depressed mood, cognitive impairment, osteoporosis, anemia</li>
<li><strong>Women:</strong> reduced libido, fatigue, bone loss, reduced muscle mass</li>
<li><strong>Causes:</strong> primary (Klinefelter, orchitis), secondary (pituitary tumor, Kallmann), late-onset (aging), chronic illness, opioids, glucocorticoids</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Always interpret with total testosterone and SHBG</strong> (free T is calculated from these)</li>
<li><strong>Diurnal variation</strong> requires morning fasting sample</li>
<li><strong>Confirm abnormal results</strong> on repeat sample (significant inter-day variability)</li>
<li><strong>Direct analog immunoassay is inaccurate</strong> -- use equilibrium dialysis or Vermeulen calculation</li>
<li><strong>Reference range varies by lab and method</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Hypogonadism workup (with total T, SHBG, LH, FSH, prolactin)</li>
<li>PCOS evaluation (with total T, SHBG, LH, FSH, DHEA-S)</li>
<li>TRT monitoring (target mid-normal range free T; q3-6 months after dose change)</li>
<li>Anti-androgen therapy monitoring (prostate cancer; target castrate-range)</li>
<li>Functional medicine: androgen deficiency symptoms despite normal total T</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>For men with functional or obesity-related declines in free testosterone, lifestyle and targeted supplementation can improve androgen status:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> (5-10% body weight if obese) -- the most impactful intervention; reduces insulin resistance and SHBG, raising free T</li>
<li><strong>Resistance training</strong> 2-3x/week -- increases testosterone and SHBG ratio</li>
<li><strong>Adequate sleep</strong> (7-9 h/night) -- sleep deprivation lowers testosterone 10-15%</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> 2000-5000 IU D3 daily (in deficient patients) -- vitamin D deficiency linked to low testosterone</li>
<li><strong>Zinc</strong> 15-30 mg daily (in deficient patients) -- zinc is essential for testosterone synthesis</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium</strong> 200-400 mg daily (in deficient patients) -- supports metabolic health and testosterone</li>
<li><strong>Boron</strong> 3-10 mg daily -- small studies suggest modest free T increase via SHBG reduction</li>
<li><strong>Tongkat Ali (Eurycoma longifolia)</strong> 200-400 mg daily -- small RCTs show free T improvement in men with low baseline</li>
<li><strong>Ashwagandha</strong> 300-600 mg daily (KSM-66) -- reduces cortisol; supports testosterone in chronically stressed men</li>
<li><strong>Fenugreek</strong> 500-600 mg daily -- small studies suggest modest effect</li>
<li><strong>Reduce alcohol</strong> (chronic excess lowers testosterone)</li>
<li><strong>Stop smoking</strong> (chronic smoking lowers testosterone)</li>
<li><strong>Address comorbidities:</strong> type 2 diabetes, OSA, depression, chronic illness</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> testosterone-lowering medications (opioids, glucocorticoids) when possible</li>
<li><strong>Confirmed pathological hypogonadism</strong> -- refer for TRT (testosterone cypionate, gel, patch, pellet); monitor q3-6 months</li>
</ol>
