<h1>SHBG</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) is a ~90 kDa glycoprotein synthesized primarily by the liver that binds circulating sex steroids -- chiefly <strong>testosterone</strong> and <strong>estradiol</strong> -- with high affinity. Hormone bound to SHBG is largely unavailable to tissues, so SHBG acts as a regulator of the bioactive (free) fraction: a rise in SHBG lowers free hormone availability, while a fall raises it. Only approximately <strong>1-2% of circulating testosterone is unbound</strong> (free) and immediately bioactive; another ~30-40% is loosely bound to albumin and considered bioavailable; the remainder (~60%) is bound to SHBG and biologically inactive. This makes SHBG an essential interpretive companion to total testosterone and estradiol measurements -- without SHBG, free or bioavailable hormone cannot be calculated accurately.</p>
<p>SHBG concentrations are dynamically controlled by metabolic and hormonal signals:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insulin suppresses SHBG</strong> -- so low SHBG commonly accompanies insulin resistance, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen raises SHBG</strong> -- oral contraceptives, pregnancy, HRT</li>
<li><strong>Thyroid hormone raises SHBG</strong> -- hyperthyroidism raises SHBG; hypothyroidism lowers it</li>
<li><strong>Androgens suppress SHBG</strong> -- testosterone, anabolic steroids</li>
<li><strong>Glucocorticoids suppress SHBG</strong> -- Cushing syndrome</li>
<li><strong>Growth hormone suppresses SHBG</strong> -- acromegaly</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease raises SHBG</strong> -- cirrhosis, hepatitis</li>
<li><strong>Aging raises SHBG</strong> -- men &gt;50; women &gt;50</li>
</ul>
<p>Clinically, SHBG helps explain discordances between symptoms and total hormone levels. A man with a &quot;normal&quot; total testosterone but high SHBG may still be functionally androgen-deficient because his free testosterone is low; a woman with low SHBG may have androgen-excess features (as in PCOS) despite an unremarkable total testosterone. SHBG therefore sharpens the assessment of hypogonadism, PCOS, and metabolic risk.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (widely measured; Endocrine Society guideline-endorsed for free testosterone calculation).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals are sex-specific and laboratory-dependent</li>
<li>Women's ranges are substantially higher than men's</li>
<li>SHBG rises progressively with age in men (key driver of declining free testosterone)</li>
<li>Pregnancy: SHBG rises 5-10x due to estrogen</li>
<li>Diurnal variation: morning higher than evening (like testosterone)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase SHBG (reduces free hormone availability):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong> (thyroid hormone upregulates SHBG synthesis)</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease / cirrhosis</strong> (impaired clearance)</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen excess</strong> (oral contraceptives, pregnancy, HRT, estrogen-secreting tumors)</li>
<li><strong>Aging</strong> (especially men &gt;60)</li>
<li><strong>Anorexia nervosa, low body weight</strong></li>
<li><strong>Anticonvulsants</strong> (phenytoin)</li>
<li><strong>Hyperprolactinemia</strong> (mild)</li>
<li><strong>HIV/AIDS</strong></li>
<li><strong>Cigarette smoking</strong> (mild)</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction, fasting</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease SHBG (raises free hormone availability):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insulin resistance</strong> (the principal driver in modern populations)</li>
<li><strong>Obesity</strong> (especially visceral adiposity)</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes</strong></li>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong></li>
<li><strong>Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)</strong></li>
<li><strong>Hyperandrogenism</strong> (testosterone, anabolic-androgenic steroids)</li>
<li><strong>Glucocorticoid excess</strong> (Cushing syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Growth hormone excess</strong> (acromegaly)</li>
<li><strong>Nephrotic syndrome</strong> (urinary loss)</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroid therapy</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low SHBG is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome:</strong> the most common cause; SHBG is an early metabolic marker</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes:</strong> low SHBG predicts incident T2DM</li>
<li><strong>PCOS:</strong> low SHBG amplifies free androgen exposure; contributes to hyperandrogenic phenotype</li>
<li><strong>Obesity:</strong> visceral fat drives insulin resistance and SHBG suppression</li>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong></li>
<li><strong>Anabolic steroid use</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>High SHBG is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Functional hypogonadism in aging men:</strong> SHBG rises with age; total testosterone may appear normal but free testosterone is low</li>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong></li>
<li><strong>Liver disease</strong></li>
<li><strong>Estrogen excess</strong> (HRT, oral contraceptives)</li>
<li><strong>Anorexia nervosa</strong></li>
<li><strong>Anticonvulsant therapy</strong> (phenytoin)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>SHBG itself is rarely a treatment target</strong> -- the goal is to address the upstream driver</li>
<li><strong>Free testosterone can be calculated</strong> (Vermeulen equation) or measured directly (equilibrium dialysis) -- calculated free T requires SHBG and albumin</li>
<li><strong>Bioavailable testosterone</strong> (albumin + free) is an alternative calculation</li>
<li><strong>Assay variability</strong> across labs</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Hypogonadism workup (with total testosterone; for free T calculation)</li>
<li>PCOS evaluation (with total testosterone, LH, FSH)</li>
<li>Hyperthyroidism / hypothyroidism workup (with TSH, FT4)</li>
<li>Monitor TRT response (SHBG may fall)</li>
<li>Insulin resistance / metabolic risk assessment (low SHBG is an early marker)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>SHBG largely tracks metabolic and hormonal context; the most evidence-based way to normalize a pathologically low SHBG is to improve insulin sensitivity:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> (5-10% body weight in obese patients) -- raises SHBG via reduced insulin resistance</li>
<li><strong>Reduce hepatic fat</strong> (Mediterranean diet, exercise) -- improves insulin sensitivity</li>
<li><strong>Aerobic and resistance exercise</strong> (150 min/week moderate intensity) -- raises SHBG</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong> rich in olive oil, fish, vegetables -- improves metabolic profile</li>
<li><strong>Reduce refined carbohydrates</strong> and added sugars</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying insulin resistance:</strong> metformin, GLP-1 agonists, pioglitazone (T2DM)</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying thyroid disease:</strong> levothyroxine for hypothyroidism; methimazole for hyperthyroidism</li>
<li><strong>Rationalize exogenous hormone exposure:</strong> adjust or discontinue oral contraceptives, HRT, anabolic steroids as appropriate</li>
<li><strong>Discontinue corticosteroids</strong> if applicable</li>
<li><strong>Boron</strong> 3-10 mg daily -- small studies suggest modest SHBG reduction in men (functional medicine; not standard care)</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium</strong> 200-400 mg daily (in deficient patients) -- supports metabolic health</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> 2000-5000 IU D3 daily (in deficient patients) -- observational link with SHBG</li>
</ol>
