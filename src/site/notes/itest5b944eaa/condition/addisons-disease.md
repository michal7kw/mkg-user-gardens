<h1>Addisons Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Addison's disease -- <strong>primary adrenal insufficiency</strong> -- is a rare disorder (prevalence ~100-140 per million) in which the <strong>adrenal cortex</strong> is destroyed, producing <strong>deficiency of cortisol, aldosterone, and adrenal androgens</strong>. The most common cause in developed countries is <strong>autoimmune adrenalitis</strong> (~70-90%; associated with HLA-DR3/DR4 and frequently part of <strong>autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 2</strong> with type 1 diabetes, Hashimoto thyroiditis, and pernicious anemia). In developing countries, <strong>tuberculosis</strong> remains the leading cause. Other causes include <strong>adrenal hemorrhage</strong> (anticoagulants, sepsis, trauma), <strong>metastatic cancer</strong> (lung, breast, melanoma), <strong>infiltrative disease</strong> (sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis), <strong>HIV/AIDS</strong>, <strong>genetic</strong> (adrenoleukodystrophy, congenital adrenal hyperplasia), and <strong>iatrogenic</strong> (bilateral adrenalectomy; withdrawal of chronic glucocorticoid therapy -- the most common cause of adrenal insufficiency overall, but technically &quot;secondary&quot; from HPA suppression).</p>
<p>The clinical presentation reflects the <strong>slow, progressive</strong> destruction of the adrenal cortex (typically &gt;90% lost before symptoms appear). <strong>Generalized fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, and gastrointestinal symptoms</strong> (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain) develop insidiously. <strong>Hyperpigmentation</strong> -- a cardinal sign distinguishing primary from secondary adrenal insufficiency -- occurs from <strong>elevated ACTH</strong> (loss of cortisol feedback -&gt; pituitary ACTH rises) and co-secreted <strong>melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH)</strong>, producing <strong>bronze darkening of the skin</strong>, especially in sun-exposed areas, <strong>palmar creases, gingival mucosa, recent scars, and extensor surfaces</strong>. <strong>Mineralocorticoid deficiency</strong> produces <strong>salt craving, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypotension</strong>, and <strong>postural dizziness</strong>. <strong>Hypoglycemia</strong> may occur (cortisol deficiency impairs gluconeogenesis), especially in children and during fasting.</p>
<p>The feared complication is <strong>adrenal crisis</strong> -- acute, severe cortisol deficiency triggered by <strong>stress</strong> (infection, surgery, trauma, vomiting, missed medication). Presentation includes <strong>shock refractory to fluids, abdominal pain, vomiting, fever, confusion, and coma</strong>; untreated, it is <strong>fatal within hours</strong>. Adrenal crisis is the leading cause of death in Addison's disease and must be <strong>anticipated and prevented</strong> through patient education on stress-dose steroids and immediate hydrocortisone during illness. <strong>Diagnosis</strong> rests on <strong>morning cortisol</strong> (low, &lt;3 mcg/dL is diagnostic; &gt;18 excludes), <strong>ACTH</strong> (elevated in primary, low in secondary), <strong>renin and aldosterone</strong> (renin high, aldosterone low in primary), <strong>21-hydroxylase antibodies</strong> (confirm autoimmune etiology), and the <strong>250 mcg cosyntropin stimulation test</strong> (cortrostim test; cortisol fails to rise above 18-20 mcg/dL at 30 and 60 minutes).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Immediate adrenal crisis management (EMERGENCY):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV bolus</strong>, then 50 mg IV every 6 hours (or 200 mg/24h continuous infusion) until stable; then taper to maintenance over 1-3 days</li>
<li><strong>Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation</strong> -- normal saline + 5% dextrose (for hypoglycemia); correct electrolyte abnormalities (hyponatremia, hyperkalemia typically correct with fluids and hydrocortisone)</li>
<li><strong>Identify and treat the precipitant</strong> -- infection (broad-spectrum antibiotics), surgery, trauma</li>
<li><strong>Do NOT delay hydrocortisone</strong> for diagnostic testing if crisis suspected; draw cortisol and ACTH first, then treat empirically</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Chronic replacement therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Glucocorticoid replacement</strong> -- <strong>hydrocortisone 15-25 mg/day in divided doses</strong> (e.g., 15 mg morning + 5 mg afternoon; or 10 mg morning + 5 mg noon + 5 mg evening) -- mimics the physiologic circadian rhythm. Alternative: <strong>prednisone 5 mg morning</strong> (longer half-life, once-daily dosing; less physiological but more convenient)</li>
<li><strong>Mineralocorticoid replacement</strong> -- <strong>fludrocortisone 0.05-0.2 mg/day</strong> (typically 0.1 mg). Dose guided by blood pressure, electrolytes, and plasma renin activity (target: high-normal renin)</li>
<li><strong>DHEA replacement</strong> -- <strong>25-50 mg/day</strong> in selected women (improves well-being, libido, mood; not recommended routinely for men; limited evidence)</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring</strong> -- clinical (blood pressure, weight, energy, electrolytes); no direct cortisol monitoring (hydrocortisone dose is clinical). Avoid <strong>over-replacement</strong> (causes Cushingoid features, osteoporosis, glucose intolerance)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Stress-dose steroids (CRITICAL -- prevents adrenal crisis):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Minor illness</strong> (fever, URI, gastroenteritis) -- <strong>double or triple</strong> the usual oral hydrocortisone dose for 24-48 hours</li>
<li><strong>Major stress</strong> (surgery, severe trauma, sepsis) -- <strong>hydrocortisone 100 mg IV</strong>, then 50 mg IV every 6-8 hours until stable; taper as patient recovers</li>
<li><strong>Vomiting or inability to take oral meds</strong> -- <strong>parenteral hydrocortisone</strong> (IM or IV); <strong>emergency hydrocortisone injection kit</strong> (Solu-Cortef Act-O-Vial 100 mg IM) at home; <strong>family trained to administer</strong></li>
<li><strong>Dental procedures</strong> -- hydrocortisone 50 mg orally or IV on the day of the procedure</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Patient education and emergency preparedness:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Medical alert bracelet or necklace</strong> stating &quot;Adrenal insufficiency -- needs hydrocortisone&quot; (mandatory)</li>
<li><strong>Emergency hydrocortisone injection kit</strong> at home, work, and when traveling; family, friends, and coworkers trained to administer IM injection</li>
<li><strong>Steroid emergency card</strong> in wallet</li>
<li><strong>Education on &quot;sick day rules&quot;</strong> -- double dose for illness; seek emergency care for vomiting, trauma, or severe illness</li>
<li><strong>Travel</strong> -- carry medication in carry-on; extra supply; medical letter for customs</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Screening for associated conditions:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 2</strong> -- annual screening for <strong>type 1 diabetes</strong> (HbA1c, fasting glucose), <strong>autoimmune thyroid disease</strong> (TSH, anti-TPO), <strong>pernicious anemia</strong> (B12), <strong>vitiligo</strong>, <strong>celiac disease</strong> (tTG-IgA)</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 1 (APECED)</strong> -- if AIRE mutation: screen for <strong>hypoparathyroidism</strong> (calcium, PTH) and <strong>chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive and supportive therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin D and calcium</strong> -- glucocorticoid replacement increases osteoporosis risk; DEXA every 2-3 years; bisphosphonate if osteoporotic</li>
<li><strong>Salt</strong> -- liberal salt intake; salt tablets in hot climates or during exercise</li>
<li><strong>Avoid excessive alcohol</strong> (precipitates crisis)</li>
<li><strong>Treat comorbid autoimmune conditions</strong> appropriately</li>
</ul>
