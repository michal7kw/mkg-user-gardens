<h1>Lupus Nephritis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Lupus nephritis (LN) is inflammation of the kidney caused by systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and is among the most consequential of the disease's organ manifestations. It is fundamentally an <strong>immune-complex glomerulonephritis</strong>: in SLE, loss of self-tolerance generates autoantibodies - particularly against double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) and nucleosomes - that form circulating and in-situ immune complexes. When these complexes deposit in the glomerular mesangium, subendothelial space, or subepithelial space, they activate complement and recruit inflammatory cells, producing the spectrum of glomerular injury that defines the disease.</p>
<p>The clinical and prognostic heterogeneity of LN is captured by the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) classification, which grades renal biopsy findings from class I (minimal mesangial) and class II (mesangial proliferative) through the inflammatory class III (focal) and class IV (diffuse) proliferative forms, class V (membranous), and class VI (advanced sclerosing). The location of immune deposits largely determines the pattern: subendothelial deposits drive proliferative, inflammatory disease with active urinary sediment, whereas subepithelial deposits produce membranous disease with heavy proteinuria. Proliferative classes (III and IV) carry the greatest risk of progressive renal failure and require the most intensive immunosuppression.</p>
<p>LN matters because it is a leading determinant of morbidity, mortality, and progression to end-stage kidney disease in people with lupus, and because it is frequently <strong>clinically silent early</strong> - detectable only through screening urinalysis and serology before symptoms appear. This makes regular monitoring of urine protein, sediment, anti-dsDNA titers, and complement levels central to lupus care, and renal biopsy the indispensable tool for accurate classification and treatment selection.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>LN often develops insidiously and may be asymptomatic until advanced, underscoring the importance of routine screening:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Renal</strong>: proteinuria (from low-grade to nephrotic-range), microscopic hematuria, cellular/red-cell casts on an active urinary sediment, and declining kidney function with rising creatinine</li>
<li><strong>Volume/pressure</strong>: peripheral and periorbital edema, weight gain, and new or worsening hypertension</li>
<li><strong>Systemic lupus features</strong>: malar rash, photosensitivity, arthralgia, fatigue, oral ulcers, and serositis that accompany active disease</li>
<li><strong>Nephrotic presentation</strong>: heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, and marked edema, most typical of class V (membranous) disease</li>
</ul>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis requires established (or concurrently diagnosed) SLE plus evidence of renal involvement - persistent proteinuria of at least 0.5 g/day (or an equivalent protein:creatinine ratio) and/or an active urinary sediment. <strong>Renal biopsy is the gold standard</strong>, providing the ISN/RPS class, activity and chronicity indices, and guidance for therapy; the 2018 revision refined definitions of activity/chronicity and removed the subdivision of class IV. Serology is supportive and useful for monitoring: positive <strong>anti-dsDNA</strong> antibodies and low serum <strong>complement (C3 and C4)</strong> reflect active immune-complex disease, with rising anti-dsDNA and falling complement often heralding a flare. Other glomerular diseases must be excluded.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The necessary precondition is SLE itself. Within lupus populations, higher renal risk is associated with younger age at disease onset, male sex, and ancestry (African, Hispanic, and Asian patients have higher LN incidence and worse outcomes). Serologic markers of risk include persistently high anti-dsDNA titers and sustained hypocomplementemia. Genetic predisposition, as well as socioeconomic factors influencing access to care and treatment adherence, further modify the risk of progression.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Treatment is staged into induction and maintenance, targeting proliferative (class III/IV) and significant class V disease most aggressively. <strong>Induction</strong> combines glucocorticoids with either mycophenolate mofetil or low-dose (&quot;Euro-Lupus&quot;) cyclophosphamide; contemporary regimens increasingly add a second agent - belimumab (anti-BAFF) or voclosporin (a calcineurin inhibitor) - to improve renal response. <strong>Maintenance</strong> uses mycophenolate or azathioprine to consolidate remission and prevent flares. <strong>Hydroxychloroquine</strong> is recommended for essentially all lupus patients because it reduces flares and offers renoprotective and survival benefits. Supportive care - renin-angiotensin system blockade for proteinuria, strict blood-pressure control, and aggressive cardiovascular risk reduction - is integral.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The principal complication is progression to chronic kidney disease and, in a substantial minority, end-stage kidney disease requiring dialysis or transplantation. Nephrotic-range disease predisposes to thromboembolism and severe edema. Hypertension and accelerated atherosclerosis substantially raise cardiovascular risk, which is a leading cause of death in lupus. Renal flares recur over time, and the immunosuppression required carries risks of serious infection and drug toxicity.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Outcomes have improved markedly with modern immunosuppression, but LN remains a major adverse prognostic factor in SLE. Achieving a complete renal response within the first year strongly predicts long-term kidney survival. Prognosis is worse with proliferative (class IV) disease, high chronicity indices on biopsy, delayed treatment, persistent proteinuria, and impaired baseline kidney function; flares and incomplete responses drive cumulative nephron loss.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Renal involvement affects a large fraction of people with SLE - on the order of 30-60% over the disease course, with higher rates in those diagnosed young and in non-European populations. Because SLE itself predominantly affects women of reproductive age, LN shares that demographic skew, though male patients tend to have more severe renal disease.</p>
