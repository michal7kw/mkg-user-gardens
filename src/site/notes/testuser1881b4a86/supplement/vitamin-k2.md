<h1>Vitamin K2</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin K2 refers to a family of fat-soluble compounds called menaquinones (MK-n), distinguished by the length of their isoprenoid side chain. The two principal supplemental forms are <strong>MK-4</strong> (menatetrenone; animal-derived or synthetic) and <strong>MK-7</strong> (bacterial-fermented, most commonly from natto -- fermented soybeans). All menaquinones function as essential cofactors for the enzyme gamma-glutamyl carboxylase, which activates vitamin K-dependent Gla proteins -- most notably <strong>osteocalcin</strong> (recruits calcium into bone hydroxyapatite) and <strong>matrix Gla protein (MGP)</strong> (the strongest known inhibitor of arterial calcification).</p>
<p>The clinical distinction between vitamins K1 (phylloquinone, from leafy greens) and K2 (menaquinones, from fermented foods and animal products) is mechanistic and clinically important: K1 is preferentially trafficked to the liver where it activates clotting factors, while K2 is transported extrahepatically to bone, vasculature, and other tissues. Consequently, K1 adequacy prevents bleeding but does not protect bone or arteries, whereas K2 adequacy supports extraskeletal calcium regulation. This distinction is reflected in observational epidemiology: the Rotterdam Study (Geleijnse 2004) and EPIC-NL cohort (Beulens 2009) found that high dietary menaquinone intake (but not phylloquinone) was associated with substantially reduced coronary artery calcification and cardiovascular events.</p>
<p>Vitamin K2 is most clinically relevant for two populations: (1) postmenopausal women and others at risk for osteoporosis, and (2) patients with chronic kidney disease, diabetes, or established atherosclerosis where arterial calcification is actively progressing. Supplementation is often paired synergistically with vitamin D, which upregulates osteocalcin expression (creating the substrate that K2 then activates).</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (multiple observational cohorts + RCTs; mechanistic plausibility strong; large outcome trials ongoing).</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Vitamin K2 is the reduced hydroquinone form (KH2) that serves as a cofactor for gamma-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX) in the endoplasmic reticulum. The enzyme carboxylates specific glutamate residues on vitamin K-dependent proteins to form gamma-carboxyglutamate (Gla), which chelates calcium ions and allows the protein to interact with hydroxyapatite or calcium phosphate crystals. After carboxylation, K2 is oxidized to K2-epoxide and must be recycled by vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1) back to KH2 -- the same enzyme warfarin inhibits, which is why K2 supplementation antagonizes warfarin therapy.</p>
<p><strong>Extrahepatic vitamin K-dependent proteins:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Osteocalcin (bone Gla protein):</strong> secreted by osteoblasts; carboxylated osteocalcin (ucOC -&gt; cOC) binds hydroxyapatite and is essential for bone mineralization. Undercarboxylated osteocalcin (ucOC) is a sensitive marker of K2 deficiency</li>
<li><strong>Matrix Gla protein (MGP):</strong> synthesized by vascular smooth muscle cells and chondrocytes; the most potent inhibitor of vascular calcification known. MGP knockout mice die from massive arterial calcification within 2 months. Undercarboxylated MGP (ucMGP) is elevated in chronic kidney disease and correlates with arterial calcification burden</li>
<li><strong>Growth arrest specific protein 6 (Gas6):</strong> involved in cell survival, apoptosis regulation, and platelet stabilization</li>
<li><strong>Periostin:</strong> involved in bone remodeling and cardiac valve calcification regulation</li>
<li><strong>Kidney Gla protein, osteopontin:</strong> other less characterized targets</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>K1 vs K2 distinction:</strong> K1 (phylloquinone) is preferentially retained by the liver (where it activates clotting factors II, VII, IX, X, protein C, protein S) and cleared rapidly from plasma. K2 (especially MK-7) circulates longer (half-life ~72 h for MK-7), reaches extrahepatic tissues at meaningful concentrations, and is the principal activator of osteocalcin and MGP.</p>
<h2>Research Summary</h2>
<h3>Cardiovascular Disease (Arterial Calcification)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rotterdam Study</strong> (Geleijnse 2004, n=4807): high menaquinone intake associated with 50% reduction in severe aortic calcification and 57% reduction in CHD mortality. Phylloquinone intake showed no association.</li>
<li><strong>EPIC-NL cohort</strong> (Beulens 2009, n=16,057): each 10-mcg/day increase in menaquinone intake associated with 9% reduction in CHD events.</li>
<li><strong>PREDIMED sub-study</strong> (Juanola-Falgarona 2014): higher phylloquinone intake associated with <em>lower</em> type 2 diabetes risk; menaquinone trended similarly.</li>
<li><strong>MK-7 RCT</strong> (Knapen 2013, healthy postmenopausal women, 180 mcg/day x 3 yr): slowed age-related decline in arterial elasticity (dc-SBP, dc-DBP); significantly reduced ucOC and ucMGP. No adverse effect on clotting.</li>
<li><strong>Mansour 2017 RCT</strong> (MK-7 360 mcg/day in hemodialysis patients, 8 weeks): trend toward slowed progression of coronary artery calcification (underpowered).</li>
<li><strong>Shea &amp; Booth 2016 review</strong> (Adv Nutr): concludes K2 has biological plausibility for CVD prevention; awaits definitive outcome trials.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Bone Health (Osteoporosis)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cockayne 2006 meta-analysis</strong> (Arch Intern Med, 13 RCTs): vitamin K supplementation improved BMD at lumbar spine; reduced undercarboxylated osteocalcin.</li>
<li><strong>Knapen 2013 RCT</strong> (MK-7 180 mcg/day x 3 yr in 244 postmenopausal women): inhibited age-related decline in BMD at lumbar spine and femoral neck; improved bone strength indices.</li>
<li><strong>Japanese menatetrenone (MK-4) trials</strong> (45 mg/day pharmacologic dose): reduced fracture incidence in postmenopausal osteoporosis; approved as Glakay in Japan.</li>
<li><strong>Emaus 2016 RCT</strong> (MK-7 360 mcg/week x 12 mo in 296 Norwegian postmenopausal women): favorable but modest bone effects.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Type 2 Diabetes / Insulin Sensitivity</h3>
<ul>
<li>** Choi 2011 RCT** (K1 1000 mcg/day x 36 mo in non-diabetic men): improved insulin sensitivity and reduced diabetes progression -- suggests K vitamins (regardless of K1 vs K2) may benefit glycemic control.</li>
<li><strong>Yoshiuchi 2009</strong>: menaquinone and beta-cell function signal.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Administration Guide</h2>
<h3>Dosage</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>MK-7 (preferred):</strong> 100-200 mcg once daily (highest bioavailability, longest half-life)</li>
<li><strong>MK-4:</strong> 1000-5000 mcg (1-5 mg) daily in divided doses (short half-life)</li>
<li><strong>Pharmacologic MK-4 (menatetrenone):</strong> 45 mg/day (used in Japanese osteoporosis pharmacotherapy; not available in US/EU as a drug)</li>
<li><strong>Children:</strong> 45-100 mcg MK-7 daily; do not use pharmacologic MK-4 doses</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> Food and Nutrition Board adequate intake (75-90 mcg/day total vitamin K) -- no separate K2 recommendations</li>
<li><strong>Anticoagulant patients:</strong> do not supplement without physician coordination; warfarin doses may need adjustment</li>
</ul>
<h3>Timing and Food</h3>
<ul>
<li>Take with the largest fat-containing meal of the day for maximal absorption (fat-soluble)</li>
<li>Morning or evening acceptable (MK-7 long half-life makes timing flexible)</li>
<li>Pair with vitamin D3 (1000-5000 IU) and a calcium source for synergistic bone benefit</li>
<li>Avoid simultaneous ingestion with olestra, orlistat, or bile acid sequestrants</li>
</ul>
<h3>Form Selection</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>MK-7 (recommended):</strong> 100-200 mcg dose, 72-hour half-life, sustained tissue levels, once-daily dosing. Look for MenaQ7-branded ingredient (clinically studied)</li>
<li><strong>MK-4:</strong> Use only if specifically targeting Japanese osteoporosis protocol; otherwise MK-7 is preferred</li>
<li><strong>Combination products:</strong> K2 + D3 combinations are convenient and cost-effective</li>
<li><strong>Avoid K1-only products</strong> for bone/cardiovascular goals (preferentially used for clotting)</li>
</ul>
<h3>Duration</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Undercarboxylated osteocalcin reduction:</strong> within 7-14 days</li>
<li><strong>Bone mineral density changes:</strong> 12-36 months for measurable change</li>
<li><strong>Arterial calcification slowing:</strong> 2-3 years for measurable difference</li>
<li><strong>Long-term use:</strong> safe for continuous use; monitor INR if on anticoagulants</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<h3>Contraindications</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Warfarin or other VKA anticoagulants:</strong> K2 supplementation antagonizes VKA mechanism by restoring VK cycle activity. Coordinate with prescribing physician; INR monitoring required. DO NOT initiate without physician oversight if on warfarin</li>
<li><strong>Severe hepatic impairment:</strong> impaired vitamin K cycling; risk of accumulation</li>
<li><strong>Hypersensitivity</strong> to product ingredients</li>
</ul>

<h3>Pregnancy and Lactation</h3>
<p>Adequate intake (AI) for total vitamin K is 75-90 mcg/day. No evidence of harm at typical supplemental K2 doses. Avoid pharmacologic MK-4 doses (45 mg) in pregnancy.</p>
<h2>Quality Considerations</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Form verification:</strong> label should specify MK-7 or MK-4 (or both); avoid products that say only &quot;vitamin K complex&quot;</li>
<li><strong>MenaQ7 brand:</strong> clinically studied MK-7 with published trial data</li>
<li><strong>All-trans isomer:</strong> bioactive form; avoid cis-isomer (synthetic byproduct)</li>
<li><strong>Third-party testing:</strong> look for USP, NSF, or ConsumerLab certification</li>
<li><strong>Source:</strong> natto-derived MK-7 is traditional and well-characterized</li>
<li><strong>Synergistic combinations:</strong> K2 + D3 products are convenient</li>
<li><strong>Avoid products with:</strong> artificial colors, fillers, or oxidized K2 (check expiration)</li>
</ul>
