<h1>Pre-Eclampsia</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Pre-eclampsia is a <strong>multisystem disorder of pregnancy</strong> characterized by new-onset hypertension (&gt;=140/90 mmHg) after 20 weeks of gestation, accompanied by evidence of end-organ dysfunction. It affects <strong>2-8% of pregnancies</strong> worldwide and is a leading cause of maternal and perinatal mortality, responsible for an estimated 76,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 infant deaths annually.</p>
<p>The pathogenesis involves <strong>abnormal placentation</strong> -- inadequate trophoblastic invasion of the spiral arteries in early pregnancy, leading to reduced uteroplacental perfusion. This triggers the release of <strong>anti-angiogenic factors</strong> (sFlt-1, soluble endoglin) into the maternal circulation, which bind and neutralize pro-angiogenic factors (VEGF, PlGF), causing widespread endothelial dysfunction. The result is hypertension, proteinuria, and multi-organ involvement (brain, liver, kidneys, lungs, hematologic system).</p>
<p>Pre-eclampsia is classified as <strong>pre-eclampsia with severe features</strong> (BP &gt;=160/110, thrombocytopenia &lt;100K, impaired liver function, renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, or new-onset headache/visual disturbances) or <strong>pre-eclampsia without severe features</strong>. The progression from pre-eclampsia to <strong>eclampsia</strong> (seizures) is unpredictable and can occur rapidly.</p>
<p><strong>Definitive treatment is delivery</strong> of the fetus and placenta, but the timing depends on gestational age, severity, and maternal-fetal status. <strong>Magnesium sulfate</strong> is the standard for seizure prophylaxis and treatment. <strong>Low-dose aspirin</strong> (75-150 mg, started before 16 weeks) reduces pre-eclampsia risk by ~50% in high-risk women.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p><strong>Diagnostic criteria (ACOG):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Blood pressure &gt;=140/90 mmHg</strong> on two occasions at least 4 hours apart, after 20 weeks gestation, in a woman with previously normal BP</li>
<li><strong>AND one or more of:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Proteinuria (&gt;=300 mg/24h or dipstick &gt;=2+ or PCR &gt;=0.3)</li>
<li>Thrombocytopenia (&lt;100,000/uL)</li>
<li>Renal insufficiency (creatinine &gt;1.1 mg/dL or doubling of baseline)</li>
<li>Impaired liver function (AST/ALT &gt;2x ULN)</li>
<li>Pulmonary edema</li>
<li>New-onset headache unresponsive to medication</li>
<li>Visual disturbances (scotoma, blurred vision)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Severe features:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>BP &gt;=160/110 mmHg (severe range)</li>
<li>Any of the end-organ criteria above</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Symptoms of severe pre-eclampsia / impending eclampsia:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Persistent severe headache</li>
<li>Visual changes (scotoma, blurred vision, photophobia)</li>
<li>Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain (liver capsule stretch)</li>
<li>Nausea, vomiting (late sign)</li>
<li>Altered mental status</li>
<li>Decreased urine output</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Blood pressure control (severe range &gt;=160/110):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>IV labetalol</strong> 10-20 mg, repeat q10min (max 300 mg)</li>
<li><strong>IV hydralazine</strong> 5-10 mg, repeat q20min (max 30 mg)</li>
<li><strong>Oral nifedipine</strong> 10 mg, repeat in 20 min (max 30 mg)</li>
<li>Target: 140-150/90-100 mmHg (avoid over-correction)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Seizure prophylaxis (magnesium sulfate):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Loading: 4-6 g IV over 20 min</li>
<li>Maintenance: 1-2 g/hr continuous infusion</li>
<li>Continue for 24 hours postpartum</li>
<li>Monitor for toxicity: deep tendon reflexes, respiratory rate, urine output</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Timing of delivery (ACOG):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Pre-eclampsia with severe features: deliver at <strong>34 weeks</strong> (or earlier if maternal/fetal deterioration)</li>
<li>Pre-eclampsia without severe features: deliver at <strong>37 weeks</strong></li>
<li>If &lt;34 weeks and stable: expectant management at a tertiary center with monitoring</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Postpartum:</strong> BP typically normalizes within 48 hours but can persist or worsen for up to 6 weeks; monitor closely</p>
