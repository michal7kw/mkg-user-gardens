<h1>Reverse T3</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Reverse T3 (rT3) is a biologically inactive isomer of triiodothyronine generated when the body deiodinates thyroxine (T4) at the inner (tyrosyl) ring rather than the outer ring that yields active T3. Under normal conditions, a relatively fixed proportion of T4 is shunted toward rT3, but this balance shifts dramatically during physiological stress. Because rT3 cannot bind the nuclear thyroid hormone receptor to drive metabolism, rising rT3 represents a hormone-conservation response that lowers metabolic demand.</p>
<p>Elevated rT3 is the metabolic signature of non-thyroidal illness syndrome (also called euthyroid sick syndrome), in which acute or chronic illness, sepsis, surgery, trauma, starvation, or caloric restriction suppress the type 1 deiodinase that converts T4 to active T3 while favoring conversion to rT3. Chronic psychological stress, elevated cortisol, systemic inflammation, and selenium deficiency (selenium is a cofactor for deiodinase enzymes) can all increase rT3. The marker is therefore most useful as a context-dependent indicator of impaired peripheral thyroid hormone conversion and utilization rather than as a standalone diagnosis.</p>
<p>Interpretation should always be paired with TSH, Free T3, and Free T4. A common clinical pattern is normal or low-normal TSH with low Free T3 and elevated rT3, suggesting the body is preferentially inactivating thyroid hormone. Low rT3 is uncommon and generally not clinically significant, though it may accompany hyperthyroidism or vigorous T4-to-T3 conversion.</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase rT3:</strong> acute or chronic illness and non-thyroidal illness syndrome, severe caloric restriction or fasting, high cortisol and chronic stress, systemic inflammation, surgery/trauma, selenium deficiency, certain medications (beta-blockers, glucocorticoids, amiodarone).</p>
<p><strong>Decrease rT3:</strong> hyperthyroidism, robust peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion, recovery from acute illness.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Reverse T3 helps distinguish true thyroid gland dysfunction from peripheral conversion problems. When TSH and T4 are within range but the patient is symptomatically hypothyroid with low Free T3, an elevated rT3 supports impaired conversion driven by illness, stress, or nutrient deficiency rather than primary thyroid failure. The rT3 value (and the Free T3 to rT3 ratio used by some clinicians) is a research- and practice-informed adjunct; it is not a substitute for the core TSH, Free T3, and Free T4 panel and should be interpreted within the overall clinical picture.</p>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>Because elevated rT3 usually reflects an underlying stressor, management targets the root cause: treating acute illness, restoring adequate caloric and protein intake, improving sleep, and reducing chronic physiological and psychological stress. Correcting selenium deficiency supports deiodinase function, and adaptogenic support (e.g., ashwagandha) may help modulate the stress axis. Adequate iron status is also relevant to thyroid hormone metabolism. Any supplementation should be guided by testing and a clinician.</p>
