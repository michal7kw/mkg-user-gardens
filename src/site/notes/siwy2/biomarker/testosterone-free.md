<h1>Testosterone Free</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Free testosterone is the fraction of total circulating testosterone that is not bound to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) or albumin and is therefore immediately available to diffuse into target cells and exert androgenic effects. Although it represents only roughly 1-3% of total testosterone, this unbound pool—together with the loosely albumin-bound fraction (the &quot;bioavailable&quot; testosterone)—is the most physiologically meaningful measure of androgen action.</p>
<p>Free testosterone is especially informative when SHBG concentrations are abnormal. Conditions that lower SHBG (insulin resistance, obesity, hypothyroidism) or raise it (aging, hyperthyroidism, estrogen excess, liver disease) can make total testosterone misleading; in these settings free testosterone better reflects true androgen status. For this reason guidelines recommend assessing free or calculated free testosterone when total values are borderline or when clinical suspicion of hypogonadism persists despite a normal total.</p>
<p>Measurement methodology matters. Equilibrium dialysis is the reference standard, while calculated free testosterone (derived from total testosterone, SHBG, and albumin) is widely used and generally reliable. Direct analog immunoassays are considered less accurate. Because of diurnal variation, samples should be collected in the morning, and abnormal results should be confirmed on a repeat fasting morning draw.</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Increase</strong>: PCOS and other hyperandrogenic states (in women), anabolic-androgenic steroid use, adrenal or testicular tumors, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, low-SHBG states (insulin resistance, obesity).</li>
<li><strong>Decrease</strong>: primary or secondary hypogonadism, aging, pituitary dysfunction, chronic illness, obesity, opioids, glucocorticoids, and high-SHBG states that increase bound (and reduce free) testosterone.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Free testosterone is a cornerstone of androgen assessment. In men, a low free testosterone with consistent symptoms (low libido, fatigue, reduced muscle mass, erectile dysfunction) supports a diagnosis of hypogonadism and may justify treatment, even when total testosterone appears normal due to high SHBG. In women, elevated free testosterone is a key biochemical feature of PCOS and other hyperandrogenic disorders. Because the free fraction is sensitive to SHBG, it should be interpreted alongside total testosterone and SHBG.</p>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>For men with functional or obesity-related declines in free testosterone, weight loss, resistance training, adequate sleep, and correction of micronutrient deficiencies (e.g., vitamin D, magnesium, zinc) can improve androgen status and—by lowering hepatic fat and insulin—shift the free fraction favorably. Certain botanical supplements have been studied for their effects on testosterone and stress-mediated suppression. Confirmed pathological hypogonadism should be evaluated by a clinician, as testosterone replacement carries specific indications and monitoring requirements.</p>
