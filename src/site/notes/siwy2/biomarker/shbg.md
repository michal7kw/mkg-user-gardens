<h1>SHBG</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) is a glycoprotein synthesized primarily by the liver that binds circulating sex steroids—chiefly testosterone and estradiol—with high affinity. Because hormone bound to SHBG is largely unavailable to tissues, SHBG acts as a regulator of the bioactive (free) fraction: a rise in SHBG lowers free hormone availability, while a fall raises it. This makes SHBG an essential interpretive companion to total testosterone and estradiol measurements, particularly when calculating free or bioavailable hormone.</p>
<p>SHBG concentrations are dynamically controlled by metabolic and hormonal signals. Insulin and hepatic fat suppress SHBG synthesis, so low SHBG commonly accompanies insulin resistance, obesity, and metabolic syndrome and can serve as an early metabolic marker. Conversely, estrogen excess, hyperthyroidism, aging, and liver disease raise SHBG. Thyroid hormone and oral estrogens (including oral contraceptives) are notable upward drivers.</p>
<p>Clinically, SHBG helps explain discordances between symptoms and total hormone levels. A man with a &quot;normal&quot; total testosterone but high SHBG may still be functionally androgen-deficient because his free testosterone is low; a woman with low SHBG may have androgen-excess features (as in PCOS) despite an unremarkable total testosterone. SHBG therefore sharpens the assessment of hypogonadism, PCOS, and metabolic risk.</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Increase</strong>: hyperthyroidism, hepatic disease, estrogen excess (including oral estrogens), aging, hypogonadism, anorexia/low body weight.</li>
<li><strong>Decrease</strong>: insulin resistance, obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypothyroidism, PCOS, hyperandrogenism, anabolic-androgenic steroid use, glucocorticoid excess.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>SHBG is most valuable as a modifier of free-hormone status rather than as a standalone diagnostic. Low SHBG is a recognized marker of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome and predicts adverse cardiometabolic outcomes, while high SHBG can mask functional androgen deficiency by lowering free testosterone despite normal totals. In women, low SHBG amplifies free androgen exposure and contributes to the hyperandrogenic phenotype of PCOS. Accurate interpretation of testosterone and estradiol therefore generally requires a concurrent SHBG measurement.</p>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>Because SHBG largely tracks metabolic and hormonal context, the most evidence-based way to normalize a pathologically low SHBG is to improve insulin sensitivity—through weight loss, reduced hepatic fat, exercise, and dietary improvement—rather than targeting SHBG directly. Treating an underlying thyroid disorder or rationalizing exogenous estrogen/steroid exposure can correct abnormally high or low values. SHBG itself is rarely a treatment target; the goal is to address the upstream driver.</p>
