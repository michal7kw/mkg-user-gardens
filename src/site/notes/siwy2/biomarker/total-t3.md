<h1>Total T3</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Total T3 quantifies the entire circulating pool of triiodothyronine, the most metabolically active thyroid hormone, encompassing both the small free fraction and the large fraction bound to carrier proteins such as thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), albumin, and transthyretin. Although the thyroid gland secretes mostly T4, roughly 80% of circulating T3 is produced peripherally by deiodination of T4 in the liver, kidney, and other tissues. T3 binds nuclear thyroid hormone receptors with high affinity to regulate basal metabolic rate, thermogenesis, cardiac output, and protein turnover.</p>
<p>Elevated Total T3 most often indicates hyperthyroidism, including Graves' disease, toxic nodular goiter, and the T3-predominant thyrotoxicosis sometimes seen with iodine excess; it can also arise from excessive thyroid hormone supplementation. Low Total T3 may reflect hypothyroidism, but it is very commonly reduced in euthyroid sick syndrome (non-thyroidal illness), malnutrition, and chronic disease, where T4-to-T3 conversion is suppressed. Because more than 99% of T3 is protein-bound, total T3 results are sensitive to changes in binding-protein concentration—pregnancy and estrogen raise TBG (and thus total T3) without affecting the active free hormone.</p>
<p>For this reason Total T3 is interpreted in conjunction with Free T3, Free T4, and TSH. Total T3 is particularly valuable in confirming and characterizing hyperthyroidism, where it may be disproportionately elevated relative to T4 (T3 toxicosis), and in monitoring response to antithyroid therapy.</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Total T3:</strong> hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease, toxic nodular goiter), T3 thyrotoxicosis, excessive thyroid hormone or iodine intake, pregnancy and estrogen therapy (via increased TBG), adequate selenium and iodine status supporting conversion.</p>
<p><strong>Decrease Total T3:</strong> hypothyroidism, euthyroid sick syndrome / non-thyroidal illness, malnutrition and severe caloric restriction, chronic illness, selenium deficiency impairing deiodinase activity, conditions lowering binding proteins (nephrotic syndrome, androgens).</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Total T3 is a key marker for diagnosing and grading hyperthyroidism and is essential for detecting T3-predominant thyrotoxicosis, in which Free T4 may still be normal. It is less reliable for diagnosing hypothyroidism because T3 is preferentially preserved until disease is advanced, and because non-thyroidal illness can lower it independent of thyroid status. Clinicians weigh total T3 against binding-protein status; when binding-protein interference is suspected, Free T3 provides a more accurate picture of active hormone.</p>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>Management depends on direction and cause. For low T3 driven by impaired conversion, addressing the underlying illness, ensuring adequate protein and calories, and correcting selenium and iodine deficiencies (selenium is a deiodinase cofactor; iodine is a hormone substrate) support healthy T4-to-T3 conversion. Adaptogenic support such as ashwagandha may help when chronic stress is a contributor. Elevated T3 from hyperthyroidism requires medical evaluation and treatment, not supplementation. All nutrient interventions should be guided by testing and a clinician, since excess iodine can worsen some thyroid conditions.</p>
