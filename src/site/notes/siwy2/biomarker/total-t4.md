<h1>Total T4</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Total T4 measures the complete serum pool of thyroxine, the principal hormone produced and secreted by the thyroid gland, including both the protein-bound majority and the small free (active-precursor) fraction. T4 is synthesized by iodination of tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin and functions largely as a prohormone: it is transported to peripheral tissues where deiodinase enzymes convert it to the more active T3. As such, total T4 is a broad indicator of the gland's secretory output and overall thyroid status.</p>
<p>More than 99% of circulating T4 is bound to carrier proteins, chiefly thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), with smaller contributions from transthyretin and albumin. This high degree of binding means total T4 is sensitive to changes in binding-protein concentration: pregnancy, estrogen therapy, and oral contraceptives raise TBG and therefore total T4 without changing the metabolically relevant free hormone, whereas androgens, nephrotic syndrome, and severe illness can lower it. Modern immunoassays for total and free T4 are standardized, but binding-protein interference remains an important interpretive consideration.</p>
<p>Elevated Total T4 suggests hyperthyroidism or excessive levothyroxine dosing, while low Total T4 indicates hypothyroidism, pituitary (central) dysfunction, or euthyroid sick syndrome. Because of binding-protein effects, total T4 is best interpreted with Free T4 and TSH; the combination distinguishes true thyroid dysfunction from binding-protein artifacts.</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Total T4:</strong> hyperthyroidism, excessive levothyroxine dosing, pregnancy, estrogen therapy and oral contraceptives (increased TBG), acute hepatitis, familial dysalbuminemic hyperthyroxinemia.</p>
<p><strong>Decrease Total T4:</strong> hypothyroidism, pituitary or hypothalamic (central) dysfunction, euthyroid sick syndrome, androgens or anabolic steroids and nephrotic syndrome (decreased binding proteins), severe iodine or selenium deficiency impairing synthesis.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Total T4 contributes to the diagnosis of hyper- and hypothyroidism and to monitoring levothyroxine replacement. Its main limitation is dependence on binding-protein status, which can produce abnormal values in euthyroid individuals (for example, the elevated total T4 of normal pregnancy). When discordance between total T4 and clinical picture or TSH arises, Free T4 clarifies whether the active hormone is truly abnormal. Together with TSH, total/free T4 helps localize the level of any dysfunction—primary thyroid versus central/pituitary.</p>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>For low T4 from inadequate thyroid synthesis, ensuring sufficient iodine (the substrate for T4) and selenium (supporting thyroid peroxidase and deiodinase function) supports hormone production, while overt hypothyroidism is treated with levothyroxine under medical supervision. Chronic stress reduction, with adaptogenic support such as ashwagandha, may modestly support thyroid axis function. Elevated T4 from hyperthyroidism requires medical treatment rather than supplementation. Because excess iodine can precipitate or worsen thyroid disease, all nutrient interventions should be guided by testing and a clinician.</p>
