<h1>Bone Mineral Density</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Bone mineral density (BMD) quantifies the amount of mineral content — chiefly calcium hydroxyapatite — per unit area of bone, expressed in grams per square centimeter (g/cm²). It is the principal measurable surrogate for bone strength and is the central biomarker used to diagnose osteoporosis and to estimate fracture risk. BMD is most often assessed by <strong>dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA/DXA)</strong>, the clinical gold standard, typically measured at the <strong>lumbar spine</strong> and <strong>proximal femur (hip)</strong>.</p>
<p>Results are reported primarily as standardized scores. The <strong>T-score</strong> compares the patient's BMD to the mean of a young healthy adult reference population (in standard deviations) and is the basis for diagnostic thresholds in postmenopausal women and older men. The <strong>Z-score</strong> compares BMD to an age-, sex-, and sometimes ethnicity-matched reference and is preferred in children, premenopausal women, and younger men, where a low value prompts a search for secondary causes.</p>
<p>BMD is a strong, continuous predictor of fragility fracture — each standard-deviation decrease roughly doubles fracture risk — and is widely used both to make the diagnosis of osteoporosis and to monitor response to anti-osteoporotic therapy over time.</p>

<ul>
<li>WHO T-score criteria apply principally to <strong>postmenopausal women and men &gt;= 50 years</strong>.</li>
<li>In premenopausal women, men &lt; 50, and children, the <strong>Z-score</strong> is used; Z-score &lt;= -2.0 is &quot;below the expected range for age.&quot;</li>
<li>DEXA reports also include absolute BMD (g/cm²) for serial monitoring of treatment response.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Interpretation</h2>
<p><strong>Low BMD:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Postmenopausal estrogen deficiency (most common cause in women)</li>
<li>Age-related bone loss; male hypogonadism</li>
<li>Secondary causes: glucocorticoid therapy, hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, hypogonadism, malabsorption, chronic kidney disease</li>
<li>Vitamin D deficiency and inadequate calcium intake</li>
<li>Clinical consequence: fragility fractures (vertebral, hip, wrist)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pitfalls of elevated/falsely high BMD:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Lumbar spine osteophytes, facet arthropathy, and aortic calcification can spuriously raise spine BMD in older adults — hip measurement is often more reliable in this setting</li>
</ul>
