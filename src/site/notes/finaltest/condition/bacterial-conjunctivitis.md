<h1>Bacterial Conjunctivitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Bacterial conjunctivitis is an infection of the conjunctiva caused by bacteria, presenting with <strong>purulent (yellow-green) discharge</strong>, conjunctival redness, and eyelash crusting. It accounts for approximately <strong>50% of all acute conjunctivitis cases</strong> in adults (the remainder being mostly viral). Unlike viral conjunctivitis (which produces watery discharge and is highly contagious), bacterial conjunctivitis produces <strong>thick, mucopurulent discharge</strong> and eyelids often stick together upon waking.</p>
<p><strong>Common pathogens:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Staphylococcus aureus</strong> (most common in adults)</li>
<li><strong>Streptococcus pneumoniae</strong></li>
<li><strong>Haemophilus influenzae</strong> (especially in children)</li>
<li><strong>Moraxella catarrhalis</strong> (children)</li>
<li><strong>Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia</strong> (contact lens users -- consider keratitis!)</li>
<li><strong>Neisseria gonorrhoeae / Chlamydia trachomatis</strong> (neonatal and sexually active adults; hyperacute, severe)</li>
</ul>
<p>Most cases are <strong>self-limiting</strong> (resolving in 7-10 days without treatment), but <strong>topical antibiotics</strong> speed resolution by 1-2 days, reduce transmission, and prevent complications. Hyperacute bacterial conjunctivitis (copious purulent discharge, rapid progression, chemosis) -- especially with <em>Neisseria</em> -- requires <strong>systemic antibiotics</strong> and urgent ophthalmology referral to prevent corneal perforation.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p><strong>Typical bacterial conjunctivitis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acute onset</strong> redness (usually starting unilateral, may spread to both eyes within 1-2 days)</li>
<li><strong>Thick, yellow-green or white purulent discharge</strong> (hallmark; distinguishes from viral watery discharge)</li>
<li><strong>Eyelids stuck together</strong> upon waking (especially in morning)</li>
<li><strong>Crusting</strong> on eyelashes and lid margins</li>
<li><strong>Mild eyelid swelling</strong> (preseptal)</li>
<li><strong>Gritty or foreign body sensation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Mild discomfort</strong> (not severe pain -- if severe pain or photophobia, suspect keratitis)</li>
<li><strong>No significant itching</strong> (distinguishes from allergic conjunctivitis)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Hyperacute (gonococcal):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Copious, continuous purulent discharge</strong> (pouring from eye)</li>
<li><strong>Severe chemosis</strong> (conjunctival swelling)</li>
<li><strong>Rapid progression</strong> (12-24 hours)</li>
<li><strong>Preauricular lymphadenopathy</strong></li>
<li><strong>Risk of corneal perforation</strong> if untreated</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Typical bacterial conjunctivitis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Topical antibiotic drops or ointment</strong> x 5-7 days:
<ul>
<li>Fluoroquinolone: ofloxacin 0.3%, ciprofloxacin 0.3%, moxifloxacin 0.5%</li>
<li>Aminoglycoside: tobramycin 0.3%</li>
<li>Combination: polymyxin B/trimethoprim, neomycin/polymyxin B/bacitracin</li>
<li>Ointments (erythromycin, bacitracin) preferred in children (easier to apply)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Warm compresses</strong> to remove crusting and discharge</li>
<li><strong>Good hand hygiene</strong> (hand washing, avoid sharing towels/pillows)</li>
<li><strong>Stay home</strong> from work/school until discharge resolves (usually 24-48h after starting antibiotics)</li>
<li><strong>Discontinue contact lenses</strong> until fully resolved</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Hyperacute (gonococcal/chlamydial):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>IM/IV ceftriaxone</strong> 1 g (gonococcal)</li>
<li><strong>Oral azithromycin</strong> 1 g or doxycycline 100 mg BID x 7 days (chlamydial)</li>
<li><strong>Topical antibiotics</strong> (in addition to systemic)</li>
<li><strong>Urgent ophthalmology referral</strong></li>
<li><strong>Saline irrigation</strong> of discharge</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Neonatal conjunctivitis (ophthalmia neonatorum):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Prophylaxis: erythromycin ointment at birth</li>
<li>If infection occurs: culture and treat based on organism (gonococcal, chlamydial, chemical, herpetic)</li>
</ul>
