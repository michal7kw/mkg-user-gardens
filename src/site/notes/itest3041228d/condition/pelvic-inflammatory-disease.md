<h1>Pelvic Inflammatory Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is an <strong>ascending infection of the upper female reproductive tract</strong> involving the endometrium (endometritis), fallopian tubes (salpingitis), ovaries (oophoritis), and peritoneum (peritonitis). It is most commonly caused by <strong>sexually transmitted bacteria</strong> -- <em>Neisseria gonorrhoeae</em> and <em>Chlamydia trachomatis</em> -- but often involves polymicrobial infection with anaerobes, Gardnerella, and other vaginal organisms. PID is the most common serious infection in women of reproductive age, affecting approximately <strong>4.4% of women aged 18-44</strong> in the US.</p>
<p>The principal clinical challenge is the <strong>wide spectrum of presentation</strong>: from subtle, atypical symptoms (minimal pain, no fever) to severe acute abdomen with peritonitis and sepsis. The CDC's clinical diagnostic criteria have <strong>high sensitivity (~87%) but low specificity (~50%)</strong> -- meaning that clinical diagnosis tends to over-treat (treat some women who don't actually have PID) rather than miss cases. This trade-off is deliberate: the consequences of <strong>missing PID are severe</strong> (infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain).</p>
<p><strong>Long-term sequelae of PID (from scarring of the fallopian tubes):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tubal factor infertility:</strong> ~12% after one episode, ~23% after two, ~54% after three</li>
<li><strong>Ectopic pregnancy:</strong> 6-10x increased risk</li>
<li><strong>Chronic pelvic pain:</strong> up to 30% of women after PID</li>
<li><strong>Tubo-ovarian abscess:</strong> ~5-15% of hospitalized PID patients</li>
</ul>
<p>These complications underscore the critical importance of <strong>prompt empiric antibiotic therapy</strong> as soon as PID is suspected -- treatment delays increase the risk of long-term sequelae.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p><strong>CDC Clinical Diagnostic Criteria (2015):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Minimum criteria (treat empirically if all present):</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Cervical motion tenderness (CMT) on bimanual exam</li>
<li>Uterine tenderness</li>
<li>Adnexal tenderness</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Additional criteria (increase specificity):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Oral temperature &gt;38.3C</li>
<li>Mucopurulent cervical or vaginal discharge</li>
<li>Abundant WBCs on saline microscopy of vaginal fluid</li>
<li>Elevated ESR or CRP</li>
<li>Laboratory confirmation of <em>N. gonorrhoeae</em> or <em>C. trachomatis</em></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Symptoms:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Lower abdominal or pelvic pain (most common; usually bilateral)</li>
<li>Abnormal vaginal discharge (purulent or yellow-green)</li>
<li>Post-coital or intermenstrual bleeding</li>
<li>Dysuria</li>
<li>Dyspareunia</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome:</strong> perihepatitis from ascending infection -&gt; right upper quadrant pain; pleuritic; RUQ tenderness; may mimic cholecystitis</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p><strong>Clinical diagnosis</strong> is the primary tool (CDC recommends empiric treatment if minimum criteria met)<br />
<strong>NAAT (nucleic acid amplification test)</strong> for <em>N. gonorrhoeae</em> and <em>C. trachomatis</em> (vaginal, cervical, or urine)<br />
<strong>WBC, CRP, ESR</strong> (supportive but not required)<br />
<strong>Pelvic ultrasound:</strong> evaluates for tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA), hydrosalpinx<br />
<strong>Laparoscopy</strong> (rarely needed; gold standard for diagnosis but invasive)<br />
<strong>Pregnancy test</strong> (rule out ectopic pregnancy; PID and ectopic can coexist)</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Outpatient treatment (mild-to-moderate PID):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ceftriaxone</strong> 500 mg IM (single dose) +</li>
<li><strong>Doxycycline</strong> 100 mg PO BID x 14 days +</li>
<li><strong>Metronidazole</strong> 500 mg PO BID x 14 days (for anaerobic coverage)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Inpatient treatment (severe PID, pregnancy, TOA, unable to tolerate PO, failed outpatient):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cefoxitin</strong> 2 g IV q6h or <strong>cefotetan</strong> 2 g IV q12h + <strong>doxycycline</strong> 100 mg PO/IV q12h</li>
<li>OR <strong>clindamycin</strong> 900 mg IV q8h + <strong>gentamicin</strong> loading then daily</li>
<li>Transition to oral doxycycline + metronidazole when improving</li>
<li>Duration: 14 days total</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Additional measures:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Treat sexual partners</strong> (empiric gonorrhea + chlamydia treatment) within past 60 days</li>
<li><strong>Abstain from intercourse</strong> until treatment completed and symptoms resolved</li>
<li><strong>Re-test</strong> for gonorrhea/chlamydia 3-6 months after treatment (high reinfection rate)</li>
<li><strong>HIV testing</strong></li>
<li><strong>TOA management:</strong> IV antibiotics; percutaneous or surgical drainage if &gt;8 cm, ruptured, or not responding to antibiotics in 48-72 hours</li>
</ul>
