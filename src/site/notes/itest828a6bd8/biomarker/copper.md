<h1>Copper</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Copper is an essential trace mineral required as a catalytic cofactor for numerous enzymes, including ceruloplasmin (iron oxidation and transport), cytochrome c oxidase (mitochondrial electron transport), copper-zinc superoxide dismutase (SOD1; antioxidant defense), lysyl oxidase (collagen and elastin cross-linking), tyrosinase (melanin synthesis), dopamine beta-hydroxylase (catecholamine synthesis), and ascorbate oxidase (vitamin C metabolism). Approximately 95% of circulating copper is bound to ceruloplasmin (an acute-phase reactant); the remaining &quot;free&quot; or non-ceruloplasmin-bound copper (~5-10 mcg/dL) is the biologically active fraction and is markedly elevated in Wilson's disease.</p>
<p>Both copper deficiency and copper excess are clinically significant. <strong>Deficiency</strong> (rare in healthy individuals with adequate diet; common after bariatric surgery, prolonged parenteral nutrition without copper, zinc excess from over-supplementation, or malabsorption) presents with anemia (microcytic, hypochromic, refractory to iron), neutropenia, and a myelopathy resembling vitamin B12 deficiency subacute combined degeneration. <strong>Excess</strong> most characteristically manifests as Wilson's disease, an autosomal recessive disorder of copper transport (ATP7B mutation) that causes copper accumulation in liver (chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis), brain (neurologic and psychiatric symptoms), and cornea (Kayser-Fleischer rings).</p>
<p>Serum copper is measured as part of the workup for Wilson's disease (alongside ceruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper, and slit-lamp exam for Kayser-Fleischer rings), unexplained anemia or neutropenia with neurologic symptoms, and monitoring of total parenteral nutrition. <strong>24-hour urinary copper</strong> is the most sensitive test for Wilson's disease (&gt;100 mcg/24h is highly suggestive, especially in conjunction with low ceruloplasmin). Ceruloplasmin is measured alongside because the interpretation of serum copper requires knowing the ceruloplasmin level: in Wilson's, total serum copper is paradoxically LOW (because ceruloplasmin is low), but free copper is HIGH.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (well-validated biomarker; standard clinical use in hepatology, hematology, and neurology).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Serum copper is an acute-phase reactant (via ceruloplasmin); elevated in inflammation, infection, pregnancy, estrogen therapy</li>
<li>In Wilson's disease, total serum copper may be low (because ceruloplasmin is low) -- measure 24-h urinary copper and ceruloplasmin</li>
<li>Pregnancy: copper rises 2-3x due to estrogen-induced ceruloplasmin</li>
<li>Infants: copper levels lower at birth, rise to adult levels by 6 months</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase serum copper:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Inflammation / infection (ceruloplasmin is an acute-phase reactant)</li>
<li>Pregnancy (2-3x elevation due to estrogen)</li>
<li>Estrogen therapy (oral contraceptives, HRT)</li>
<li>Primary biliary cholangitis</li>
<li>Hemochromatosis</li>
<li>Hyperthyroidism</li>
<li>Malignancy (some)</li>
<li>Wilson's disease (free copper elevated; total copper may be low due to low ceruloplasmin)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease serum copper:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Copper deficiency (malabsorption, bariatric surgery, gastrectomy, celiac disease)</li>
<li>Excess zinc supplementation (&gt;50 mg/day for &gt;8 weeks)</li>
<li>Prolonged total parenteral nutrition without copper</li>
<li>Menkes disease (X-linked copper transport defect; very low serum copper)</li>
<li>Nephrotic syndrome (urinary loss of ceruloplasmin)</li>
<li>Severe malnutrition, anorexia nervosa</li>
<li>Corticosteroid therapy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Increase 24-h urinary copper:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Wilson's disease (&gt;100 mcg/24h)</li>
<li>Cholestatic liver disease (primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis)</li>
<li>Autoimmune hepatitis</li>
<li>Severe hepatocellular disease (any cause)</li>
<li>Nephrotic syndrome (urinary loss of ceruloplasmin-bound copper)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low serum copper is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Copper deficiency anemia:</strong> microcytic, hypochromic, refractory to iron therapy; corrected by copper supplementation</li>
<li><strong>Neutropenia:</strong> common in copper deficiency; resolves with repletion</li>
<li><strong>Myelopathy:</strong> subacute combined degeneration-like (posterior column, pyramidal tracts); can mimic B12 deficiency</li>
<li><strong>Peripheral neuropathy:</strong> sensorimotor</li>
<li><strong>Connective tissue disorders:</strong> lysyl oxidase dysfunction impairs collagen/elastin cross-linking</li>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis:</strong> lysyl oxidase dysfunction impairs bone matrix</li>
<li><strong>Menkes disease:</strong> X-linked recessive copper transport defect (ATP7A); severe neurodegeneration in male infants; very low serum copper</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated serum copper / Wilson's disease presentation:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hepatic:</strong> chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis, fulminant hepatic failure (especially with Coombs-negative hemolytic anemia)</li>
<li><strong>Neurologic:</strong> parkinsonism, tremor, dystonia, dysarthria, gait disturbance</li>
<li><strong>Psychiatric:</strong> depression, anxiety, psychosis, personality changes</li>
<li><strong>Ocular:</strong> Kayser-Fleischer rings (copper deposition in Descemet's membrane)</li>
<li><strong>Renal:</strong> Fanconi syndrome, aminoaciduria</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute-phase reactant; inflammation confounds</li>
<li>In Wilson's, total serum copper may be low (counterintuitive) -- always interpret with ceruloplasmin</li>
<li>24-h urinary copper is more sensitive but requires complete collection</li>
<li>Penicillamine challenge improves sensitivity in pediatric Wilson's</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Wilson's disease diagnosis (alongside ceruloplasmin, 24-h urine, slit-lamp exam)</li>
<li>Unexplained anemia, neutropenia, or myelopathy workup</li>
<li>TPN monitoring</li>
<li>Zinc supplementation monitoring (long-term high-dose)</li>
<li>Post-bariatric surgery nutritional assessment</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>Copper levels respond within 1-3 months to targeted therapy:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For copper deficiency:</strong> oral copper supplementation 2-4 mg elemental copper daily (copper gluconate, copper glycinate); in severe malabsorption, IV copper chloride 1-2 mg daily x 5 days then weekly x maintenance; identify and treat underlying cause</li>
<li><strong>Reduce zinc supplementation</strong> if copper deficiency from zinc excess (&gt;50 mg/day)</li>
<li><strong>For Wilson's disease:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Chelation: penicillamine 1-2 g/day (with pyridoxine); trientine 750-1500 mg/day (better tolerated)</li>
<li>Zinc acetate 50 mg TID (blocks intestinal copper absorption; maintenance therapy)</li>
<li>Tetrathiomolybdate (investigational; for neurologic Wilson's)</li>
<li>Lifelong therapy; liver transplant for fulminant or refractory disease</li>
<li>Dietary copper restriction (shellfish, nuts, chocolate, liver, mushrooms)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For Menkes disease:</strong> subcutaneous copper histidine or copper chloride (limited efficacy; disease often fatal in early childhood)</li>
<li><strong>Dietary copper sources:</strong> liver, oysters, shellfish, nuts (cashews, walnuts), seeds (sunflower), dark chocolate, mushrooms, lentils</li>
<li><strong>TPN/parenteral nutrition:</strong> ensure copper is included in trace element additive; monitor serial copper levels</li>
<li><strong>Post-bariatric surgery:</strong> lifelong mineral supplementation including copper; monitor at least annually</li>
<li><strong>Balance with zinc:</strong> maintain zinc:copper ratio; high-dose zinc (&gt;30 mg/day long-term) requires copper monitoring and supplementation</li>
</ol>
