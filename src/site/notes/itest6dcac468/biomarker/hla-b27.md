<h1>HLA-B27</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>HLA-B27 (human leukocyte antigen B27) is a class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecule encoded by the HLA-B gene on chromosome 6p21.3. It is present on the surface of all nucleated cells, where it presents intracellular peptide antigens to CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. Over 100 HLA-B27 subtypes have been identified (B<em>27:01 through B</em>27:111), with B<em>27:05 the most common in European-derived populations and B</em>27:04 common in East Asian populations.</p>
<p>HLA-B27 is one of the strongest genetic associations with human disease. It is found in <strong>&gt;90% of ankylosing spondylitis (AS) patients</strong> vs ~6-8% of the general European-derived population (population prevalence varies: ~2% in Japanese, ~6% in northern Europeans, ~13% in Finnish, ~50% in some Native American groups like Haida). The relative risk of AS in HLA-B27-positive individuals is approximately 100-200x compared to negative individuals.</p>
<p>Several hypotheses explain HLA-B27's role in disease pathogenesis: (1) <strong>&quot;Arthritogenic peptide&quot; hypothesis</strong> -- HLA-B27 presents specific self-peptides to CD8+ T cells, triggering autoimmunity; (2) <strong>&quot;Misfolding&quot; hypothesis</strong> -- misfolded HLA-B27 heavy chains accumulate in the endoplasmic reticulum, activating the unfolded protein response and stimulating IL-23 production; (3) <strong>&quot;Homodimer&quot; hypothesis</strong> -- free HLA-B27 heavy chain homodimers on the cell surface activate NK cells and Th17 cells via KIR3DL2 receptor, driving IL-17-mediated inflammation; (4) <strong>Altered microbiome</strong> -- HLA-B27 transgenic rats develop arthritis only when exposed to specific gut bacteria, implicating microbiome-HLA interactions.</p>
<p>Clinically, HLA-B27 testing is most useful in the workup of <strong>inflammatory back pain</strong> (especially in young men &lt;45 years with insidious onset, morning stiffness, improvement with exercise). The Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) classification criteria include HLA-B27 as a key entry point for axial spondyloarthritis diagnosis. HLA-B27 is also tested in: (a) <strong>acute anterior uveitis</strong> (the majority of cases are HLA-B27 positive); (b) <strong>reactive arthritis</strong> (post-chlamydial, post-enteric); (c) <strong>psoriatic arthritis with axial involvement</strong>; (d) <strong>IBD-associated spondyloarthritis</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (ASAS classification criteria component; universal clinical use).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low PPV in general population</strong> (~20%) -- do NOT screen asymptomatic individuals</li>
<li><strong>High PPV in symptomatic patients</strong> -- in a young patient with inflammatory back pain, HLA-B27 positivity has PPV ~80% for axial spondyloarthritis</li>
<li><strong>10% of AS patients are HLA-B27 negative</strong> -- a negative test does not exclude AS</li>
<li>More than 100 known subtypes; most labs report positive/negative without subtype</li>
<li>Subtype B<em>27:06 (Thai population) and B</em>27:09 (Sardinia) are NOT associated with AS -- rare exceptions</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Risk modifiers for developing AS in HLA-B27-positive individuals:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Family history of spondyloarthritis (RR ~6-12x)</li>
<li>Male sex (AS affects men 2-3x more often; psoriatic arthritis and reactive arthritis equally)</li>
<li>Age &lt;45 (AS onset typically 20-40 years)</li>
<li>Smoking (worsens AS severity and treatment response)</li>
<li>Microbiome composition</li>
<li>Other HLA markers (HLA-B<em>27:05 vs B</em>27:04 vs B*27:02 -- varying risk)</li>
<li>ERAP1 polymorphisms (epistatic interaction with HLA-B27)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>HLA-B27 positivity is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ankylosing spondylitis:</strong> &gt;90% of AS patients are HLA-B27 positive; in the appropriate clinical context (inflammatory back pain, sacroiliitis on imaging), positivity confirms the diagnosis</li>
<li><strong>Axial spondyloarthritis (non-radiographic):</strong> ASAS criteria allow diagnosis based on HLA-B27 + sacroiliitis on MRI without radiographic changes</li>
<li><strong>Reactive arthritis (Reiter's syndrome):</strong> 60-80% of reactive arthritis patients are HLA-B27 positive; post-chlamydial or post-enteric (Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, Campylobacter)</li>
<li><strong>Acute anterior uveitis:</strong> ~50% of cases are HLA-B27 positive; classic presentation is unilateral acute painful red eye</li>
<li><strong>Psoriatic arthritis (axial form):</strong> ~30-50% of axial PsA patients are HLA-B27 positive (peripheral PsA much lower)</li>
<li><strong>IBD-associated spondyloarthritis:</strong> HLA-B27 positivity similar to AS (60-70%)</li>
<li><strong>Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (enthesitis-related):</strong> HLA-B27 positive in ERA subtype</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low PPV in general population</strong> (~20%) -- screening asymptomatic individuals is NOT recommended</li>
<li><strong>10% of AS patients are HLA-B27 negative</strong> -- a negative test does NOT exclude AS</li>
<li><strong>Not useful for disease monitoring</strong> -- status is fixed; titers don't apply</li>
<li><strong>Population prevalence varies</strong> -- interpretation differs by ethnicity</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Inflammatory back pain workup in young patients</li>
<li>Acute anterior uveitis workup (HLA-B27 + sacroiliac imaging)</li>
<li>Reactive arthritis diagnosis (post-infectious asymmetric oligoarthritis)</li>
<li>Psoriatic arthritis with axial symptoms</li>
<li>IBD with spondyloarthritis features</li>
<li>Family studies (research)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>HLA-B27 status is genetic and cannot be modified. Management targets the spondyloarthropathy when present:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>NSAIDs (first-line for AS)</strong> at full dose (e.g., naproxen 500 mg BID, indomethacin 50 mg TID, celecoxib 200 mg BID) -- cornerstone of AS therapy; continuous therapy preferred over PRN</li>
<li><strong>TNF inhibitors</strong> for inadequate NSAID response:
<ul>
<li>Adalimumab 40 mg SC q2 weeks</li>
<li>Etanercept 50 mg SC weekly</li>
<li>Infliximab 5 mg/kg IV q6 weeks</li>
<li>Certolizumab 200 mg SC q2 weeks</li>
<li>Golimumab 50 mg SC monthly</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>IL-17 inhibitors</strong> for inadequate NSAID/TNF response:
<ul>
<li>Secukinumab 150 mg SC weekly x5 then monthly</li>
<li>Ixekizumab 80 mg q4 weeks</li>
<li>Bimekizumab (most recent; dual IL-17A/F)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>JAK inhibitors</strong> for refractory disease: upadacitinib, tofacitinib (FDA-approved 2021-2024)</li>
<li><strong>Physical therapy</strong> -- the cornerstone of long-term management; daily spinal extension exercises, deep breathing, swimming, postural training</li>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong> -- smoking worsens AS severity, radiographic progression, and biologic response</li>
<li><strong>DMARDs (sulfasalazine, methotrexate)</strong> -- may help peripheral arthritis but NOT axial disease</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroid injections</strong> -- for symptomatic sacroiliac joints, peripheral entheses</li>
<li><strong>Uveitis management:</strong> ophthalmology referral; topical corticosteroids; TNF inhibitors (especially adalimumab, infliximab) for recurrent uveitis</li>
<li><strong>Treat comorbidities:</strong> inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, cardiovascular risk reduction</li>
<li><strong>Surgery</strong> (total hip replacement, spinal surgery for severe kyphosis) -- late-stage disease</li>
</ol>
