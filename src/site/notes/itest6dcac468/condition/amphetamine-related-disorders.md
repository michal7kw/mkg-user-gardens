<h1>Amphetamine-Related Disorders</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Amphetamine-related disorders encompass a spectrum of conditions resulting from the use of <strong>amphetamine-type stimulants (ATS)</strong>, including:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prescription amphetamines:</strong> amphetamine/dextroamphetamine (Adderall), methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta), lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) -- primarily used for ADHD</li>
<li><strong>Methamphetamine:</strong> highly potent, illicitly manufactured; smoked, snorted, or injected</li>
<li><strong>MDMA (3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine; &quot;ecstasy&quot; or &quot;molly&quot;):</strong> entactogen with stimulant and mild hallucinogenic properties</li>
</ul>
<p>The neuropharmacology involves <strong>massive release of monoamine neurotransmitters</strong> (dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin) from presynaptic terminals, primarily through reversal of the dopamine transporter (DAT) and vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT2). This produces intense euphoria, increased energy, alertness, and confidence -- reinforcing repeated use.</p>
<p><strong>Disorder spectrum:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Stimulant intoxication:</strong> euphoria, tachycardia, hypertension, mydriasis, psychomotor agitation, paranoia, hallucinations (in severe cases)</li>
<li><strong>Stimulant withdrawal:</strong> depression, fatigue, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, anhedonia, vivid unpleasant dreams, irritability (&quot;crash&quot;); typically begins 24 hours after last use and peaks at 3-5 days</li>
<li><strong>Stimulant use disorder:</strong> compulsive use despite harm, tolerance, withdrawal, loss of control, continued use despite adverse consequences</li>
<li><strong>Stimulant-induced psychotic disorder:</strong> paranoia, delusions, hallucinations (may persist for weeks to months after cessation, especially with methamphetamine)</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Key clinical dangers:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular:</strong> hypertension, tachyarrhythmias, acute MI, aortic dissection, sudden cardiac death (especially with chronic methamphetamine use)</li>
<li><strong>Neurological:</strong> hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke, seizures</li>
<li><strong>Hyperthermia:</strong> especially with MDMA (serotonin syndrome; body temperature can exceed 41C -&gt; rhabdomyolysis, DIC, multi-organ failure)</li>
<li><strong>Psychiatric:</strong> stimulant psychosis (indistinguishable from paranoid schizophrenia); violent behavior</li>
<li><strong>Infectious:</strong> HIV and HCV from injection use; endocarditis</li>
<li><strong>Dental:</strong> &quot;meth mouth&quot; (severe caries and tooth loss from dry mouth, bruxism, and poor hygiene in chronic methamphetamine users)</li>
</ul>
<p>Unlike opioid use disorder, there is <strong>no FDA-approved pharmacotherapy</strong> for stimulant use disorder. Treatment relies on <strong>behavioral interventions</strong> (CBT, contingency management, the Matrix Model).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Acute intoxication management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Benzodiazepines</strong> are first-line for agitation, anxiety, seizures, and sympathetic overactivation:
<ul>
<li>Lorazepam 2-4 mg IV q15min PRN (or diazepam 5-10 mg IV)</li>
<li>Reduces agitation, controls hypertension, prevents seizures</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Severe hypertension:</strong> phentolamine 2.5-5 mg IV (alpha-blocker); or nitroprusside infusion</li>
<li><strong>Hyperthermia (&gt;40C):</strong> rapid cooling (ice baths, evaporative cooling); IV fluids; <strong>benzodiazepines</strong> (reduce muscle rigidity); consider dantrolene if malignant hyperthermia-like syndrome</li>
<li><strong>Cardiac ischemia/arrhythmia:</strong> standard ACLS protocols; avoid beta-blockers alone (unopposed alpha stimulation; can worsen hypertension) -- use mixed alpha/beta blockers (labetalol) or alpha-blocker + benzodiazepine</li>
<li><strong>Rhabdomyolysis:</strong> aggressive IV fluids; monitor CK and renal function</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Serotonin syndrome (especially MDMA):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Stop serotonergic agents</li>
<li>Benzodiazepines for agitation</li>
<li><strong>Cyproheptadine</strong> (5-HT2A antagonist; 12 mg PO/NG initially, then 2 mg q2h until response; max 32 mg/24h)</li>
<li>Cooling for hyperthermia</li>
<li>ICU monitoring</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Withdrawal management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Supportive care; no specific pharmacotherapy</li>
<li>Treat depression and anxiety symptomatically (SSRIs; but delayed initiation)</li>
<li>Promote sleep (melatonin, trazodone; avoid benzodiazepines long-term due to addiction potential)</li>
<li>Nutritional support (frequent small meals)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Stimulant use disorder treatment:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Contingency management</strong> (most evidence-based; voucher-based reinforcement of abstinence)</li>
<li><strong>Cognitive behavioral therapy</strong> (CBT; identifies triggers, develops coping skills)</li>
<li><strong>Matrix Model</strong> (integrated outpatient program; group and individual therapy; 16 weeks)</li>
<li><strong>Motivational interviewing</strong> (enhances readiness to change)</li>
<li><strong>Community reinforcement approach</strong> (CRA)</li>
<li><strong>12-step programs</strong> (Crystal Meth Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous)</li>
<li><strong>No FDA-approved medications</strong> for stimulant use disorder; emerging agents: modafinil, bupropion, naltrexone, topiramate (limited evidence)</li>
</ul>
