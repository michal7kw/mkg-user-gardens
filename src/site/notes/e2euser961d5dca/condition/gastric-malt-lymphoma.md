<h1>Gastric MALT Lymphoma</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Gastric MALT lymphoma is a low-grade, extranodal marginal-zone B-cell lymphoma that develops in the mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) of the stomach. The normal stomach contains little organized lymphoid tissue; chronic antigenic stimulation — overwhelmingly from <strong>Helicobacter pylori</strong> infection — recruits and sustains a reactive lymphoid population from which a monoclonal B-cell tumor can eventually emerge. It is the most common form of extranodal marginal-zone lymphoma and a paradigm of antigen-driven lymphomagenesis.</p>
<p>The defining and clinically remarkable feature of this lymphoma is its dependence on H. pylori in early disease. Because the malignant B cells rely on signals derived from the infection-driven inflammatory microenvironment, eradicating H. pylori with antibiotics removes that stimulus and induces durable tumor regression in a majority of early-stage, infection-positive cases. This makes gastric MALT lymphoma one of the very few malignancies that can be cured with antibiotic therapy. A subset of tumors, however, carry the <strong>t(11;18)(q21;q21) API2-MALT1 translocation</strong>, which renders them autonomous of H. pylori signaling and predicts failure of eradication therapy.</p>
<p>Clinically, the disease is indolent and often presents with vague dyspeptic symptoms or is found incidentally during endoscopy for other indications. Diagnosis requires careful histologic and immunophenotypic evaluation of gastric biopsies, assessment of H. pylori status, and molecular testing for predictive translocations. Management is staged: eradication first for infection-positive localized disease, with radiotherapy, rituximab-based therapy, or immunochemotherapy reserved for non-responders and more advanced disease.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Symptoms are typically mild and nonspecific. Patients most often report dyspepsia, epigastric pain, nausea, or early satiety, and many are asymptomatic. Chronic mucosal involvement can cause occult gastrointestinal bleeding and iron-deficiency anemia. Constitutional symptoms such as weight loss or B symptoms are uncommon and, when present, raise concern for high-grade transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis rests on upper endoscopy with multiple, mapped biopsies. Histology shows a dense marginal-zone B-cell infiltrate with characteristic lymphoepithelial lesions; immunohistochemistry confirms a CD20-positive, CD5- and CD10-negative monoclonal B-cell population. H. pylori status is assessed by histology, stool antigen, or urea breath testing, and molecular analysis for the t(11;18) translocation identifies eradication-resistant tumors. Endoscopic ultrasound defines the depth of mucosal/submucosal involvement, and CT completes Lugano staging.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant risk factor is chronic H. pylori infection, present in the great majority of cases. Other contributors include chronic antigenic or autoimmune stimulation and, occasionally, infection with non-pylori Helicobacter species. The molecular t(11;18) lesion is not a risk factor for developing the disease but is a key determinant of treatment response.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>For early-stage, H. pylori-positive disease, <strong>eradication therapy is first-line</strong>, with endoscopic and histologic surveillance to confirm regression, which may take months. Localized disease that is H. pylori-negative or fails to regress after eradication is typically treated with involved-field radiotherapy, achieving high local control. Advanced, disseminated, refractory, or t(11;18)-positive disease is managed with rituximab-based immunotherapy or immunochemotherapy. High-grade transformation is treated as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The principal complications are gastrointestinal bleeding from mucosal involvement, disease persistence or relapse despite eradication (especially in t(11;18)-positive tumors), and transformation to an aggressive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, which markedly changes the therapeutic approach and prognosis.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis is generally excellent. Early-stage, H. pylori-positive disease frequently achieves long-term remission after antibiotic eradication alone, and radiotherapy provides durable control for localized cases that do not respond. Outcomes are less favorable in the presence of t(11;18), deep invasion, advanced stage, or histologic transformation.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Gastric MALT lymphoma is the most common extranodal marginal-zone lymphoma and accounts for a substantial share of primary gastric lymphomas. Its incidence parallels the regional prevalence of H. pylori, and it most often affects middle-aged and older adults. Declining H. pylori prevalence in many countries has been accompanied by changes in the incidence of associated gastric lymphoid malignancies.</p>
