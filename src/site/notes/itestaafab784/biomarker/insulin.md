<h1>Insulin</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Insulin is a 51-amino-acid peptide hormone produced by the beta-cells of the pancreatic islets of Langerhans. It is the body's primary anabolic and glucose-lowering hormone, secreted in response to rising blood glucose after a meal. Insulin binds the insulin receptor on liver, muscle, and adipose tissue, triggering translocation of GLUT4 transporters to the cell surface so glucose can enter cells for energy or storage. It simultaneously suppresses hepatic glucose production, promotes glycogen and fat synthesis, and inhibits lipolysis.</p>
<p>Fasting insulin is measured to assess how much hormone the pancreas must secrete to keep glucose in the normal range. When peripheral tissues become resistant to insulin's action, the beta-cells compensate by secreting more, producing chronically elevated fasting insulin (hyperinsulinemia) long before fasting glucose or HbA1c rise. For this reason, fasting insulin is one of the earliest detectable signals of metabolic dysfunction. It is most informative when interpreted alongside fasting glucose, often combined into the HOMA-IR index (fasting insulin x fasting glucose / 22.5) to quantify insulin resistance and residual beta-cell function.</p>
<p>Unlike glucose, insulin is not part of routine metabolic panels and assay results vary between laboratories because there is no fully harmonized international standard. It is ordered primarily to investigate suspected insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), reactive hypoglycemia, or to distinguish endogenous from exogenous insulin. Very low fasting insulin in the setting of high glucose suggests beta-cell failure, as seen in type 1 diabetes and advanced insulin-deficient type 2 diabetes.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Insulin must be drawn fasting (8-12 hours) for meaningful interpretation; post-prandial values are far higher and not comparable.</li>
<li>Reference intervals differ substantially across immunoassay platforms because insulin assays are not fully standardized; use the issuing laboratory's range.</li>
<li>A single elevated insulin is less informative than the HOMA-IR ratio, which contextualizes insulin against glucose.</li>
<li>Pregnancy, puberty, and exogenous insulin therapy alter values and limit interpretation.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Insulin:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Insulin resistance (compensatory hypersecretion by beta-cells)</li>
<li>Obesity and excess visceral adiposity (drives resistance)</li>
<li>High-carbohydrate or high-glycemic diet</li>
<li>Physical inactivity and poor sleep</li>
<li>PCOS and metabolic syndrome</li>
<li>Chronic stress and elevated cortisol (counter-regulatory)</li>
<li>Certain medications (corticosteroids, some antipsychotics)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Insulin:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Weight loss and reduced visceral fat</li>
<li>Regular aerobic and resistance exercise (improves sensitivity)</li>
<li>Caloric restriction and intermittent fasting</li>
<li>Low-carbohydrate / low-glycemic dietary patterns</li>
<li>Beta-cell failure (pathological decrease, as in type 1 diabetes)</li>
<li>Insulin-sensitizing agents (metformin, GLP-1 agonists, thiazolidinediones)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insulin Resistance</strong> -- chronic hyperinsulinemia is the hallmark of impaired insulin signaling and the central abnormality of metabolic syndrome.</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 Diabetes</strong> -- elevated fasting insulin precedes overt diabetes by years; risk rises as beta-cells eventually fail to keep pace.</li>
<li><strong>Metabolic Syndrome</strong> -- clusters with central obesity, hypertension, high triglycerides, and low HDL.</li>
<li><strong>PCOS</strong> -- hyperinsulinemia drives ovarian androgen excess and anovulation.</li>
<li><strong>NAFLD</strong> -- hepatic insulin resistance promotes de novo lipogenesis and fat accumulation in the liver.</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular Disease</strong> -- hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance are independent contributors to atherogenic dyslipidemia and vascular risk.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Type 1 Diabetes</strong> -- autoimmune destruction of beta-cells causes absolute insulin deficiency.</li>
<li><strong>Advanced type 2 diabetes</strong> -- progressive beta-cell exhaustion lowers endogenous output.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Detecting insulin resistance before glucose or HbA1c become abnormal</li>
<li>Computing HOMA-IR and QUICKI indices for metabolic risk stratification</li>
<li>Tracking response to weight loss, exercise, and insulin-sensitizing therapy</li>
<li>Evaluating reactive hypoglycemia and distinguishing endogenous from exogenous insulin (with C-peptide)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Reduce refined carbohydrate intake</strong> -- low-glycemic, fiber-rich diets lower post-prandial insulin demand and improve sensitivity.</li>
<li><strong>Exercise regularly</strong> -- both aerobic and resistance training increase GLUT4-mediated, insulin-independent glucose uptake; aim for 150+ minutes per week.</li>
<li><strong>Lose visceral fat</strong> -- even 5-10% weight loss markedly reduces fasting insulin and HOMA-IR.</li>
<li><strong>Practice time-restricted eating or intermittent fasting</strong> -- lowers daily insulin exposure and improves sensitivity in many individuals.</li>
<li><strong>Berberine</strong> -- 500 mg two to three times daily has demonstrated glucose- and insulin-lowering effects comparable to metformin in trials (PMID:18397984).</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium</strong> -- supplementation modestly improves insulin sensitivity in deficient and prediabetic individuals (PMID:27530471).</li>
<li><strong>Prioritize sleep</strong> -- even short-term sleep restriction acutely reduces insulin sensitivity.</li>
</ol>
