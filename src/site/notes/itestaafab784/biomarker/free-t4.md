<h1>Free T4</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Free thyroxine (Free T4, FT4) is the unbound, biologically available form of T4 (thyroxine), representing approximately 0.03% of total T4 in the bloodstream. The remaining 99.97% is bound to plasma proteins: thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG; ~70%), transthyretin (~10-15%), and albumin (~15-20%). Only the free fraction is biologically active and able to enter cells. T4 is the predominant hormone secreted by the thyroid gland (~90% of thyroid hormone output) and primarily serves as a <strong>prohormone</strong> that is converted peripherally to the more active T3 (3-5x more potent) by type 1 and type 2 deiodinases in the liver, kidneys, skeletal muscle, brain, and pituitary.</p>
<p>FT4 is the most commonly measured thyroid hormone in clinical practice and, together with TSH, forms the foundation of thyroid function assessment. In the <strong>hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis</strong>, TSH secreted by the pituitary stimulates T4 production by the thyroid, and circulating FT4 feeds back negatively to suppress TSH release. This inverse <strong>log-linear relationship</strong> means small changes in FT4 produce large changes in TSH, making TSH the more sensitive screening test for early thyroid dysfunction. However, FT4 measurement is essential for: (1) <strong>confirming thyroid dysfunction</strong> when TSH is abnormal; (2) <strong>differentiating primary from secondary/central causes</strong> (primary: high TSH + low FT4; central: low/normal TSH + low FT4); (3) <strong>monitoring thyroid hormone replacement therapy</strong> (levothyroxine dosing); (4) <strong>diagnosing thyroid storm</strong> (markedly elevated FT4/FT3).</p>
<p>FT4 is more stable than FT3 in non-thyroidal illness, making it the preferred hormone measurement in acutely ill patients. However, FT4 can be affected by <strong>protein-binding abnormalities</strong> (pregnancy, estrogen therapy raising TBG; genetic TBG variants), and severe illness can suppress FT4 in late euthyroid sick syndrome. <strong>Biotin supplementation</strong> can interfere with streptavidin-biotin immunoassays, producing falsely elevated FT4. The American Thyroid Association recommends LC-MS/MS or equilibrium dialysis as the gold-standard methods, especially in pregnancy and severe illness.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (universally measured; ATA/ETA guideline-endorsed; foundational thyroid test).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range is assay-specific (immunoassay vs LC-MS/MS vs equilibrium dialysis)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> FT4 lower in 2nd/3rd trimester due to TBG elevation; use pregnancy-specific reference ranges</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy, pregnancy:</strong> TBG elevation affects total T4 more than free T4 (but FT4 immunoassays can be artifactually affected)</li>
<li><strong>Biotin supplementation</strong> can falsely elevate FT4 in immunoassays (stop biotin 48 h before testing)</li>
<li><strong>Severe illness:</strong> FT4 may fall in late euthyroid sick syndrome</li>
<li><strong>Amiodarone:</strong> inhibits T4-to-T3 conversion; raises FT4</li>
<li><strong>Phenytoin, carbamazepine:</strong> displace T4 from TBG; lower total T4 more than FT4</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase FT4 (hyperthyroxinemia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Graves disease</strong> (most common cause of overt hyperthyroidism)</li>
<li><strong>Toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma</strong></li>
<li><strong>Thyroiditis</strong> (subacute, postpartum, painless, amiodarone-induced -- transient elevation)</li>
<li><strong>Excessive levothyroxine dosing</strong> (over-replacement)</li>
<li><strong>TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma</strong> (rare; elevated FT4 with non-suppressed TSH)</li>
<li><strong>Thyroid hormone resistance</strong> (Refetoff syndrome; elevated FT4 with normal/elevated TSH)</li>
<li><strong>Amiodarone therapy</strong> (inhibits T4-to-T3 conversion; raises FT4)</li>
<li><strong>Acute psychiatric illness</strong> (transient FT4 elevation)</li>
<li><strong>Biotin interference</strong> (artifactual in streptavidin-biotin immunoassays)</li>
<li><strong>Heparin therapy</strong> (in vitro lipoprotein lipase activation releases free fatty acids that displace T4)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease FT4 (hypothyroxinemia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hypothyroidism</strong> (Hashimoto thyroiditis most common; post-thyroidectomy, post-radioiodine)</li>
<li><strong>Secondary/central hypothyroidism</strong> (pituitary or hypothalamic disease; low FT4 with low/normal TSH)</li>
<li><strong>Iodine deficiency</strong></li>
<li><strong>Severe non-thyroidal illness</strong> (late-stage euthyroid sick syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Inadequate levothyroxine replacement</strong> (non-adherence, malabsorption, drug interactions)</li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> lithium, amiodarone (type 2), carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampin, proton pump inhibitors, iron, calcium (interfere with levothyroxine absorption)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (apparent low FT4 in immunoassays due to high TBG)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated FT4 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hyperthyroidism:</strong> suppressed TSH + elevated FT4</li>
<li><strong>Graves disease:</strong> the most common cause of overt hyperthyroidism</li>
<li><strong>Toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma</strong></li>
<li><strong>Thyroiditis</strong> (early phase; transient; &quot;triphasic&quot; pattern: hyperthyroid -&gt; euthyroid -&gt; hypothyroid)</li>
<li><strong>Exogenous thyroid hormone</strong> (over-replacement of levothyroxine; factitious hyperthyroidism)</li>
<li><strong>Central hyperthyroidism</strong> (TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma or thyroid hormone resistance): elevated FT4 with non-suppressed TSH</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Low FT4 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hypothyroidism:</strong> high TSH + low FT4 (Hashimoto thyroiditis most common)</li>
<li><strong>Central hypothyroidism:</strong> low/normal TSH + low FT4 (pituitary or hypothalamic disease)</li>
<li><strong>Subclinical hypothyroidism:</strong> high TSH + normal FT4 (early primary hypothyroidism)</li>
<li><strong>Severe illness</strong> (late euthyroid sick syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (apparent low FT4 in immunoassays; check TSH primarily)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>FT4 immunoassays can be affected by protein-binding abnormalities</strong> (pregnancy, estrogen, familial dysalbuminemic hyperthyroxinemia)</li>
<li><strong>Biotin interference</strong> is a common source of falsely elevated FT4</li>
<li><strong>LC-MS/MS or equilibrium dialysis</strong> preferred in pregnancy and severe illness</li>
<li><strong>TSH is preferred for routine screening</strong> (more sensitive due to log-linear feedback)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Routine thyroid screening (with TSH)</li>
<li>Hyperthyroidism diagnosis (with TSH, FT3)</li>
<li>Hypothyroidism diagnosis (with TSH)</li>
<li>Levothyroxine therapy monitoring (target TSH in reference range; FT4 may be high-normal or elevated)</li>
<li>Pregnancy thyroid monitoring</li>
<li>Central hypothyroidism monitoring (FT4 is primary target since TSH unreliable)</li>
<li>Thyroid storm diagnosis (markedly elevated FT4/FT3)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>FT4 reflects thyroid hormone production; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For elevated FT4 (hyperthyroidism):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Graves disease:</strong> methimazole (preferred); radioactive iodine ablation; thyroidectomy</li>
<li><strong>Toxic multinodular goiter / adenoma:</strong> radioactive iodine or surgery</li>
<li><strong>Beta-blocker</strong> (propranolol) for symptomatic control</li>
<li><strong>Thyroid storm:</strong> IV methimazole or PTU, beta-blocker, corticosteroids, iodine (after methimazole)</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> levothyroxine dose</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For low FT4 (hypothyroidism):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hypothyroidism:</strong> levothyroxine replacement; dose 1.6 mcg/kg/day; titrate to normalize TSH</li>
<li><strong>Central hypothyroidism:</strong> levothyroxine replacement; dose by FT4 (not TSH); assess other pituitary axes</li>
<li><strong>Monitor absorption:</strong> take on empty stomach 30-60 min before food/drink; separate from iron, calcium, PPIs by 4 h</li>
<li><strong>Address non-adherence</strong> if TSH remains high on adequate dose</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy-specific management:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Increase levothyroxine dose by 25-30% at confirmation of pregnancy (TSH rises with hCG; FT4 target depends on trimester)</li>
<li>Monitor TSH every 4 weeks during pregnancy; target TSH &lt;2.5 (1st trimester), &lt;3.0 (2nd/3rd)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Adequate iodine intake</strong> (150 mcg/day; 250 mcg/day in pregnancy) -- essential substrate</li>
<li><strong>Selenium</strong> 200 mcg daily in mild autoimmune hypothyroidism (controversial; some evidence for antibody reduction)</li>
<li><strong>Avoid excessive iodine</strong> (kelp, contrast agents) in Hashimoto's</li>
<li><strong>Reduce goitrogens</strong> (raw cruciferous vegetables, soy) in susceptible individuals</li>
</ol>
