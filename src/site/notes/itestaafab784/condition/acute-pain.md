<h1>Acute Pain</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Acute pain is defined as pain of sudden onset and limited duration (typically &lt;3-6 months), serving as a <strong>protective biological warning signal</strong> of actual or potential tissue damage. It is one of the most common reasons for healthcare visits and is a fundamental aspect of the clinical presentation of injury, surgery, infection, inflammation, and ischemia.</p>
<p>The <strong>pain pathway</strong> involves four steps:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Transduction:</strong> nociceptors (free nerve endings of A-delta and C fibers) detect noxious stimuli (mechanical, thermal, chemical)</li>
<li><strong>Transmission:</strong> signals travel via peripheral nerves -&gt; dorsal horn of spinal cord -&gt; spinothalamic tract -&gt; thalamus -&gt; somatosensory cortex</li>
<li><strong>Perception:</strong> the brain interprets the signal as pain (location, intensity, quality)</li>
<li><strong>Modulation:</strong> descending pathways from brainstem (serotonin, norepinephrine, endogenous opioids) modulate pain transmission at the spinal cord level</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Classification by type:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Somatic pain:</strong> well-localized, sharp, aching; from skin, muscles, bone (e.g., laceration, fracture)</li>
<li><strong>Visceral pain:</strong> poorly localized, dull, cramping; from internal organs (e.g., appendicitis, renal colic)</li>
<li><strong>Neuropathic pain:</strong> burning, shooting, electric; from nerve damage (e.g., sciatica, postherpetic neuralgia)</li>
<li><strong>Referred pain:</strong> felt at a site distant from the source (e.g., left arm pain in MI, shoulder pain from diaphragmatic irritation)</li>
</ul>
<p>Untreated acute pain has significant physiological consequences: <strong>sympathetic activation</strong> (tachycardia, hypertension, increased myocardial oxygen demand), <strong>impaired ventilation</strong> (splinting; atelectasis, pneumonia), <strong>ileus</strong> (reduced GI motility), <strong>immunosuppression</strong>, <strong>hyperglycemia</strong>, and <strong>thromboembolism</strong> (from immobility). Furthermore, poorly controlled acute pain increases the risk of developing <strong>chronic post-surgical pain</strong> (CPSP), which affects 10-50% of surgical patients.</p>
<p>The modern approach to acute pain management emphasizes <strong>multimodal analgesia</strong> -- combining agents with different mechanisms of action to maximize pain relief while minimizing opioid use and side effects.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Multimodal analgesia (the current standard):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Step 1: Non-opioid foundation:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acetaminophen</strong> 1 g QID (or 650 mg Q6h); safe; ceiling dose 4 g/day (2 g/day in liver disease)</li>
<li><strong>NSAIDs</strong> (ibuprofen 400-600 mg Q6h, ketorolac 15-30 mg IV Q6h, celecoxib 200 mg BID); effective for inflammatory pain; renal/GI precautions</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Step 2: Adjuvants:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Gabapentinoids</strong> (gabapentin 300-600 mg pre-op, pregabalin 75-150 mg); reduce opioid requirements; for neuropathic component</li>
<li><strong>Alpha-2 agonists</strong> (dexmedetomidine; intraoperative); opioid-sparing</li>
<li><strong>Ketamine</strong> (subanesthetic dose 0.1-0.3 mg/kg/hr); NMDA antagonist; prevents central sensitization; for severe pain</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Step 3: Opioids (for moderate-severe pain; used judiciously):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Morphine</strong> 2-4 mg IV q2-4h PRN (or PCA: 1 mg bolus, 6 min lockout)</li>
<li><strong>Hydromorphone</strong> 0.2-0.4 mg IV q2-4h PRN (preferred in renal impairment)</li>
<li><strong>Fentanyl</strong> 25-50 mcg IV q1h PRN (preferred in hemodynamic instability)</li>
<li><strong>Tramadol</strong> 50-100 mg PO q4-6h (weak opioid + SNRI; less respiratory depression)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Step 4: Regional anesthesia (when appropriate):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Peripheral nerve blocks</strong> (femoral, brachial plexus, transverse abdominis plane)</li>
<li><strong>Epidural analgesia</strong> (thoracic epidural for thoracic/abdominal surgery; excellent for major surgery)</li>
<li><strong>Continuous catheter techniques</strong> (extends block duration)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Non-pharmacologic:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Ice/heat therapy</li>
<li>Positioning and splinting (pillow support for surgical incisions)</li>
<li>Distraction, relaxation, music therapy, guided imagery</li>
<li>Early mobilization</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pain assessment scales:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>NRS (0-10):</strong> &quot;On a scale of 0 to 10, where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain imaginable...&quot;</li>
<li><strong>VAS (Visual Analog Scale):</strong> 100 mm line</li>
<li><strong>Faces scale:</strong> for children and cognitively impaired</li>
</ul>
