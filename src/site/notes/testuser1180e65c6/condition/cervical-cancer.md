<h1>Cervical Cancer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cervical cancer is the <strong>fourth most common cancer in women worldwide</strong> (~604,000 new cases annually) and the fourth leading cause of cancer death in women (~342,000 deaths). It is one of the most <strong>preventable cancers</strong> through HPV vaccination and organized cervical screening programs. Over <strong>99% of cases</strong> are caused by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) -- most commonly types <strong>16 and 18</strong> (responsible for ~70% of cases), followed by types 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58.</p>
<p><strong>HPV and the carcinogenesis cascade:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>HPV acquisition:</strong> sexually transmitted; most infections are transient (cleared within 1-2 years by cell-mediated immunity)</li>
<li><strong>Persistent infection:</strong> failure of immune clearance; high-risk HPV integrates into host genome; E6 and E7 oncoproteins degrade p53 and Rb tumor suppressors</li>
<li><strong>Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN):</strong> precancerous changes (CIN1/mild, CIN2/moderate, CIN3/severe) -- detectable by Pap smear and HPV testing; preventable with treatment</li>
<li><strong>Invasive cancer:</strong> typically develops over 10-20 years from persistent CIN3</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>HPV vaccination (Gardasil 9):</strong> covers 9 HPV types (6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58); prevents ~90% of cervical cancers, 90% of genital warts, and other HPV-related cancers (anal, oropharyngeal, vulvar, vaginal, penile). Recommended for ages 9-45 (optimally before sexual debut). Since introduction in 2006, HPV prevalence has declined by <strong>~88%</strong> in vaccinated populations.</p>
<p><strong>Screening (USPSTF 2018):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ages 21-29:</strong> Pap smear every 3 years</li>
<li><strong>Ages 30-65:</strong> Pap smear every 3 years, OR HPV testing every 5 years, OR co-testing every 5 years</li>
<li><strong>&gt;65:</strong> discontinue if adequate prior screening with normal results</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Pre-invasive disease (CIN):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>CIN1:</strong> observation (usually regresses spontaneously)</li>
<li><strong>CIN2/3:</strong> excisional treatment -- <strong>LEEP</strong> (loop electrosurgical excision procedure) or <strong>cold knife cone biopsy</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Early invasive cancer (Stage IA-IB1):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Radical hysterectomy</strong> with pelvic lymphadenectomy (Wertheim procedure)</li>
<li><strong>Fertility-sparing:</strong> radical trachelectomy + pelvic lymphadenectomy (for selected young patients desiring future fertility)</li>
<li><strong>Sentinel lymph node biopsy</strong> increasingly replacing full lymphadenectomy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Locally advanced (Stage IB2-IVA):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Concurrent chemoradiation (CCRT):</strong> the standard of care
<ul>
<li>External beam radiation therapy (45-50 Gy in 25-28 fractions)</li>
<li><strong>Cisplatin</strong> 40 mg/m² weekly during radiation</li>
<li><strong>Brachytherapy</strong> (intracavitary; boosts tumor dose to 80-90 Gy)</li>
<li>CCRT reduces recurrence and improves survival compared to radiation alone</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Metastatic or recurrent:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Platinum-based chemotherapy</strong> (cisplatin + paclitaxel)</li>
<li><strong>Pembrolizumab</strong> (anti-PD-1; KEYNOTE-826; for PD-L1+ or MSI-H tumors; improved OS)</li>
<li><strong>Bevacizumab</strong> (anti-VEGF; improves PFS in recurrent disease)</li>
<li><strong>Tisotumab vedotin</strong> (anti-TF-ADC; for recurrent/metastatic; accelerated approval 2021)</li>
</ul>
