<h1>Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), formerly known as Wegener's granulomatosis, is a rare, potentially life-threatening systemic autoimmune disease belonging to the family of anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitides (AAV), alongside microscopic polyangiitis and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis. It is defined pathologically by the triad of necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, small-to-medium-vessel necrotizing vasculitis, and pauci-immune (sparse immune-complex) glomerulonephritis. The classic clinical pattern involves the upper respiratory tract, the lungs, and the kidneys, although virtually any organ can be affected.</p>
<p>The disease is closely linked to circulating ANCA, most commonly the cytoplasmic (c-ANCA) pattern directed against proteinase-3 (PR3). These autoantibodies are not merely diagnostic markers but are believed to be directly pathogenic: PR3-ANCA activate primed neutrophils, triggering a respiratory burst, degranulation, and the release of neutrophil extracellular traps that damage the vascular endothelium and recruit further inflammatory cells. The result is the granuloma formation and necrotizing capillaritis that destroy nasal cartilage, cavitate lung tissue, and obliterate glomerular capillaries.</p>
<p>GPA matters clinically because, in its severe generalized form, untreated disease was historically near-uniformly fatal within months, usually from renal or respiratory failure. Modern immunosuppression has transformed it into a chronic relapsing-remitting disease in which the goals shift toward durable remission, organ preservation, and minimizing the cumulative toxicity of glucocorticoids and cytotoxic agents. Early recognition - often prompted by the combination of refractory sinusitis, pulmonary nodules, and active urinary sediment - is critical to preventing irreversible organ damage.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>GPA frequently begins insidiously with upper-airway disease that is mistaken for ordinary chronic infection, then progresses to multi-system involvement:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Upper respiratory tract</strong>: persistent rhinosinusitis, nasal crusting and ulceration, epistaxis, and the characteristic saddle-nose deformity from septal cartilage collapse; otitis media and conductive hearing loss; subglottic stenosis causing hoarseness and stridor</li>
<li><strong>Lower respiratory tract</strong>: cough, dyspnea, hemoptysis, migratory pulmonary nodules or cavities, and, in severe disease, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage</li>
<li><strong>Renal</strong>: rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis with microscopic hematuria, red-cell casts, proteinuria, and a rising serum creatinine - a hallmark of the generalized vasculitic phase</li>
<li><strong>Systemic and other organs</strong>: fever, weight loss, arthralgia, palpable purpura, scleritis or orbital masses, and mononeuritis multiplex</li>
</ul>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis combines serology, histopathology, and a compatible clinical-radiographic picture. PR3-ANCA (c-ANCA) is positive in the majority of generalized cases, though a minority are MPO-ANCA-positive or ANCA-negative, especially in limited disease. Tissue biopsy - of nasal mucosa, lung, or kidney - confirms necrotizing granulomatous inflammation and/or pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis. The 2017 international consensus emphasizes antigen-specific immunoassays (anti-PR3, anti-MPO) over indirect immunofluorescence alone for ANCA testing, and the 2022 ACR/EULAR criteria provide a weighted scoring system incorporating clinical, laboratory, imaging, and biopsy findings to classify established small-vessel vasculitis.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>GPA has a multifactorial etiology. Genetic associations include HLA-DPB1*04:01 and polymorphisms in SERPINA1 (encoding alpha-1 antitrypsin, the major physiologic inhibitor of PR3) and PRTN3. Environmental contributors include silica exposure, and chronic nasal carriage of Staphylococcus aureus is associated with a higher relapse rate, suggesting an infectious trigger or perpetuating factor. The disease is most common in people of Northern European ancestry and typically presents in middle-to-older adulthood, with roughly equal sex distribution.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Treatment is divided into remission induction and maintenance. <strong>Induction</strong> for organ- or life-threatening disease uses high-dose glucocorticoids combined with either rituximab (an anti-CD20 B-cell-depleting monoclonal antibody) or cyclophosphamide; rituximab is at least as effective and is preferred for relapsing disease and to spare fertility. Plasma exchange may be added in severe renal impairment or alveolar hemorrhage. Avacopan, an oral C5a-receptor antagonist, allows substantial glucocorticoid reduction. <strong>Maintenance</strong> with scheduled rituximab (or azathioprine/methotrexate) sustains remission, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is used both for Pneumocystis prophylaxis and to control limited upper-airway disease.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most feared complications are end-stage kidney disease requiring renal replacement therapy and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage with respiratory failure. Fixed airway damage produces subglottic and tracheobronchial stenosis. Long-term immunosuppression carries substantial risks of serious infection, secondary malignancy (notably with cumulative cyclophosphamide exposure), and infertility. Relapse is common - particularly in PR3-ANCA-positive patients - and drives much of the chronic organ damage.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis has improved dramatically from the pre-immunosuppression era, when median survival was measured in months. With modern induction-maintenance regimens, multi-year remission is achievable in most patients, though relapse rates remain high and a significant minority progress to dialysis-dependent kidney failure. Outcomes are worse with advanced age, severe renal impairment at diagnosis, and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage; much of the late mortality and morbidity now stems from treatment toxicity and cardiovascular disease rather than uncontrolled vasculitis.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>GPA is rare, with an estimated annual incidence on the order of 5-20 per million and a prevalence of roughly 20-160 per million, highest in Northern European and other higher-latitude populations. It can occur at any age but peaks in the fifth to seventh decades, affecting men and women approximately equally.</p>
