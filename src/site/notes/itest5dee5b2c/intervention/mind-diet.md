<h1>MIND Diet</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The MIND diet (Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay) is a hybrid dietary pattern developed by researchers at Rush University Medical Center that merges elements of the Mediterranean diet and the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet, then refines them toward foods with the strongest evidence for brain health. It specifies ten &quot;brain-healthy&quot; food groups to emphasize—green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, berries, nuts, whole grains, fish, poultry, beans, olive oil, and (optionally) wine—and five groups to limit: red meat, butter and stick margarine, cheese, pastries and sweets, and fried or fast food.</p>
<p>The MIND diet differs from its parent diets in two notable ways: it singles out berries (rather than fruit generally) and green leafy vegetables for their neuroprotective flavonoids and folate, and it explicitly de-emphasizes high-saturated-fat dairy. Observational cohort data from the Rush Memory and Aging Project found that high adherence was associated with markedly reduced incidence of Alzheimer's disease and slower cognitive decline, even at moderate adherence levels—an attractive feature for real-world sustainability.</p>
<p>Evidence Level: 3/5. Strong observational data support the MIND diet; a subsequent multi-site randomized controlled trial (the MIND trial) showed cognitive improvement in both the MIND and control (mild caloric restriction) arms, tempering the magnitude of effect attributable to the diet specifically.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>The MIND diet acts through several convergent neuroprotective pathways. Berry and leafy-green flavonoids (anthocyanins, flavonols) and olive-oil polyphenols are antioxidant and anti-inflammatory, reducing reactive oxygen species and dampening NF-kB-driven neuroinflammation. Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA, DHA) from fish support neuronal membrane integrity, synaptic plasticity, and resolution of inflammation. Folate and B-vitamins from leafy greens lower homocysteine, a vascular and neurotoxic risk factor. Collectively these mechanisms slow amyloid-beta aggregation and tau hyperphosphorylation while preserving cerebrovascular function—addressing both the neurodegenerative and vascular contributions to cognitive decline.</p>
<h2>Protocol Details</h2>
<ul>
<li>Green leafy vegetables: 6+ servings/week</li>
<li>Other vegetables: 1+ serving/day</li>
<li>Berries: 2+ servings/week (blueberries and strawberries favored)</li>
<li>Nuts: 5+ servings/week</li>
<li>Whole grains: 3+ servings/day</li>
<li>Fish: 1+ serving/week</li>
<li>Beans/legumes: 3+ servings/week</li>
<li>Poultry: 2+ servings/week</li>
<li>Olive oil as the primary cooking fat</li>
<li>Limit: red meat (&lt;4 servings/week), butter (&lt;1 tbsp/day), cheese, pastries/sweets, fried/fast food (&lt;1 serving/week)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<p>The MIND diet is generally safe and nutritionally adequate for most adults. Caution is warranted for individuals with nut or fish allergies (use alternative protein and omega-3 sources) and those requiring texture-modified diets for dysphagia. The optional wine component should be omitted in anyone with alcohol use disorder, liver disease, or contraindicating medications.</p>
