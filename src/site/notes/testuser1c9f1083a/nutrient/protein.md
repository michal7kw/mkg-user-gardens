<h1>Protein</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Protein is an essential macronutrient composed of amino acids linked by peptide bonds, providing the structural and functional building blocks for virtually every tissue and process in the body. Of the 20 standard amino acids, 9 are essential (must be obtained from diet): histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine. Protein provides 4 kilocalories per gram and has the highest thermic effect of food (20-30%), meaning it increases metabolic rate more than carbohydrates or fats during digestion.</p>
<p>Protein quality is assessed by the Protein Digestibility-Corrected Amino Acid Score (PDCAAS) and the newer Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score (DIAAS). Animal proteins (eggs, dairy, meat, fish) generally have higher scores due to complete essential amino acid profiles and superior digestibility. Plant proteins (legumes, grains, nuts) can provide complete nutrition when combined complementarily throughout the day. The leucine threshold of approximately 2.5 grams per meal is important for maximally stimulating muscle protein synthesis via mTOR pathway activation.</p>
<p>Current RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day represents the minimum to prevent deficiency, not the optimal intake. Active individuals, athletes, older adults, and those recovering from illness benefit from 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day. Distributing protein evenly across meals (25-40 g per meal) optimizes muscle protein synthesis compared to concentrating intake at one meal. Higher protein intake supports satiety, thermic effect, lean mass preservation during caloric restriction, and healthy aging.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Provides amino acids for tissue growth, repair, and maintenance</li>
<li>Serves as the structural basis for enzymes, hormones, and antibodies</li>
<li>Supports immune function through immunoglobulin and cytokine synthesis</li>
<li>Activates muscle protein synthesis via mTOR signaling (leucine-driven)</li>
<li>Maintains fluid balance through plasma proteins (albumin, globulins)</li>
<li>Provides gluconeogenic substrates during fasting or prolonged exercise</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Eggs (highest bioavailability reference protein)</li>
<li>Poultry (chicken breast, turkey)</li>
<li>Fish and seafood (salmon, tuna, shrimp)</li>
<li>Lean red meat (beef, bison, venison)</li>
<li>Dairy (Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, whey protein)</li>
<li>Legumes (lentils, chickpeas, black beans)</li>
<li>Tofu, tempeh, and edamame</li>
<li>Nuts and seeds (almonds, pumpkin seeds)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Protein deficiency (protein-energy malnutrition) manifests as muscle wasting (sarcopenia), edema (due to low albumin), impaired immune function, hair loss, poor wound healing, and in severe cases, kwashiorkor (edematous malnutrition) or marasmus (wasting). Elderly populations and individuals with chronic illness are particularly vulnerable to inadequate protein intake leading to sarcopenia and frailty.</p>
<p>Excessive protein intake beyond 2.0 g/kg/day in individuals with pre-existing kidney disease may accelerate renal decline due to increased glomerular hyperfiltration. In healthy individuals, high protein intake is generally well-tolerated, though very high intakes may cause dehydration (increased urea excretion), increased urinary calcium losses, and gastrointestinal discomfort. There is no strong evidence that high protein diets damage healthy kidneys.</p>
