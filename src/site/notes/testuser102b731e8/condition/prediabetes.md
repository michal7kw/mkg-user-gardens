<h1>Prediabetes</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Prediabetes is an intermediate stage of glucose dysregulation in which blood glucose levels are higher than normal but do not yet meet the diagnostic criteria for type 2 diabetes. It is defined biochemically by impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose tolerance, or an intermediate HbA1c, and it represents the clinically detectable window between normal metabolism and overt diabetes. Although often dismissed as &quot;borderline,&quot; prediabetes is a meaningful pathophysiologic state that signals established insulin resistance and beta-cell stress.</p>
<p>The underlying biology mirrors that of type 2 diabetes in miniature: peripheral tissues (muscle, liver, adipose) become resistant to insulin, and the pancreatic beta cells compensate by secreting more insulin. For a time this hyperinsulinemia keeps glucose nearly normal, but as compensation begins to fail, fasting and post-prandial glucose drift upward into the prediabetic range. This makes prediabetes both a warning sign and an opportunity, because the trajectory toward diabetes is not fixed.</p>
<p>Clinically, prediabetes matters for two reasons. First, without intervention a substantial fraction of people progress to type 2 diabetes each year. Second, cardiovascular risk is already elevated during the prediabetic phase. Crucially, landmark trials such as the Diabetes Prevention Program demonstrated that intensive lifestyle change can reduce progression to diabetes by 58%, making prediabetes one of the most actionable diagnoses in preventive medicine.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Prediabetes is overwhelmingly asymptomatic and is usually discovered incidentally on screening blood work or during evaluation of related conditions such as obesity, hypertension, or polycystic ovary syndrome. Some individuals show physical markers of insulin resistance, most notably acanthosis nigricans, a velvety darkening of the skin in the neck, axillae, and other folds. Overt hyperglycemic symptoms (polyuria, polydipsia) are uncommon and, when present, suggest glucose is approaching the diabetic threshold.</p>

<p>These categories can occur alone or together; impaired glucose tolerance carries the highest cardiovascular risk, while combined impairment confers the highest risk of progression. Because the tests probe different physiology, results may be discordant.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The risk profile is essentially identical to that of type 2 diabetes: overweight/obesity (especially visceral fat), physical inactivity, family history, older age, high-risk ethnicity, and a history of gestational diabetes or polycystic ovary syndrome. Coexisting hypertension and atherogenic dyslipidemia frequently cluster with the underlying insulin resistance.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Lifestyle intervention is first-line and highly effective. The evidence-based target is 5-7% weight loss combined with at least 150 minutes per week of moderate physical activity and a shift toward a Mediterranean or lower-glycemic dietary pattern. Metformin is recommended for selected higher-risk individuals (BMI &gt;=35 kg/m², age under 60, or prior gestational diabetes) where it reduces progression, though less effectively than lifestyle change. Regular monitoring of glucose, HbA1c, blood pressure, and lipids allows early escalation if progression occurs.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The principal complication is progression to type 2 diabetes, but cardiovascular risk is already increased during the prediabetic phase, and early microvascular changes (such as incipient retinopathy) can appear before diabetes is formally diagnosed. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease commonly coexists.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis is favorable when prediabetes is identified and addressed early: a meaningful proportion of people revert to normoglycemia with sustained lifestyle change. Left unmanaged, however, the natural history is gradual progression to diabetes and accumulating cardiometabolic risk.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Prediabetes is extremely common. In the United States more than one in three adults is estimated to have it, and the great majority are unaware of their status. Prevalence rises with age and with the global increase in obesity, and a large share of cases will progress to diabetes over the following decade if untreated.</p>
