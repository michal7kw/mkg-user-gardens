<h1>Peptic Ulcer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) is a <strong>mucosal break that penetrates the muscularis mucosae</strong> of the <strong>esophagus, stomach, or duodenum</strong>, produced when aggressive factors (<strong>acid, pepsin</strong>) overwhelm the protective mucosal barrier (<strong>mucus, bicarbonate, blood flow, prostaglandins, rapid epithelial renewal</strong>). The two dominant causes are <strong>Helicobacter pylori infection</strong> (responsible for ~60-80% of gastric ulcers and ~90% of duodenal ulcers worldwide) and <strong>NSAID use</strong> (accounting for most of the remainder); together these explain <strong>&gt;90% of peptic ulcers</strong>. Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma), Crohn disease, and stress ulcers (critical illness) account for the rest.</p>
<p>The pathophysiology differs by cause. <strong>H. pylori</strong> is a gram-negative, microaerophilic bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucus layer and produces <strong>urease</strong> (which hydrolyzes urea to ammonia, creating a neutral microenvironment) and virulence factors (<strong>CagA</strong> injected into epithelial cells, <strong>VacA</strong> cytotoxin). It causes <strong>chronic antral gastritis</strong>, stimulates gastrin release, and -- in susceptible individuals -- produces duodenal or gastric ulceration. <strong>NSAIDs</strong> inhibit cyclooxygenase-1, reducing protective gastric prostaglandins and mucus/bicarbonate secretion while allowing acid to injure the mucosa. The two factors synergize: <strong>H. pylori-positive NSAID users have a 3-6x higher risk of ulcer</strong> than either alone.</p>
<p>The clinical hallmark is <strong>epigastric burning or gnawing pain</strong> with a characteristic temporal pattern: <strong>duodenal ulcer</strong> pain is relieved by food and antacids, occurs 2-3 hours after meals and at night, and is more common in younger patients; <strong>gastric ulcer</strong> pain is worsened by food and is more common in older patients. However, <strong>up to 30% of ulcers are asymptomatic</strong> (especially NSAID-induced in the elderly), and the first sign may be <strong>complications</strong> -- <strong>GI bleeding</strong> (15-20% of ulcers), <strong>perforation</strong> (2-10%), or <strong>gastric outlet obstruction</strong> (2%). Diagnosis is by <strong>upper endoscopy</strong> (EGD; direct visualization, biopsy for H. pylori and to exclude malignancy in gastric ulcers); <strong>H. pylori testing</strong> (urea breath test, stool antigen, or biopsy-based) is mandatory.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Diagnosis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Upper endoscopy (EGD)</strong> -- gold standard; visualizes ulcer, allows biopsy (gastric ulcers must be biopsied to exclude malignancy; duodenal ulcers rarely malignant)</li>
<li><strong>H. pylori testing</strong> -- urea breath test (UBT), stool antigen, or rapid urease test (CLOtest) on biopsy; serology (cannot distinguish active vs past infection)</li>
<li><strong>Upper GI series</strong> (barium) if endoscopy contraindicated; less sensitive</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>H. pylori eradication (first-line regimens, 14 days):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bismuth quadruple therapy</strong> -- PPI BID + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole; preferred first-line (especially with clarithromycin resistance &gt;15%)</li>
<li><strong>Concomitant quadruple</strong> -- PPI BID + clarithromycin + amoxicillin + metronidazole</li>
<li><strong>Triple therapy</strong> (PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin) -- only where clarithromycin resistance &lt;15%</li>
<li><strong>Vonoprazan-amoxicillin dual</strong> -- novel potassium-competitive acid blocker (PCAB); approved 2023-2024; high eradication rates</li>
<li>Eradication success <strong>80-90%</strong>; <strong>confirm eradication</strong> with UBT or stool antigen &gt;=4 weeks after completing therapy (especially for gastric ulcers)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Acid suppression for healing:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PPI</strong> -- omeprazole 20-40 mg daily, pantoprazole 40 mg, esomeprazole 40 mg; for <strong>4 weeks (duodenal ulcer)</strong> or <strong>8 weeks (gastric ulcer)</strong></li>
<li>Healing rate &gt;90% for duodenal, &gt;85% for gastric ulcers</li>
<li><strong>H2 blockers</strong> (famotidine) as alternative; less effective</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>NSAID-induced ulcers:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Discontinue NSAIDs</strong> if possible</li>
<li>If must continue: <strong>add PPI</strong> or switch to <strong>COX-2 selective inhibitor</strong> (celecoxib; lower GI risk but higher cardiovascular risk)</li>
<li><strong>Test for and treat H. pylori</strong> before starting long-term NSAID therapy (reduces ulcer risk)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Complications:</strong></p>
<p><strong>GI bleeding (15-20%):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Resuscitate</strong> (IV fluids, transfuse to Hb &gt;7 g/dL)</li>
<li><strong>PPI infusion</strong> (pantoprazole 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/h x 72 hours)</li>
<li><strong>EGD within 24 hours</strong> for endoscopic hemostasis (epinephrine injection + hemoclip or thermal coagulation) for actively bleeding or visible-vessel ulcers (Forrest Ia-IIb)</li>
<li><strong>Rebleeding</strong> in 10-20%; consider repeat EGD or surgery/IR embolization</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Perforation (2-10%):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Surgical emergency</strong> -- sudden severe abdominal pain, rigid abdomen, free air on upright CXR</li>
<li><strong>Graham patch omentopexy</strong> (laparoscopic or open) for duodenal perforation</li>
<li><strong>Truncal vagotomy + pyloroplasty</strong> or <strong>antrectomy</strong> for recurrent or complex disease (less common now)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Gastric outlet obstruction (2%):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>From edema (acute, responds to PPI) or scarring (chronic, needs endoscopic balloon dilation or surgery)</li>
<li><strong>NG decompression</strong>, correct electrolytes</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Gastric cancer surveillance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Gastric ulcers must be followed to complete healing</strong> with repeat EGD at 8-12 weeks (biopsy to exclude malignancy)</li>
<li>Duodenal ulcers do not require follow-up EGD (rarely malignant)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive nutritional and mucosal-supportive therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Zinc carnosine</strong> (75 mg BID; mucosal healing, evidence from Japan)</li>
<li><strong>Probiotics</strong> (Lactobacillus, Saccharomyces boulardii) -- may improve H. pylori eradication and reduce side effects</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C</strong> -- cofactor for collagen synthesis and healing</li>
<li><strong>NAC</strong> -- mucolytic and antioxidant; limited evidence</li>
<li><strong>Avoid smoking</strong> (impairs healing, increases recurrence)</li>
</ul>
