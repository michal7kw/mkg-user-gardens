<h1>HDL-P</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>HDL-P (HDL Particle Number) quantifies the actual number of high-density lipoprotein particles circulating in the blood, measured using <strong>nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy</strong> or <strong>ion mobility analysis</strong>. Unlike standard HDL cholesterol (HDL-C), which measures the total mass of cholesterol carried within HDL particles, HDL-P counts the number of discrete HDL particles regardless of their cholesterol cargo. This distinction is clinically important because two individuals with identical HDL-C levels can have very different numbers of HDL particles, leading to divergent cardiovascular risk profiles.</p>
<p>The clinical significance of HDL-P lies in the concept that it is the <strong>HDL particles themselves -- not the cholesterol they carry -- that perform the cardioprotective functions</strong> of reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), anti-inflammatory signaling, antioxidant activity (via paraoxonase), and endothelial protection. Higher HDL-P indicates more particles available to extract cholesterol from arterial walls and transport it to the liver for excretion. Studies (including the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, MESA, and the Framingham Heart Study) have shown that <strong>HDL-P is a stronger predictor of cardiovascular events than HDL-C</strong>, particularly in situations of discordance where HDL-C is normal but HDL-P is low (increased risk) or where HDL-C is low but HDL-P is adequate (lower risk than expected).</p>
<p>HDL-P is especially useful in <strong>insulin-resistant states</strong> (metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, obesity) where HDL particles become cholesterol-depleted and dysfunctional; HDL-C underestimates risk while HDL-P captures the true deficit. HDL-P is measured as part of an advanced (NMR) lipid panel and is ordered when standard lipid panels provide an incomplete cardiovascular risk picture, in patients with metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, or type 2 diabetes, and for refined risk stratification in patients with intermediate cardiovascular risk.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (well-validated in epidemiology; not in primary prevention guidelines; useful in metabolic syndrome).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Some labs report in nmol/L (multiply umol/L by 1000)</li>
<li>Women tend to have higher HDL-P than men (estrogen effect)</li>
<li>HDL-P does not have well-established age-specific cutoffs but generally declines with metabolic dysfunction</li>
<li>HDL-P and HDL-C can be discordant -- when discordant, HDL-P is the better prognostic marker</li>
<li>Lab-specific reference ranges vary</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase HDL-P (favorable):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Aerobic exercise</strong> (the most impactful intervention; 150 min/week)</li>
<li><strong>Moderate alcohol consumption</strong> (1-2 drinks/day; wine preferred)</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> 1-4 g/day EPA+DHA</li>
<li><strong>Niacin</strong> 1-3 g/day (also raises HDL-C)</li>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> in obese patients</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy</strong> (oral contraceptives, HRT)</li>
<li><strong>CETP deficiency</strong> (genetic)</li>
<li>** Mediterranean diet** rich in olive oil, fish, vegetables</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease HDL-P (unfavorable):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insulin resistance / metabolic syndrome</strong> (the most common cause)</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes</strong> (impaired HDL particle production and increased catabolism)</li>
<li><strong>Obesity</strong> (especially visceral adiposity)</li>
<li><strong>Sedentary lifestyle</strong></li>
<li><strong>Smoking</strong></li>
<li><strong>High-carbohydrate / refined sugar diets</strong></li>
<li><strong>Chronic inflammation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Anabolic steroid use</strong></li>
<li><strong>Beta-blockers, thiazides</strong> (chronic)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low HDL-P (&lt;28 umol/L) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular events:</strong> HDL-P predicts CV events even when HDL-C is normal (discordant pattern in metabolic syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Metabolic syndrome:</strong> insulin resistance drives low HDL-P with dysfunctional HDL</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes:</strong> diabetic dyslipidemia with low HDL-P, high small-dense LDL</li>
<li><strong>Atherosclerosis progression:</strong> low HDL-P impairs reverse cholesterol transport from arterial walls</li>
<li><strong>Carotid intima-media thickness, coronary calcium</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Discordance patterns (HDL-C vs HDL-P):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>High HDL-C / Low HDL-P:</strong> fewer, cholesterol-enriched particles; CV risk is higher than HDL-C suggests (insulin resistance pattern)</li>
<li><strong>Low HDL-C / High HDL-P:</strong> many small, cholesterol-poor but functional particles; risk is lower than HDL-C suggests</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Not in primary prevention guidelines</strong> (ACC/AHA, ESC) -- HDL-C remains the standard</li>
<li><strong>Limited insurance coverage</strong> -- often out-of-pocket cost (~$50-150)</li>
<li><strong>Lab-specific reference ranges</strong> -- interpret with care across labs</li>
<li><strong>HDL function vs concentration</strong> debate continues (HDL efflux capacity may be more important)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Advanced CV risk assessment in intermediate-risk patients</li>
<li>Patients with metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, T2DM</li>
<li>Refine risk when HDL-C is borderline or discordant</li>
<li>Monitor response to lifestyle interventions (exercise, omega-3)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>HDL-P responds to lifestyle and targeted supplements:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Aerobic exercise</strong> 150 min/week moderate intensity (or 75 min/week vigorous) -- raises HDL-P 5-15%</li>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> (5-10% body weight if obese) -- raises HDL-P via reduced insulin resistance</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> 1-4 g/day EPA+DHA -- modest HDL-P increase</li>
<li><strong>Niacin</strong> 1-3 g/day -- raises HDL-P (and HDL-C); limited by side effects (flushing, hyperglycemia) and lack of outcome benefit in statin era</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong> rich in olive oil, fish, vegetables</li>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong> -- raises HDL-P 5-10%</li>
<li><strong>Reduce refined carbohydrates</strong> and added sugars</li>
<li><strong>Moderate alcohol</strong> (1-2 drinks/day; wine preferred) -- balance against cancer risk</li>
<li><strong>Improve glycemic control</strong> in diabetics</li>
<li><strong>Treat hypothyroidism</strong> (low thyroid lowers HDL-P)</li>
</ol>
