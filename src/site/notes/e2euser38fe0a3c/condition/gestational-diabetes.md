<h1>Gestational Diabetes</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy, in a woman not previously diagnosed with diabetes. It affects <strong>5-15% of pregnancies worldwide</strong> (prevalence rising with obesity and advanced maternal age) and is driven by the physiologic insulin resistance of pregnancy: placental hormones -- <strong>human placental lactogen, progesterone, cortisol, prolactin, and TNF-alpha</strong> -- rise progressively through the second and third trimesters, antagonizing insulin action. In most women the pancreatic beta cells compensate by increasing insulin secretion 2-3 fold; GDM develops when this compensation fails, reflecting a pre-existing beta-cell defect often unmasked by the metabolic stress of pregnancy.</p>
<p>Untreated GDM is associated with significant maternal and fetal morbidity. Maternal complications include <strong>preeclampsia</strong> (2x risk), <strong>cesarean delivery</strong>, and <strong>polyhydramnios</strong>. Fetal complications stem from fetal hyperinsulinemia in response to maternal hyperglycemia, producing <strong>macrosomia</strong> (birthweight &gt;4 kg, from anabolic effects of insulin) with risk of <strong>shoulder dystocia</strong> and birth injury, <strong>neonatal hypoglycemia</strong>, <strong>hyperbilirubinemia</strong>, and <strong>respiratory distress syndrome</strong>. Perhaps the most important long-term consequence is that <strong>50% of women with GDM develop type 2 diabetes within 5-10 years</strong> of the index pregnancy, and offspring face higher lifetime risk of obesity and glucose intolerance -- making GDM a sentinel marker for the metabolic syndrome trajectory in the family.</p>
<p>Diagnosis follows either a <strong>one-step</strong> (75-g 2-hour OGTT at 24-28 weeks; IADPSG/WHO criteria) or <strong>two-step</strong> approach (50-g screening challenge, then 100-g 3-hour diagnostic OGTT; ACOG preferred). High-risk women should be screened at the first prenatal visit to detect undiagnosed pre-existing type 2 diabetes.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Lifestyle (first-line; controls 70-85% of cases):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Medical nutrition therapy</strong> -- carbohydrate-controlled diet (~40% of calories, spread across meals; low glycemic index; adequate protein and fiber; ~30 kcal/kg/day for normal weight)</li>
<li><strong>Moderate exercise</strong> -- 30 minutes of walking or prenatal exercise daily after meals reduces postprandial glucose</li>
<li><strong>Gestational weight gain</strong> per IOM guidelines (25-35 lb normal weight; 15-25 lb overweight; 11-20 lb obese)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Glucose monitoring and targets (ADA):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Fasting &lt;95 mg/dL (5.3 mmol/L)</li>
<li>1-hour postprandial &lt;140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L)</li>
<li>2-hour postprandial &lt;120 mg/dL (6.7 mmol/L)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pharmacotherapy when targets unmet within 1-2 weeks:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insulin</strong> -- preferred pharmacotherapy; does not cross placenta. Basal (detemir, NPH) + bolus (aspart, lispro) regimen; total ~0.7-1.0 U/kg/day in third trimester</li>
<li><strong>Metformin</strong> -- oral alternative (500-2000 mg/day); crosses placenta but short-term safety data reassuring; ~25% will still need insulin</li>
<li><strong>Glyburide</strong> -- higher failure rate and neonatal hypoglycemia; not preferred</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Intrapartum and postpartum:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Insulin requirements fall sharply at delivery; discontinue insulin and monitor neonatal glucose</li>
<li><strong>Postpartum 75-g OGTT at 6-12 weeks</strong> to exclude overt diabetes or prediabetes (up to 10% have persistent diabetes)</li>
<li>Counsel on <strong>lifelong diabetes risk</strong> and preventive lifestyle (weight loss, exercise, metformin if high risk); retest at least every 3 years</li>
</ul>
