<h1>Dementia</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Dementia is an umbrella term for an acquired, usually progressive decline in cognitive ability that is severe enough to interfere with independent daily functioning and occurs without clouding of consciousness (distinguishing it from delirium). The decline affects two or more cognitive domains — commonly memory, language, executive function, attention, visuospatial skills, and social behavior. It is a clinical syndrome with many underlying causes rather than a single disease.</p>
<p>The most common subtype is Alzheimer's disease, characterized pathologically by amyloid-beta plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles. Vascular dementia results from cerebrovascular disease and infarction; dementia with Lewy bodies features alpha-synuclein pathology, fluctuating cognition, and parkinsonism; and frontotemporal dementia preferentially affects behavior and language. Mixed pathology is common in older patients.</p>
<h2>Assessment</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is clinical, supported by cognitive testing (e.g., MMSE, MoCA), a collateral history of functional decline, and investigation to identify the subtype and exclude reversible contributors. Reversible or contributing factors that should be sought include vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, depression, medication effects, and elevated homocysteine. Structural imaging and, in selected cases, biomarkers of amyloid and tau help characterize the cause.</p>
<h2>Prevention and Management</h2>
<p>A substantial fraction of dementia risk is attributable to modifiable factors across the life course, including hypertension, diabetes, physical inactivity, smoking, hearing loss, and social isolation; addressing these reduces population risk. Management is largely supportive and symptomatic — cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine for some subtypes, treatment of vascular risk factors, caregiver support, and management of behavioral symptoms — with disease-modifying anti-amyloid therapies emerging for early Alzheimer's disease.</p>
