<h1>Kraft Test</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The Kraft Test, also known as the Glucose Tolerance with Insulin Response (GTIR) test, is a modified oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) that measures both glucose and insulin at multiple time points -- typically fasting, 30, 60, 120, and 180 minutes after a 75g or 100g glucose load. Unlike a standard OGTT that only tracks glucose, the Kraft Test captures the dynamic insulin response curve, enabling classification into five distinct patterns that reveal the stage of metabolic dysfunction.</p>
<p>The test was developed from the Kraft database, which contains over 10,000 serial insulin measurements from OGTTs collected by Dr. Joseph Kraft. Analysis of this dataset demonstrated that approximately 65% of individuals with normal glucose tolerance nonetheless had abnormal insulin response patterns (Patterns II-IV), meaning conventional glucose-based screening misses a substantial proportion of people with early insulin resistance (PMID: 27344544). The pathophysiological basis is that compensatory hyperinsulinemia maintains normoglycemia for years before glucose elevations become detectable.</p>
<p>Clinically, the Kraft Test is used in functional and integrative medicine for early metabolic risk detection. It identifies hyperinsulinemia in patients with normal fasting glucose and normal HbA1c -- a population that would be classified as metabolically healthy by standard screening but may already have significant insulin resistance. It is particularly valuable for evaluating PCOS, unexplained weight gain, reactive hypoglycemia, and family history of diabetes.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Pattern classification is based on the overall shape and magnitude of the insulin response curve, not a single cutoff value</li>
<li>The original Kraft database used a 100g glucose load; some modern implementations use 75g, which may shift insulin values</li>
<li>No universally standardized reference ranges exist across laboratories</li>
<li>Fasting insulin alone misses Patterns II and III -- serial measurements are essential for accurate classification</li>
<li>Insulin units may vary (mIU/L vs. uIU/mL vs. pmol/L); 1 mIU/L = 1 uIU/mL = 6.945 pmol/L</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Worsen Insulin Response Pattern (shift toward Patterns II-IV):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Obesity and visceral adiposity (increased free fatty acids and inflammatory cytokines impair insulin signaling)</li>
<li>Sedentary lifestyle (reduced GLUT4 translocation and muscle insulin sensitivity)</li>
<li>High-glycemic diet (chronic glycemic load drives compensatory hyperinsulinemia)</li>
<li>Chronic stress and elevated cortisol (promotes hepatic gluconeogenesis and peripheral insulin resistance)</li>
<li>Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) (insulin resistance is a core pathophysiological feature)</li>
<li>Family history of type 2 diabetes (genetic predisposition to beta-cell dysfunction)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Improve Insulin Response Pattern (shift toward Pattern I):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Regular aerobic and resistance exercise (increases muscle glucose uptake independent of insulin)</li>
<li>Low-glycemic or ketogenic diet (reduces demand for insulin secretion)</li>
<li>Weight loss of 5-10% body weight (significantly improves insulin sensitivity)</li>
<li>Adequate sleep (7-9 hours; sleep deprivation impairs glucose tolerance within days)</li>
<li>Berberine supplementation (activates AMPK, improves insulin sensitivity)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Abnormal patterns (II-IV) are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Early Insulin Resistance</strong> -- Pattern II identifies insulin resistance years before fasting glucose or HbA1c become abnormal. The Kraft database showed that 75% of individuals with normal 2-hour glucose had abnormal insulin patterns (PMID: 27344544)</li>
<li><strong>Prediabetes and Diabetes Risk</strong> -- Patterns III and IV indicate progressive beta-cell decompensation and significantly elevated risk of progression to type 2 diabetes</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular Risk</strong> -- hyperinsulinemia independently promotes atherosclerosis, hypertension, and dyslipidemia via sympathetic activation and sodium retention</li>
<li><strong>PCOS</strong> -- insulin resistance drives ovarian androgen production; the Kraft Test characterizes the severity of metabolic dysfunction in PCOS patients</li>
<li><strong>NAFLD</strong> -- hyperinsulinemia promotes hepatic de novo lipogenesis and steatosis; abnormal Kraft patterns correlate with fatty liver severity</li>
<li><strong>Reactive Hypoglycemia</strong> -- an exaggerated early insulin peak followed by rapid glucose drop (a variant of Pattern II) explains postprandial symptoms in normoglycemic patients</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pattern V (Insulinopenia) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Advanced beta-cell failure (late-stage type 2 diabetes or latent autoimmune diabetes)</li>
<li>Pancreatic insufficiency or damage</li>
<li>Requires urgent clinical evaluation for insulin therapy needs</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Early detection of insulin resistance in at-risk individuals (family history, obesity, PCOS)</li>
<li>Monitoring response to lifestyle or pharmacologic interventions targeting insulin sensitivity</li>
<li>Differentiating between hyperinsulinemia-driven and beta-cell failure-driven diabetes</li>
<li>Evaluating unexplained hypoglycemia or postprandial symptoms</li>
<li>Research tool for metabolic phenotyping and trajectory analysis (PMID: 29853473)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Low-glycemic nutrition</strong> -- a whole-food diet emphasizing protein, healthy fats, and complex carbohydrates with glycemic index &lt;55 reduces postprandial insulin demand and can shift Patterns II-III toward Pattern I within 8-12 weeks</li>
<li><strong>Regular exercise</strong> -- 150+ minutes per week of combined aerobic and resistance training improves muscle insulin sensitivity via GLUT4 upregulation, with measurable pattern improvement in 12-16 weeks</li>
<li><strong>Weight management</strong> -- 5-10% body weight loss reduces hepatic and muscular insulin resistance, often normalizing Pattern II to Pattern I</li>
<li><strong>Berberine supplementation</strong> -- 500 mg 2-3 times daily activates AMPK and improves insulin sensitivity comparably to metformin in some studies</li>
<li><strong>Time-restricted eating</strong> -- 14-16 hour daily fasting window reduces basal insulin levels and improves insulin sensitivity through metabolic switching</li>
<li><strong>Chromium supplementation</strong> -- 200-1000 mcg chromium picolinate daily may improve glucose tolerance in insulin-resistant individuals by enhancing insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity</li>
<li><strong>Alpha lipoic acid</strong> -- 600-1200 mg daily improves insulin-mediated glucose uptake via PI3K/Akt pathway activation and reduces oxidative stress</li>
</ol>
