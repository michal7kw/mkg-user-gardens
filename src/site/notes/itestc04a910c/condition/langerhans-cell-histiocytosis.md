<h1>Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare myeloid neoplastic disorder characterized by the <strong>clonal proliferation and accumulation of pathological Langerhans cells</strong> (CD1a+/CD207+/langerin+ dendritic cells) in one or more tissues, forming inflammatory granulomatous lesions that damage surrounding structures. Incidence is <strong>~5 per million children per year</strong> (most common in children 1-3 years) and ~1-2 per million in adults. Although historically classified as an inflammatory or &quot;histiocytic&quot; disease, modern genomics has reframed LCH as a <strong>neoplastic disease driven by somatic activating mutations in the MAPK pathway</strong> -- most commonly <strong>BRAF V600E</strong> (50-60%), and MAP2K1, ARAF, KRAS, or ERBB3 in others -- confirming it as a clonal myeloid neoplasm rather than a reactive process.</p>
<p>LCH exists on a spectrum from <strong>single-system disease</strong> (one organ involved; e.g., solitary <strong>eosinophilic granuloma</strong> of bone -- the most common and benign form) to <strong>multisystem disease</strong> affecting several organs with potential for life-threatening organ dysfunction (liver, spleen, bone marrow, lung). The historical eponyms -- <strong>eosinophilic granuloma</strong> (single-system bone), <strong>Hand-Schuller-Christian disease</strong> (triad of skull lesions, diabetes insipidus, exophthalmos), and <strong>Letterer-Siwe disease</strong> (fulminant multisystem disease in infants) -- are now grouped under the unifying LCH label, though they describe the same disease at different points on the severity continuum.</p>
<p>The most common presentations are <strong>bone lesions</strong> (skull, femur, pelvis; painful or incidental on imaging), <strong>skin rash</strong> (seborrheic-like scaly rash on scalp, diaper area, or trunk in infants), <strong>chronic otorrhea</strong> (ear involvement mimics chronic otitis media), and <strong>diabetes insipidus</strong> (hypothalamic-pituitary infiltration -- polyuria/polydipsia, often permanent). <strong>Pulmonary LCH</strong> in adults is strongly associated with smoking; high-resolution CT shows characteristic <strong>upper-lobe nodules and cysts</strong> with preserved costophrenic angles. <strong>Risk organ involvement</strong> (liver, spleen, bone marrow, lung in children) defines high-risk disease with worse prognosis; absence of risk-organ involvement defines low-risk disease.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Risk stratification</strong> (Histiocyte Society):</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Single-system LCH</strong> -- one organ/region; often indolent</li>
<li><strong>Multisystem LCH without risk-organ involvement</strong> -- lower risk; usually responds to therapy</li>
<li><strong>Multisystem LCH with risk-organ involvement</strong> (liver, spleen, bone marrow, lung) -- <strong>high risk</strong>; ~20% mortality in children, worse in infants</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Single-system disease:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Solitary bone lesion (eosinophilic granuloma)</strong> -- observation if asymptomatic, or <strong>curettage with intralesional corticosteroids</strong>, low-dose radiation (rarely), or percutaneous steroid injection</li>
<li><strong>Isolated skin LCH</strong> -- topical steroids, topical nitrogen mustard, photodynamic therapy, or observation</li>
<li><strong>Isolated pulmonary LCH (adults)</strong> -- <strong>smoking cessation is the primary therapy</strong>; many cases regress spontaneously. Corticosteroids reserved for progressive disease</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Multisystem disease:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>First-line chemotherapy</strong> -- <strong>cytarabine</strong> (the international standard since LCH-IV; reduces reactivation and long-term sequelae) OR <strong>cladribine</strong> (2-CdA) monotherapy. Vinblastine + prednisone is <strong>no longer the preferred frontline</strong> (high reactivation rate and neurotoxicity)</li>
<li><strong>BRAF inhibitors</strong> -- vemurafenib, dabrafenib for BRAF V600E-mutated refractory disease; rapid responses but high relapse on discontinuation, so used as a bridge to transplant or alongside MAPK-targeted strategies</li>
<li><strong>MEK inhibitors</strong> -- trametinib, cobimetinib for BRAF-wildtype disease with other MAPK mutations</li>
<li><strong>Hematopoietic stem cell transplant</strong> -- for refractory or relapsed multisystem disease</li>
<li><strong>MAPK pathway inhibition</strong> is rapidly becoming standard for relapsed/refractory disease</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Supportive and endocrine care:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Desmopressin (DDAVP)</strong> for diabetes insipidus (permanent in most cases; treat promptly)</li>
<li><strong>Hormone replacement</strong> for growth hormone deficiency, hypothyroidism, hypogonadism</li>
<li><strong>Orthopedic management</strong> of bone lesions (pathologic fracture prevention)</li>
<li><strong>Long-term surveillance</strong> for neurodegenerative LCH (cerebellar ataxia, cognitive decline; MRI surveillance) and secondary malignancies (myelodysplastic syndrome, solid tumors)</li>
</ul>
