<h1>Cervical Dysplasia</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cervical dysplasia, formally termed cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), describes precancerous changes in the squamous epithelium of the uterine cervix in which abnormal, immature cells replace the normal epithelial layers without yet invading the underlying basement membrane. It is caused almost universally by persistent infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly HPV-16 and HPV-18, which produce the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 that inactivate the p53 and Rb tumor-suppressor pathways and drive uncontrolled epithelial proliferation. Most HPV infections are transient and cleared by the immune system, but a minority persist and can establish dysplasia over months to years.</p>
<p>CIN is graded by how much of the epithelial thickness is occupied by dysplastic cells: CIN 1 (low-grade) involves the lower third and frequently regresses spontaneously, whereas CIN 2 and CIN 3 (high-grade) involve two-thirds or the full thickness and carry a substantial risk of progressing to invasive cancer if untreated. Because dysplasia is confined to the epithelium and produces no symptoms, it is detected through organized screening — cervical cytology (the Pap test) and high-risk HPV testing — with colposcopy and directed biopsy used to confirm and grade abnormalities. The biomarker p16, often combined with Ki-67, helps distinguish transforming high-risk HPV infection from reactive changes.</p>
<p>The clinical significance of cervical dysplasia is that it represents a long, detectable, and treatable window before cervical cancer develops. Primary prevention through HPV vaccination, combined with secondary prevention through screening and treatment of high-grade lesions, has made cervical cancer one of the most preventable malignancies, and the World Health Organization has set targets for its global elimination on this basis.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Cervical dysplasia is characteristically asymptomatic and is identified through screening rather than symptoms. When abnormalities are present, they may include postcoital or intermenstrual bleeding, usually associated with higher-grade lesions or coexisting infection. On colposcopy, dysplastic areas appear as acetowhite epithelium with abnormal vascular patterns.</p>

<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The necessary cause is persistent high-risk HPV infection; cofactors that promote persistence and progression include smoking, immunosuppression (notably HIV), early sexual debut and multiple partners, long-term oral contraceptive use, and absence of HPV vaccination or screening.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Low-grade (CIN 1) lesions are commonly managed by active surveillance with repeat cytology and HPV testing, given their high rate of spontaneous regression. High-grade lesions (CIN 2-3) are treated to prevent cancer, most often by excisional procedures such as loop electrosurgical excision (LEEP) or cone biopsy, with ablative methods used in selected cases. HPV vaccination provides primary prevention, and post-treatment surveillance with co-testing detects persistence or recurrence.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most important complication is progression of untreated high-grade dysplasia to invasive cervical cancer. Excisional treatment can cause cervical stenosis and is associated with a modestly increased risk of preterm birth in subsequent pregnancies, and dysplasia may persist or recur after treatment, necessitating follow-up.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis is excellent with screening and appropriate treatment: low-grade lesions frequently regress, and excision of high-grade lesions is highly effective at preventing cancer. Outcomes are worse where screening is unavailable or in immunosuppressed patients, in whom persistence and recurrence are more common.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>HPV infection is extremely common among sexually active people, and cervical dysplasia is correspondingly frequent, peaking in younger women. The burden of high-grade disease and cervical cancer is concentrated in regions with limited screening and vaccination access, where cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer death in women.</p>
