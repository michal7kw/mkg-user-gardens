<h1>Mild Cognitive Impairment</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) describes an acquired decline in one or more cognitive domains — most commonly memory — that is greater than expected for a person's age and education, yet is not severe enough to interfere with the functional independence that defines dementia. First formalized as a clinical entity by Petersen and colleagues, MCI occupies the conceptual space between normal cognitive aging and overt dementia, and it has become a central target for early intervention because it identifies people at substantially elevated risk before irreversible disability develops.</p>
<p>MCI is clinically and biologically heterogeneous. It is conventionally subtyped into <strong>amnestic MCI</strong> (memory-predominant, most likely to reflect underlying Alzheimer's disease pathology) and <strong>non-amnestic MCI</strong> (involving attention, executive function, language, or visuospatial domains, which may signal vascular, Lewy body, or frontotemporal processes). Outcomes are correspondingly variable: in clinical cohorts roughly 10-15% of people with MCI progress to dementia each year, but a substantial minority remain stable for years and 15-20% revert toward normal cognition, often when a reversible contributor is identified and treated.</p>
<p>The clinical importance of MCI lies in its actionability. It provides a window during which reversible causes (B12 deficiency, thyroid dysfunction, depression, obstructive sleep apnea, sedating medications) can be corrected, vascular and metabolic risk factors can be optimized, and biomarker testing can stratify who is on an Alzheimer's trajectory versus a non-progressive course. Multidomain lifestyle programs that combine exercise, diet, cognitive training, and vascular risk monitoring have demonstrated measurable cognitive benefit in at-risk older adults.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>People with MCI typically report — or have noted by a family member — increasing forgetfulness for recent events, conversations, and appointments, along with word-finding pauses, slowed thinking, and difficulty managing complex tasks such as finances or planning. Crucially, these changes are detectable on objective testing (commonly 1 to 1.5 standard deviations below age- and education-adjusted norms) while basic activities of daily living remain intact. Insight is often preserved early, which can cause distress. Progression, when it occurs, is gradual; abrupt or stepwise decline points toward a vascular contribution.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is clinical and follows the Petersen/International Working Group framework and the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA-AA) criteria for <em>MCI due to Alzheimer's disease</em>: (1) a concern about cognitive change, (2) objective impairment in at least one domain, (3) preserved functional independence, and (4) absence of dementia. Cognitive screening with instruments such as the MMSE or more sensitive batteries quantifies the deficit, while structural imaging excludes alternative pathology and assesses regional atrophy. Fluid and imaging biomarkers — CSF and plasma amyloid and phosphorylated-tau markers, and hippocampal volume on MRI — increasingly refine the likelihood that MCI reflects underlying Alzheimer's pathology, consistent with the biomarker cascade model of disease.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant risk factor is older age, amplified by the APOE e4 allele in Alzheimer's-type MCI. Modifiable contributors include hypertension, type 2 diabetes and insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, cerebrovascular disease, depression, physical inactivity, poor sleep, untreated hearing loss, and metabolic factors such as hyperhomocysteinemia and B12 deficiency. Low educational attainment and limited cognitive reserve lower the threshold at which underlying pathology becomes symptomatic.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is twofold. First, <strong>identify and treat reversible contributors</strong> — replete vitamin B12 and vitamin D, screen and treat thyroid disease, depression, and sleep apnea, and rationalize anticholinergic and sedating medications. Second, <strong>reduce progression risk through evidence-based lifestyle modification</strong>: regular aerobic and resistance exercise, a Mediterranean or MIND dietary pattern, structured cognitive training, and tight control of vascular risk factors. The FINGER randomized trial showed that a multidomain program combining these elements improved or maintained cognitive performance in at-risk older adults. No drug is currently approved specifically to halt MCI, so disease-monitoring and risk stratification — including biomarker testing where appropriate — remain core to ongoing care.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis depends heavily on subtype and biomarker status. Amnestic MCI with positive amyloid and tau biomarkers carries the highest annual conversion to Alzheimer's dementia, whereas biomarker-negative or non-amnestic presentations more often remain stable or improve. Because reversion to normal cognition is well documented, a single MCI diagnosis should prompt longitudinal follow-up rather than a fixed prognosis.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>MCI is common in older populations, with prevalence estimates generally in the range of 10-20% among adults aged 65 and older and rising steeply with age. It is more frequent in men in some cohorts and is strongly associated with vascular and metabolic comorbidity, making it a major public-health target as populations age.</p>
