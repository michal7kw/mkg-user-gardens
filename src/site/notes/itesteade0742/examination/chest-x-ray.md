<h1>Chest X-Ray</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The chest X-ray (chest radiograph, CXR) is a projectional radiographic examination of the thorax and the single most frequently performed imaging study in medicine. A brief exposure to a small dose of X-rays produces a two-dimensional image in which tissues are differentiated by their density: air-filled lungs appear dark, while bone, the heart, and consolidated or fluid-filled tissue appear lighter. Standard views are the posteroanterior (PA) and lateral projections, with anteroposterior (AP) portable films used for patients who cannot stand.</p>
<p>CXR is valued for being fast, inexpensive, widely available, and low-dose. It is a first-line test for evaluating respiratory and cardiac symptoms — detecting pneumonia, pulmonary edema, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, cardiomegaly, masses, and rib fractures — and for monitoring lines, tubes, and disease progression. Its limitations include lower sensitivity than CT for small or early lesions and the inherent ambiguity of overlapping structures on a 2D projection.</p>
<h2>Indications</h2>
<p>Common indications include cough, dyspnea, chest pain, fever with suspected pneumonia, trauma, suspected heart failure, preoperative assessment, and confirmation of device placement (endotracheal tubes, central lines, chest tubes). It is not the preferred test for definitive lung cancer screening (low-dose CT is used for that) but frequently raises the initial suspicion of a thoracic abnormality.</p>
<h2>Interpretation and Limitations</h2>
<p>Systematic reading covers airway, breathing (lung fields), cardiac silhouette, diaphragm, and everything else (bones, soft tissue, devices). Projectional overlap, patient rotation, and inspiration depth affect interpretation, and subtle or early disease may be missed; correlation with clinical findings and, when needed, cross-sectional imaging (CT) improves accuracy.</p>
