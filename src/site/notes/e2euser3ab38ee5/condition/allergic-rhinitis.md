<h1>Allergic Rhinitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a chronic <strong>IgE-mediated inflammatory disease of the nasal mucosa</strong> triggered by inhaled allergens in sensitized individuals. On re-exposure to an allergen, cross-linking of allergen-specific IgE on mast cells and basophils releases histamine and other mediators, producing the early-phase symptoms of sneezing, itching, and watery rhinorrhea, followed by a late-phase response dominated by eosinophilic inflammation and nasal congestion. It is one of the most common chronic conditions worldwide and a frequent reason for primary-care and allergy consultations.</p>
<p>AR is traditionally classified by timing into <strong>seasonal</strong> forms (driven by outdoor allergens such as tree, grass, and weed pollens) and <strong>perennial</strong> forms (driven by year-round indoor allergens such as house dust mites, animal dander, and molds). The Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma (ARIA) framework reclassifies the disease by symptom duration (intermittent vs persistent) and severity (mild vs moderate-severe), which better guides stepwise therapy. AR is a core component of the &quot;atopic march&quot; and commonly coexists with allergic conjunctivitis, asthma, and atopic dermatitis (eczema), reflecting a shared underlying type-2 immune predisposition.</p>
<p>Although rarely life-threatening, AR carries a substantial burden: it impairs sleep, concentration, and quality of life, reduces work and school productivity, and is strongly linked to asthma—the &quot;united airways&quot; concept holds that the upper and lower airways are a single functional unit, so treating rhinitis can improve asthma control. Diagnosis combines a careful history with confirmation of allergen-specific IgE, and management spans allergen avoidance, pharmacotherapy led by intranasal corticosteroids, and disease-modifying allergen immunotherapy.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The cardinal symptoms reflect mucosal inflammation and the nasal-ocular reflex:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sneezing</strong>: often paroxysmal, especially on first allergen contact</li>
<li><strong>Rhinorrhea</strong>: clear and watery</li>
<li><strong>Nasal congestion</strong>: predominates in persistent disease and disturbs sleep</li>
<li><strong>Pruritus</strong>: of the nose, palate, throat, and eyes</li>
<li><strong>Ocular involvement</strong>: itchy, red, watery eyes (allergic conjunctivitis)</li>
<li><strong>Pediatric signs</strong>: allergic shiners (infraorbital darkening), the transverse nasal crease from the &quot;allergic salute,&quot; and mouth breathing</li>
</ul>

<p>Sensitization (a positive IgE test) must correlate with clinical symptoms; positive tests without symptoms do not establish disease. Non-allergic and infectious rhinitis should be excluded.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The strongest determinant is <strong>atopy</strong>, with a personal or family history of allergic disease markedly increasing risk. Cumulative exposure to relevant aeroallergens, early-life environmental and microbial exposures, air pollution, and tobacco smoke all influence the development and severity of AR.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management follows a stepwise, severity-based approach. <strong>Allergen avoidance</strong> and environmental control come first. <strong>Intranasal corticosteroids</strong> are the most effective single agents and the first-line choice for moderate-to-severe or persistent disease. <strong>Antihistamines</strong> (oral or intranasal) relieve sneezing, itching, and rhinorrhea, and combination intranasal corticosteroid-antihistamine sprays are useful in more severe cases. <strong>Leukotriene receptor antagonists</strong> are an option in selected patients, particularly those with concomitant asthma. For inadequately controlled disease or to modify its natural course, <strong>allergen immunotherapy</strong> (subcutaneous or sublingual) can induce lasting tolerance.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Uncontrolled AR contributes to and worsens <strong>asthma</strong>, predisposes to <strong>chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps</strong>, and—particularly in children—to <strong>otitis media with effusion</strong>. The chronic symptom burden causes sleep disruption, daytime fatigue, and measurable declines in quality of life and academic or occupational performance.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>AR is generally a benign but chronic and relapsing condition. Symptoms can often be well controlled with appropriate pharmacotherapy, and allergen immunotherapy offers the prospect of long-term remission and reduced progression to asthma. Many children experience improvement over time, although a substantial proportion have persistent disease into adulthood.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Allergic rhinitis affects an estimated 10-30% of adults and an even higher proportion of children in many populations, and its prevalence has risen in recent decades, particularly in urbanizing and higher-income settings. It is a leading chronic respiratory disease globally and frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated.</p>
