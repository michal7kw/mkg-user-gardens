<h1>Ferritin</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Ferritin is a large, hollow, spherical protein complex (24 subunits of heavy and light chains) that sequesters iron in a soluble, non-toxic, biologically available form. Each ferritin molecule can store up to ~4,500 iron atoms as a ferric oxyhydroxide mineral core. Although the bulk of ferritin is intracellular—concentrated in hepatocytes, macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system, and bone marrow—a small amount is secreted into plasma in proportion to total body iron stores, which is what laboratories measure as &quot;serum ferritin.&quot;</p>
<p>Because serum ferritin correlates directly with mobilizable iron reserves, it is regarded as the single most useful and most sensitive non-invasive marker for diagnosing iron deficiency. A low serum ferritin is essentially diagnostic of depleted iron stores; no other condition lowers it. This high specificity for the low end makes ferritin the cornerstone of the diagnostic work-up for iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia, and the standard test for monitoring response to oral or intravenous iron replacement.</p>
<p>The principal interpretive caveat is that ferritin is also a positive acute-phase reactant. Inflammation, infection, chronic kidney disease, liver injury, and malignancy raise serum ferritin independently of iron status, so a &quot;normal&quot; or &quot;elevated&quot; ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency in an inflamed patient. For this reason ferritin is best interpreted alongside C-reactive protein and the rest of the iron panel (serum iron, transferrin saturation), and in inflammatory states a higher diagnostic threshold (e.g., &lt;70-100 ng/mL) or correction for acute-phase proteins is applied.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals are assay- and laboratory-dependent; immunoturbidimetric and chemiluminescent immunoassays predominate, and values are not fully harmonized across platforms.</li>
<li>Ferritin rises with age and is generally higher in men and postmenopausal women than in premenopausal women.</li>
<li>In the presence of inflammation (elevated CRP), apply a higher cut-off (≈70-100 ng/mL) or use the WHO/BRINDA correction factors to avoid missing iron deficiency.</li>
<li>Ferritin &gt;1,000 ng/mL warrants evaluation for hemochromatosis, hemophagocytic syndromes, adult-onset Still disease, or hepatic/malignant disease.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Ferritin:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Iron supplementation or repletion (replenishes stores)</li>
<li>Acute and chronic inflammation or infection (positive acute-phase reactant)</li>
<li>Liver disease and hepatocellular injury (release of stored ferritin)</li>
<li>Hereditary hemochromatosis and transfusional iron overload</li>
<li>Malignancy and metabolic syndrome / insulin resistance</li>
<li>Alcohol excess and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Ferritin:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Iron deficiency from inadequate dietary intake or malabsorption</li>
<li>Chronic blood loss (gastrointestinal bleeding, heavy menstruation)</li>
<li>Pregnancy (expanded plasma volume and fetal iron demand)</li>
<li>Vitamin C deficiency impairing dietary iron absorption (indirect)</li>
<li>Hypothyroidism and rapid growth periods (increased demand)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia</strong> — ferritin below ~30 ng/mL is the most reliable single test for depleted iron stores and the trigger to investigate the underlying cause (occult GI bleeding, menorrhagia, malabsorption).</li>
<li><strong>Restless legs and fatigue</strong> — low brain/body iron is implicated even before frank anemia develops.</li>
<li><strong>Impaired exercise performance and cognition</strong> — iron-dependent oxygen transport and mitochondrial enzyme function decline.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Inflammation and infection</strong> — as an acute-phase reactant, ferritin rises non-specifically; correlates with CRP.</li>
<li><strong>Iron overload (hemochromatosis, transfusion dependence)</strong> — sustained high ferritin with elevated transferrin saturation prompts genetic and hepatic evaluation.</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease</strong> — hepatocyte injury releases ferritin; common in NAFLD and alcoholic liver disease.</li>
<li><strong>Metabolic syndrome and malignancy</strong> — moderate elevations track with insulin resistance and tumor burden.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Confirming iron deficiency as the cause of anemia and guiding the need for endoscopic work-up.</li>
<li>Tracking response to oral or intravenous iron therapy.</li>
<li>Surveillance of iron overload in hemochromatosis and chronically transfused patients.</li>
<li>Distinguishing iron deficiency anemia from anemia of chronic disease (combined with CRP and transferrin saturation).</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>For <strong>low ferritin</strong> (most common abnormality):</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying cause</strong> — investigate and address sources of blood loss (GI evaluation, gynecologic causes) rather than supplementing indefinitely.</li>
<li><strong>Oral iron supplementation</strong> — elemental iron (e.g., ferrous sulfate ~65 mg) on alternate days improves absorption and tolerability; recheck ferritin after 8-12 weeks.</li>
<li><strong>Co-ingest vitamin C</strong> — ascorbic acid reduces ferric to ferrous iron and modestly enhances non-heme iron absorption when taken with meals.</li>
<li><strong>Optimize dietary iron</strong> — include heme iron (lean red meat, poultry, fish) and pair plant sources (legumes, leafy greens) with vitamin C; separate iron-rich meals from tea, coffee, and calcium.</li>
<li><strong>Intravenous iron</strong> — for malabsorption, intolerance, or severe deficiency, IV iron repletes stores rapidly and reliably.</li>
</ol>
<p>For <strong>high ferritin</strong> (manage cause, not the number):</p>
<ol start="6">
<li><strong>Evaluate for iron overload and inflammation</strong> — confirm with transferrin saturation and CRP; therapeutic phlebotomy is first-line for hereditary hemochromatosis, and chelation for transfusional overload.</li>
</ol>
