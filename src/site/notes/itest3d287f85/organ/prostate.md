<h1>Prostate</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The prostate is a walnut-sized exocrine gland of the male reproductive system situated immediately below the urinary bladder, encircling the proximal urethra. It is composed of glandular acini embedded in fibromuscular stroma and is organized into zones (peripheral, central, and transition) that are clinically important: prostate cancer arises most often in the peripheral zone, whereas benign prostatic hyperplasia develops in the transition zone surrounding the urethra, explaining the obstructive urinary symptoms of enlargement.</p>
<p>The prostate secretes a slightly acidic fluid rich in enzymes, citrate, and zinc that constitutes a substantial fraction of seminal volume. It produces prostate-specific antigen (PSA), a serine protease that liquefies the seminal coagulum and serves as the principal serum biomarker for prostate disease. Prostatic tissue concentrates zinc to among the highest levels in the body, reflecting its role in citrate metabolism.</p>
<h2>Hormonal Regulation</h2>
<p>Prostate growth and function are androgen-dependent. Circulating testosterone is converted within prostatic tissue to the more potent dihydrotestosterone by 5-alpha reductase; dihydrotestosterone drives both normal glandular maintenance and the hyperplasia of BPH, which is why 5-alpha reductase inhibitors reduce prostate volume.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Serum PSA is used in the detection and monitoring of prostate disease, though it is organ-specific rather than cancer-specific and is elevated by benign enlargement, inflammation, and instrumentation. Digital rectal examination, multiparametric MRI, and biopsy complement PSA. The two dominant age-related conditions are benign prostatic hyperplasia (lower urinary tract symptoms) and prostate cancer.</p>
