<h1>Tobacco Use Disorder</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Tobacco use disorder is a chronic relapsing condition defined by <strong>compulsive tobacco seeking and use despite harm</strong>, driven by <strong>nicotine dependence</strong>. Nicotine acts on <strong>alpha-4 beta-2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors</strong> in the ventral tegmental area, releasing dopamine in the nucleus accumbens and establishing the <strong>reward circuit</strong> that underpins addiction. With repeated exposure, receptor upregulation, counter-adaptation, and associative learning produce tolerance, withdrawal, and craving -- the core DSM-5 criteria of tobacco use disorder.</p>
<p>Tobacco is the <strong>single largest preventable cause of death worldwide</strong>, killing <strong>&gt;8 million people each year</strong> (including ~1.2 million from secondhand smoke). Smoking causes <strong>~85% of lung cancers</strong>, <strong>~70% of COPD</strong>, and substantially increases risk of <strong>coronary heart disease, stroke, and &gt;15 other cancers</strong> (head and neck, bladder, cervix, pancreas, kidney, stomach). On average, <strong>long-term smokers lose about 10 years of life</strong>; quitting at any age substantially reduces risk -- quitting by age 40 avoids ~90% of the excess mortality.</p>
<p>The clinical presentation is dominated by the <strong>addiction syndrome</strong>: strong cravings, withdrawal symptoms (irritability, anxiety, restlessness, depressed mood, difficulty concentrating, increased appetite, insomnia) within hours of abstinence, tolerance, failed quit attempts, and continued use despite knowledge of harm. Quitting is hard: unaided 6-month abstinence rates are only ~3-5%, but combining pharmacotherapy with behavioral support raises this to <strong>20-30%</strong>. Tobacco use disorder frequently co-occurs with mental health disorders (depression, schizophrenia, bipolar, ADHD) and other substance use disorders.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Screening and brief intervention (every clinical encounter) -- the &quot;5 As&quot;:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Ask</strong> about tobacco use</li>
<li><strong>Advise</strong> to quit (clear, personalized)</li>
<li><strong>Assess</strong> willingness to quit</li>
<li><strong>Assist</strong> with counseling and pharmacotherapy</li>
<li><strong>Arrange</strong> follow-up</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>First-line pharmacotherapy (all roughly double quit rates vs placebo):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Varenicline (Chantix)</strong> -- partial agonist at alpha-4 beta-2 receptor; reduces craving and reward. <strong>Most effective single agent</strong> (OR ~2.9 vs placebo; meta-analysis). Dose titrated 0.5 mg daily x3 days, then BID, to 1 mg BID for 12 weeks. Neuropsychiatric warning (depression, suicidality) has been substantially de-emphasized by EAGLES trial</li>
<li><strong>Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT)</strong> -- combine <strong>long-acting patch</strong> (21 mg, taper) with <strong>short-acting</strong> (gum, lozenge, inhaler, nasal spray) for breakthrough craving. Combining forms is more effective than monotherapy</li>
<li><strong>Bupropion (Zyban)</strong> -- 150 mg daily x3 days, then BID for 7-12 weeks. Reduces craving via dopamine/norepinephrine. <strong>Avoid in seizure disorders, eating disorders, MAOI use</strong></li>
<li><strong>Cytisine</strong> -- partial agonist (like varenicline); low cost; available in Eastern Europe and increasingly elsewhere; ~3-6x more effective than placebo</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Combination therapy</strong> (e.g., varenicline + NRT patch, or NRT patch + short-acting) improves outcomes over monotherapy in heavy smokers.</p>
<p><strong>Behavioral support:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cognitive behavioral therapy</strong> (identify triggers, coping strategies)</li>
<li><strong>Motivational interviewing</strong> for those not ready to quit</li>
<li><strong>Quitlines</strong> (telephone counseling; free in most countries)</li>
<li><strong>Digital support</strong> -- apps, SMS programs (e.g., SmokefreeTXT)</li>
<li><strong>Group support</strong> and peer counseling</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Harm reduction (for those who cannot quit):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Reducing cigarettes per day</strong> (still harmful; less effective than complete cessation)</li>
<li><strong>Nicotine-containing e-cigarettes</strong> -- controversial; helps some smokers quit but carries its own risks and youth uptake concerns</li>
<li><strong>Smokeless tobacco / snus</strong> -- lower lung-cancer risk but increases oral, pancreatic, esophageal, and cardiovascular risk</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Population-level interventions (most effective at reducing smoking prevalence):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Tobacco taxation (price elasticity ~-0.4)</li>
<li>Smoke-free work and public spaces</li>
<li>Plain packaging and graphic warning labels</li>
<li>Advertising bans and counter-marketing</li>
<li>Mass-media campaigns</li>
</ul>
