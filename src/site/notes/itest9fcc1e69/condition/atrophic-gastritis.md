<h1>Atrophic Gastritis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Atrophic gastritis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the stomach lining in which the normal secretory glands of the gastric mucosa are progressively destroyed and replaced by fibrous tissue and intestinal-type epithelium (intestinal metaplasia). The net consequence is a thinned, atrophic mucosa with reduced or absent acid secretion (hypochlorhydria or achlorhydria) and, in corpus-predominant disease, loss of intrinsic factor needed for vitamin B12 absorption. It is best understood not as a single entity but as a final common pathway reached through different etiologies.</p>
<p>Two dominant mechanisms drive the condition. The most common worldwide is <strong>chronic Helicobacter pylori infection</strong>, which begins as a non-atrophic gastritis and, over years, can advance through the Correa cascade — chronic gastritis to atrophy, to intestinal metaplasia, to dysplasia, and ultimately to intestinal-type gastric adenocarcinoma. The second is <strong>autoimmune metaplastic atrophic gastritis (AMAG)</strong>, in which autoantibodies target gastric parietal cells and intrinsic factor, selectively destroying the acid-secreting corpus and fundus while sparing the antrum. AMAG produces marked hypergastrinemia, enterochromaffin-like cell hyperplasia, and a risk of type 1 gastric carcinoids.</p>
<p>Clinically, atrophic gastritis matters mostly because it is silent yet consequential. Many patients have no digestive symptoms and present instead with the downstream effects of malabsorption — iron-deficiency or vitamin B12 (pernicious) anemia — or are detected incidentally on endoscopy. Because advanced atrophy and intestinal metaplasia confer an elevated risk of gastric malignancy, accurate histologic staging (OLGA/OLGIM) and, where indicated, endoscopic surveillance are central to management.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The condition is frequently asymptomatic and may be discovered only when complications appear. When present, symptoms are nonspecific: dyspepsia, epigastric discomfort, early satiety, and bloating. Hematologic and neurologic features often dominate the picture — fatigue and pallor from anemia, glossitis, and, in B12 deficiency, paresthesias, gait disturbance, or cognitive changes. The autoimmune subtype is commonly diagnosed during the workup of unexplained macrocytic anemia or in patients already known to have other autoimmune disorders.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is histologic. Upper endoscopy with systematically mapped biopsies (Sydney system, staged by OLGA/OLGIM) establishes the presence, distribution, and severity of glandular atrophy and intestinal metaplasia. Non-invasive serology supports the diagnosis: low pepsinogen I and a low pepsinogen I/II ratio reflect corpus atrophy, while elevated fasting gastrin indicates loss of acid-mediated feedback. Anti-parietal-cell and anti-intrinsic-factor antibodies identify the autoimmune subtype, and testing for active H. pylori (stool antigen, urea breath test, or biopsy) guides eradication therapy.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The principal risk factor is chronic H. pylori infection; eradication can halt or partially reverse early atrophy. Autoimmune disease, older age, a family history of gastric cancer, dietary salt and nitrates, and smoking further increase risk. Autoimmune atrophic gastritis clusters with other organ-specific autoimmune conditions such as autoimmune thyroid disease and type 1 diabetes.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management targets the cause and the consequences. When H. pylori is present, eradication is first-line and may slow progression. Micronutrient deficiencies are corrected — oral or intravenous iron and lifelong parenteral (or high-dose oral) vitamin B12 in autoimmune disease. Because of malignant potential, patients with advanced-stage atrophy or extensive intestinal metaplasia are entered into endoscopic surveillance programs at intervals informed by histologic stage.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most clinically important complications are nutritional and neoplastic. Hypochlorhydria impairs iron absorption (iron-deficiency anemia), and loss of intrinsic factor causes vitamin B12 malabsorption with pernicious anemia and potential irreversible neurologic damage. Over the long term, atrophy with intestinal metaplasia and dysplasia raises the risk of gastric adenocarcinoma, and the hypergastrinemia of autoimmune disease predisposes to type 1 gastric neuroendocrine tumors.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis depends on stage and etiology. Early H. pylori-associated atrophy may stabilize or partially regress after eradication, whereas autoimmune atrophic gastritis is generally irreversible and requires lifelong nutritional support and surveillance. With appropriate monitoring, the lifetime cancer risk — though elevated relative to the general population — can be mitigated through early detection of dysplasia.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Atrophic gastritis is common globally and rises in prevalence with age and with the regional burden of H. pylori. It is most frequent in East Asian, Andean, and Eastern European populations where gastric cancer incidence is also high. The autoimmune subtype is less common but is increasingly recognized, particularly in women and in individuals with coexisting autoimmune disease.</p>
