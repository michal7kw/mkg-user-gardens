<h1>Troponin</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cardiac troponin is a complex of three regulatory proteins (troponin C, troponin I, and troponin T) bound to the thin filament of the cardiac sarcomere, where it controls the calcium-mediated interaction between actin and myosin during contraction. The two cardiac-specific subunits — cardiac troponin I (cTnI) and cardiac troponin T (cTnT) — are encoded by genes distinct from their skeletal-muscle counterparts, which makes them highly specific markers of cardiomyocyte injury. When myocardial cells are damaged, troponin is released into the bloodstream, where it can be quantified to detect and grade the extent of injury.</p>
<p>Under normal conditions, circulating troponin is very low or undetectable. Following myocardial injury, levels begin to rise within 1–3 hours, peak at roughly 12–48 hours, and can remain elevated for up to 7–14 days (cTnT often longer than cTnI). The introduction of high-sensitivity (hs-cTn) assays has dramatically improved early detection, enabling reliable measurement at the 99th-percentile upper reference limit and supporting rapid 0/1-hour and 0/2-hour rule-out and rule-in algorithms in patients presenting with chest pain. A rising and/or falling pattern on serial sampling, together with clinical context and ischemic findings, distinguishes acute myocardial infarction from chronic, stable troponin elevation.</p>
<p>Troponin is one of the most ordered cardiac biomarkers in acute care. It is the cornerstone of the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, which requires a rise and/or fall of troponin with at least one value above the 99th percentile plus evidence of ischemia. Beyond acute coronary syndromes, troponin is elevated in a wide range of non-ischemic conditions, so interpretation always demands clinical correlation. It is increasingly used not only for diagnosis but also for prognosis and risk stratification, where even modest chronic elevations predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference ranges are <strong>assay-specific</strong>; conventional and high-sensitivity assays use different units (ng/mL vs ng/L) and cannot be compared directly.</li>
<li>Sex-specific 99th-percentile thresholds (lower for women) are recommended for hs-cTn assays and may improve diagnostic accuracy.</li>
<li>A single value above the threshold indicates myocardial injury but not necessarily infarction — the <strong>dynamic change</strong> on serial testing is what supports an acute MI diagnosis.</li>
<li>There is no single universally standardized cutoff across all manufacturers; clinicians should use the local laboratory's validated reference limits.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Troponin:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute myocardial infarction (ischemic necrosis of cardiomyocytes)</li>
<li>Myocarditis and pericarditis (inflammatory myocardial injury)</li>
<li>Heart failure decompensation (myocardial strain and subendocardial ischemia)</li>
<li>Pulmonary embolism (right-ventricular strain)</li>
<li>Sepsis and critical illness (demand ischemia, cytokine-mediated injury)</li>
<li>Chronic kidney disease (reduced clearance and subclinical myocardial injury)</li>
<li>Tachyarrhythmias, hypertensive emergency, and strenuous endurance exercise</li>
<li>Cardiac procedures (PCI, ablation, cardioversion) and blunt cardiac trauma</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Troponin:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Resolution of the acute injury and myocardial recovery over days</li>
<li>Reperfusion therapy limiting infarct size when delivered early</li>
<li>Cardioprotective strategies during cardiac surgery (e.g., perioperative coenzyme Q10)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Myocardial Infarction</strong> — a rising/falling troponin above the 99th percentile with ischemic symptoms or ECG/imaging changes defines acute MI; magnitude correlates with infarct size.</li>
<li><strong>Heart Failure</strong> — chronic low-grade elevation reflects ongoing myocyte injury and is a powerful predictor of mortality and rehospitalization.</li>
<li><strong>Coronary Artery Disease</strong> — even intermediate hs-cTnI values identify patients at increased short-term risk of major adverse cardiovascular events.</li>
<li><strong>Sepsis</strong> — myocardial injury during sepsis carries worse prognosis and longer ICU stays.</li>
<li><strong>Chronic Kidney Disease</strong> — stable elevation is common and must be interpreted with serial sampling to avoid misdiagnosis of MI.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Rapid rule-in/rule-out of acute coronary syndrome using 0/1-hour or 0/2-hour algorithms.</li>
<li>Risk stratification of chest-pain and heart-failure patients.</li>
<li>Detection of perioperative and procedural myocardial injury.</li>
<li>Quantifying cardiotoxicity in patients on cardiotoxic chemotherapy.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Interpretation caveat:</strong> Troponin is highly sensitive but its elevation is not specific to MI. The Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction explicitly distinguishes acute MI (with a dynamic ischemic pattern) from non-ischemic myocardial injury, and the appropriateness of that definition remains an area of active debate in the cardiology literature.</p>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>For troponin, &quot;improvement&quot; means minimizing myocardial injury and keeping levels low — it is driven by cardiovascular disease management rather than direct supplementation.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Optimize cardiovascular risk factors</strong> — control blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, and glycemia to reduce ischemic burden and chronic troponin release.</li>
<li><strong>Early reperfusion in acute MI</strong> — timely PCI or thrombolysis limits infarct size and thereby peak troponin.</li>
<li><strong>Guideline-directed heart-failure therapy</strong> — beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, and SGLT2 inhibitors reduce myocardial strain and chronic injury.</li>
<li><strong>Perioperative cardioprotection</strong> — high-dose ubiquinone (coenzyme Q10) before cardiac surgery has been shown in a randomized trial to lower postoperative hs-cTnI compared with placebo.</li>
<li><strong>Manage comorbidities</strong> — treat sepsis, anemia, and renal dysfunction promptly to limit demand-related and clearance-related elevations.</li>
<li><strong>Lifestyle measures</strong> — smoking cessation, regular moderate aerobic activity, and a Mediterranean-style diet support long-term cardiovascular health and lower baseline injury markers.</li>
</ol>
