<h1>Growth Hormone</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Growth Hormone (GH, somatotropin) is a 191-amino acid single-chain peptide hormone synthesized and secreted by somatotroph cells, which make up roughly 30-40% of the anterior pituitary. It is the principal endocrine driver of postnatal linear growth and a master regulator of body composition and intermediary metabolism throughout life. Although named for its role in childhood growth, GH remains metabolically active in adults, where it preserves lean mass, regulates fat distribution, and supports bone density, cardiac function, and quality of life.</p>
<p>GH acts through two complementary mechanisms. Many of its growth-promoting (anabolic) effects are indirect, mediated by insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), which GH stimulates the liver and peripheral tissues to produce. Other effects — notably lipolysis and the suppression of glucose uptake — are direct, occurring at the GH receptor on adipocytes, muscle, and liver. This dual action makes GH a key player in nutrient partitioning: during fasting it mobilizes fat for fuel and spares protein and glucose, while in the fed state it cooperates with insulin and IGF-1 to favor anabolism.</p>
<p>GH secretion is pulsatile, with 7-12 bursts per 24 hours and the largest pulse occurring 30-60 minutes after sleep onset during slow-wave sleep; 60-70% of daily output is released during sleep. Because circulating levels swing widely between pulses, a single random GH measurement is clinically uninformative — IGF-1 (which is stable) and dynamic stimulation or suppression tests are used instead. Both deficiency (short stature in children, reduced lean mass and impaired well-being in adults) and excess (gigantism in children, acromegaly in adults) cause well-defined clinical syndromes.</p>
<h2>Physiology and Regulation</h2>
<p>GH release is governed by an integrated hypothalamic-pituitary axis. Growth Hormone-Releasing Hormone (GHRH) from the arcuate nucleus stimulates synthesis and secretion, while somatostatin (from the periventricular nucleus) inhibits release; the alternating dominance of these two peptides generates the characteristic pulsatile pattern. Ghrelin, secreted mainly by the stomach, potently amplifies GH release by acting as a functional antagonist of somatostatin and a potentiator of GHRH. Negative feedback is provided by IGF-1, which acts at both the hypothalamus (increasing somatostatin tone) and the pituitary, and by GH itself.</p>
<p>Secretion is strongly modulated by physiological state. It rises with deep sleep, exercise, fasting, hypoglycemia, and amino acid intake (especially arginine), and is suppressed by hyperglycemia, free fatty acid elevation, and chronic glucocorticoid excess. GH output declines progressively with age (&quot;somatopause&quot;), paralleling the age-related fall in lean mass and bone density.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>GH binds a single transmembrane GH receptor (GHR), inducing receptor dimerization and activation of the associated JAK2 tyrosine kinase. JAK2 phosphorylates STAT5 (principally STAT5b), which translocates to the nucleus to drive transcription of target genes, foremost among them IGF-1 and acid-labile subunit. Parallel signaling through the MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways mediates additional metabolic and proliferative effects. In the liver, this cascade produces the bulk of circulating IGF-1; in adipose tissue, GH directly promotes lipolysis and reduces lipoprotein lipase activity; in muscle and liver it antagonizes insulin action, accounting for GH's diabetogenic, &quot;anti-insulin&quot; tendency when chronically elevated.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Deficiency: In children, GH deficiency causes growth failure and short stature; in adults it produces increased visceral adiposity, reduced lean and bone mass, dyslipidemia, impaired exercise capacity, and diminished quality of life. Treatment with recombinant human GH (daily injections, or newer once-weekly long-acting analogs such as lonapegsomatropin) restores body composition and metabolic parameters.</p>
<p>Excess: Chronic GH oversecretion — almost always from a somatotroph pituitary adenoma — causes gigantism before epiphyseal closure and acromegaly in adults (enlarged hands, feet, and facial features; arthropathy; insulin resistance and diabetes; cardiomyopathy; sleep apnea; and increased mortality if untreated). Because GH opposes insulin, both deficiency and excess perturb glucose metabolism, and acromegaly is a recognized cause of secondary diabetes.</p>
<p>Diagnostics rely on IGF-1 as a screening surrogate, GH suppression after oral glucose (to confirm excess), and GH stimulation testing (to confirm deficiency), reflecting the unreliability of random GH levels.</p>
