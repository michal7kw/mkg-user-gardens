<h1>Postpartum Thyroiditis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Postpartum thyroiditis (PPT) is an <strong>autoimmune inflammatory disorder of the thyroid</strong> that develops within the <strong>first year after childbirth</strong> (or after miscarriage), affecting approximately <strong>5-10% of postpartum women</strong> overall and <strong>30-50% of women who are TPO-antibody positive</strong> in early pregnancy. The pathophysiology is a <strong>rebound of autoimmune activity</strong> after the relative immunosuppression of pregnancy (the &quot;immunologic rebound&quot; hypothesis) -- during pregnancy, T-helper-2 skewing suppresses Th1-mediated autoimmunity, and delivery removes this suppression, allowing pre-existing thyroid-directed autoimmunity to flare. Histologically there is a <strong>lymphocytic infiltration</strong> of the thyroid identical to Hashimoto thyroiditis.</p>
<p>The classic clinical course has <strong>three phases</strong> (though not all patients experience all three):</p>
<ol>
<li>
<p><strong>Thyrotoxic phase (1-4 months postpartum)</strong> -- the inflamed thyroid releases pre-formed thyroid hormone, producing a <strong>transient thyrotoxicosis</strong> (~25-30% of patients). Symptoms include <strong>palpitations, anxiety, heat intolerance, weight loss, irritability, and fatigue</strong>. This phase is often misdiagnosed as Graves disease or attributed to postpartum anxiety. Differentiation from Graves: PPT has <strong>absent thyroid receptor antibodies (TRAb)</strong> and <strong>low radioactive iodine uptake</strong> (destructive release vs synthesis). The thyrotoxic phase is typically mild and self-limited</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Hypothyroid phase (4-8 months postpartum)</strong> -- the depleted thyroid cannot synthesize adequate hormone, producing a <strong>transient hypothyroidism</strong> (~40-50% of patients). Symptoms: <strong>fatigue, weight gain, depression, cold intolerance, dry skin, constipation, impaired concentration</strong>. This is the phase most likely to be symptomatic and to require treatment. <strong>Overlap with postpartum depression</strong> is common and the two must be distinguished</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Recovery phase (6-12 months postpartum)</strong> -- thyroid function normalizes in ~<strong>70-80%</strong> of patients, but <strong>20-30% develop permanent hypothyroidism</strong> (typically Hashimoto thyroiditis)</p>
</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Diagnosis</strong> requires a <strong>high index of suspicion</strong> and <strong>TSH monitoring</strong> in symptomatic postpartum women or TPO-positive women. The clinical importance is twofold: (1) the <strong>thyrotoxic phase is frequently misdiagnosed and overtreated with antithyroid drugs</strong> (which are ineffective in destructive thyroiditis); and (2) the <strong>hypothyroid phase overlaps with postpartum depression</strong>, and untreated hypothyroidism can worsen depressive symptoms and impair maternal-infant bonding.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Diagnosis and monitoring:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>TSH and free T4</strong> at 3 and 6 months postpartum in <strong>TPO-positive women</strong> (universal screening is debated; targeted screening of high-risk women is cost-effective)</li>
<li>Measure <strong>TSH and free T4</strong> in any symptomatic postpartum woman</li>
<li><strong>Thyroid receptor antibodies (TRAb)</strong> to differentiate thyrotoxic phase from Graves disease (TRAb positive in Graves, negative in PPT)</li>
<li><strong>24-hour radioactive iodine uptake (RAIU)</strong> -- low in PPT (destructive release), high in Graves (synthesis). Often impractical in nursing women; TRAb usually suffices</li>
<li><strong>Annual TSH for life</strong> -- women with PPT have <strong>~25-50% lifetime risk</strong> of permanent hypothyroidism</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Treatment by phase:</strong></p>
<p><strong>Thyrotoxic phase (1-4 months postpartum):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Observation alone</strong> if mild and asymptomatic (most cases)</li>
<li><strong>Beta-blockers</strong> (propranolol 20-40 mg as needed) for symptomatic palpitations, anxiety, tremor. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration. <strong>Avoid atenolol</strong> in breastfeeding (concentrates in milk; propranolol and metoprolol are preferred)</li>
<li><strong>Antithyroid drugs (methimazole, PTU) are NOT effective</strong> -- the thyrotoxicosis is from hormone release, not synthesis. PTU is preferred if an antithyroid is mistakenly considered (safer in lactation), but the correct approach is to not use them</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroids</strong> (prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/day for 2-4 weeks) for severe painful thyroiditis; rarely needed</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Hypothyroid phase (4-8 months postpartum):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Levothyroxine</strong> for symptomatic patients, those with TSH &gt;10 mIU/L, those planning pregnancy, or those with significant depression/anxiety. Dose 1.6 mcg/kg/day; titrate to TSH in normal range (0.4-4.0 mIU/L; target ~1-2 for fertility)</li>
<li><strong>Trial of levothyroxine discontinuation at 12 months postpartum</strong> in most patients -- recheck TSH 6-8 weeks after stopping; resume permanently if TSH rises</li>
<li><strong>Continue levothyroxine permanently</strong> for: women planning another pregnancy, those with TSH &gt;10-20 mIU/L at diagnosis, persistent hypothyroidism at 12 months, or strong family history</li>
<li><strong>Breastfeeding is safe</strong> on levothyroxine (minimal transfer to milk)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive therapies:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Selenium</strong> (200 mcg/day) -- limited evidence that selenium supplementation during pregnancy and postpartum reduces PPT incidence and progression to permanent hypothyroidism in TPO-positive women; reasonable to offer</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> -- correct deficiency (common in postpartum; associated with autoimmune thyroid disease)</li>
<li><strong>Treat postpartum depression</strong> with SSRIs if present (do not assume thyroid alone)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Counseling for future pregnancies:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>70% recurrence rate</strong> in subsequent pregnancies -- monitor TSH at each trimester and 3 and 6 months postpartum</li>
<li><strong>Preconception counseling</strong> -- ensure euthyroid before conception (TSH &lt;2.5 mIU/L); adjust levothyroxine early in pregnancy (typically +25-30% dose increase)</li>
<li><strong>Lifetime annual TSH</strong> monitoring for permanent hypothyroidism risk</li>
</ul>
