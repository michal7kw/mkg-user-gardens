<h1>Cocaine-Related Disorders</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cocaine is a powerful <strong>central nervous system stimulant</strong> extracted from the leaves of the coca plant (<em>Erythroxylum coca</em>). It acts by <strong>blocking reuptake of dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin</strong> at synaptic terminals -- primarily by binding to the dopamine active transporter (DAT). The resulting synaptic dopamine accumulation in the mesolimbic reward pathway produces the characteristic <strong>intense euphoria</strong> (&quot;rush&quot;), increased energy, confidence, and alertness that drives repeated use.</p>
<p><strong>Routes of administration:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Intranasal (snorting):</strong> onset 3-5 min; duration 15-30 min; causes nasal septum perforation with chronic use</li>
<li><strong>Intravenous (injection):</strong> onset seconds; duration 15-30 min; highest overdose risk; HIV/HCV transmission</li>
<li><strong>Smoked (crack/freebase):</strong> onset 8-10 sec; duration 5-10 min; most rapid and intense effects; highest addiction potential</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Clinical dangers of cocaine use:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular (most common cause of cocaine-related death):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Coronary vasospasm</strong> (even in young, otherwise healthy users; can cause acute MI without atherosclerosis)</li>
<li><strong>Aortic dissection</strong> (from severe hypertension)</li>
<li><strong>Arrhythmias</strong> (from sodium channel blockade and sympathetic excess)</li>
<li><strong>Hypertensive emergency</strong></li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Neurological:</strong> ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke; seizures</li>
<li><strong>Psychiatric:</strong> stimulant psychosis (paranoia, hallucinations; indistinguishable from schizophrenia)</li>
<li><strong>Infectious:</strong> HIV, HCV, endocarditis (from injection use)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> placental abruption, premature labor, fetal growth restriction</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The &quot;cocaine chest pain&quot; clinical challenge:</strong><br />
Cocaine-associated chest pain accounts for ~40% of all cocaine-related ED visits. The mechanism involves <strong>coronary vasospasm, thrombosis, increased myocardial oxygen demand (from tachycardia and hypertension), and accelerated atherosclerosis</strong>. The treatment paradox: <strong>pure beta-blockers are contraindicated</strong> (unopposed alpha stimulation can worsen vasospasm and hypertension). Instead, <strong>benzodiazepines</strong> (reduce sympathetic outflow) and <strong>nitroglycerin</strong> (vasodilation) are first-line.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Acute cocaine intoxication:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Benzodiazepines</strong> are first-line for agitation, anxiety, hypertension, and chest pain:
<ul>
<li>Lorazepam 2-4 mg IV q10min PRN (titrate to calm)</li>
<li>Diazepam 5-10 mg IV q10min PRN</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Cocaine-associated chest pain:</strong> benzodiazepines + <strong>nitroglycerin</strong> (sublingual or IV) + <strong>aspirin</strong> 325 mg</li>
<li><strong>Nitroglycerin</strong> and <strong>calcium channel blockers</strong> (verapamil, diltiazem) for coronary vasospasm</li>
<li><strong>Phentolamine</strong> (alpha-blocker) for refractory hypertension</li>
<li><strong>AVOID pure beta-blockers</strong> (propranolol, metoprolol) -- unopposed alpha stimulation -&gt; worsened hypertension and coronary vasospasm</li>
<li><strong>Mixed alpha/beta blockers</strong> (labetalol, carvedilol) may be used with caution</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cocaine withdrawal:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Primarily supportive; not life-threatening (unlike alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal)</li>
<li>Depression, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, vivid dreams (&quot;crash&quot;)</li>
<li>Treat symptomatically (SSRIs for depression; trazodone for sleep; avoid stimulants)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cocaine use disorder treatment:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Contingency management</strong> (most evidence-based; voucher-based reinforcement of abstinence)</li>
<li><strong>Cognitive behavioral therapy</strong> (identifies triggers; develops coping strategies)</li>
<li><strong>Community reinforcement approach</strong> (CRA)</li>
<li><strong>12-step programs</strong> (Cocaine Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous)</li>
<li><strong>No FDA-approved medications</strong> (investigational: disulfiram, topiramate, modafinil, baclofen, propranolol for stress-induced craving)</li>
<li><strong>Emerging:</strong> cocaine vaccine (TA-CD; stimulates anti-cocaine antibodies); ketamine (under investigation)</li>
</ul>
