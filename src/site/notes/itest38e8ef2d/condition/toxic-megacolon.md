<h1>Toxic Megacolon</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Toxic megacolon is a rare but life-threatening complication of severe colonic disease, defined by non-obstructive dilation of all or part of the colon together with signs of systemic toxicity. The radiographic hallmark is dilation of the transverse colon to 6 cm or more (or the cecum to 9 cm or more) in the absence of a mechanical obstruction. It represents a transmural extension of inflammation that paralyzes the colonic smooth muscle, leading to progressive distension, thinning of the wall, and a precarious risk of perforation.</p>
<p>The condition arises when severe inflammation — whether inflammatory or infectious — disrupts the neuromuscular control of the colon. Inflammatory mediators such as nitric oxide impair smooth-muscle tone, the colon becomes atonic and dilates, and the dilation itself worsens ischemia and bacterial translocation. The most common precipitants are acute severe <strong>ulcerative colitis</strong> and <strong>Crohns disease</strong>, and severe infectious colitis, particularly fulminant <strong>Clostridioides difficile</strong> infection. Certain interventions during active colitis — antimotility and opioid drugs, anticholinergics, hypokalemia, and instrumentation such as colonoscopy — can tip severe colitis into toxic megacolon.</p>
<p>Clinically, toxic megacolon is a surgical and medical emergency. A characteristic and dangerous feature is that diarrhea may actually decrease as the colon becomes atonic, falsely reassuring the unwary clinician even as the patient deteriorates. Diagnosis combines radiographic colonic dilation with systemic toxicity criteria (the Jalan criteria), and management requires intensive medical therapy directed at the underlying cause with a low threshold for urgent colectomy if the patient does not rapidly improve.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Patients present acutely ill with abdominal distension, pain, and tenderness, high fever, and tachycardia. There is usually a background of severe colitis with bloody or profuse diarrhea, although stool frequency can paradoxically fall as colonic motility ceases. Systemic toxicity manifests as hypotension, dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, and altered mental status. Bowel sounds are reduced or absent, and the development of peritoneal signs suggests impending or actual perforation.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is made by combining radiographic and clinical criteria. Plain abdominal radiography or CT demonstrates colonic dilation (transverse colon &gt;=6 cm) without mechanical obstruction. The widely used <strong>Jalan criteria</strong> require radiographic dilation plus at least three of fever, tachycardia, neutrophilic leukocytosis, or anemia, together with at least one of dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, hypotension, or altered consciousness. Identifying the underlying cause — through stool testing for C. difficile and assessment of inflammatory bowel disease activity — guides specific therapy. Colonoscopy is generally avoided or performed with extreme caution because of perforation risk.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Toxic megacolon complicates severe colitis of any cause, most commonly acute severe ulcerative colitis and Crohns disease, and fulminant infectious colitis including C. difficile. Hypokalemia, antimotility and opioid medications, anticholinergic drugs, and instrumentation of an acutely inflamed colon are important precipitating and aggravating factors.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is intensive and multidisciplinary. Initial care focuses on aggressive fluid resuscitation, correction of electrolyte abnormalities (especially potassium), bowel rest with nasogastric decompression, and withdrawal of any antimotility agents. The underlying disease is treated directly — intravenous corticosteroids for an inflammatory bowel disease flare, and targeted antimicrobials for infectious causes such as C. difficile. Patients require close clinical observation with serial abdominal examinations and imaging. <strong>Failure to improve within a short window, clinical deterioration, perforation, or uncontrolled hemorrhage mandates urgent subtotal colectomy.</strong></p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The dominant complication is colonic perforation, which precipitates peritonitis, sepsis, and multi-organ failure and dramatically increases mortality. Massive gastrointestinal hemorrhage may also occur. Even with surgery, the physiologic stress of fulminant colitis and perforation carries significant risk.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Outcomes depend heavily on the underlying cause and the timeliness of treatment. With prompt recognition, aggressive medical therapy, and appropriately timed surgery, many patients survive. Mortality rises sharply once perforation occurs, and delays in escalating to surgery are a recognized contributor to poor outcomes.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Toxic megacolon is uncommon. Historically it complicated a small percentage of severe inflammatory bowel disease admissions, but with better management of acute severe colitis its incidence in that setting has declined. Conversely, the rising burden of C. difficile infection has made infectious colitis an increasingly important cause. It can occur at any age and is associated with substantial morbidity whenever it develops.</p>
