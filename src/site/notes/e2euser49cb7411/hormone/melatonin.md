<h1>Melatonin</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) is an indoleamine hormone produced principally by the pineal gland, where it is synthesized from the neurotransmitter serotonin via two enzymatic steps (arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase, AANAT, followed by acetylserotonin O-methyltransferase, ASMT). Its secretion is tightly gated by the light-dark cycle: photic input reaching the suprachiasmatic nucleus suppresses synthesis, so circulating melatonin rises in the evening, peaks in the middle of the biological night (typically 2-4 AM), and falls toward morning. Because of this pattern, melatonin functions as the body's chemical &quot;signal of darkness,&quot; conveying time-of-day and time-of-year information to virtually every tissue.</p>
<p>Beyond its canonical role as a chronobiotic that entrains the circadian rhythm and facilitates sleep onset, melatonin is a potent, amphiphilic free-radical scavenger and indirect antioxidant. It and its metabolites (such as N1-acetyl-N2-formyl-5-methoxykynuramine) neutralize reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, upregulate endogenous antioxidant enzymes, and concentrate within mitochondria, where high-amplitude local production helps preserve redox homeostasis and bioenergetic function. These properties underlie its broad neuroprotective, cardiometabolic, and immunomodulatory actions documented across the lifespan.</p>
<p>Clinically, melatonin is the most widely studied agent for circadian sleep-wake disorders and is used to shorten sleep-onset latency in primary insomnia, to manage jet lag and shift-work misalignment, and to treat delayed sleep-phase syndrome. Endogenous nocturnal melatonin amplitude declines progressively with age, a change implicated in age-related sleep fragmentation and reduced antioxidant defense. Evidence Level: 5/5 for circadian/sleep applications.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Melatonin signals primarily through two high-affinity G-protein-coupled receptors, MT1 (MTNR1A) and MT2 (MTNR1B), which are densely expressed in the suprachiasmatic nucleus and broadly across the brain and periphery. MT1 activation suppresses neuronal firing and promotes sleep propensity, while MT2 activation mediates phase-shifting of the circadian clock. Both couple to Gi proteins, inhibiting adenylate cyclase and lowering cAMP. In addition to receptor-mediated chronobiotic effects, melatonin exerts receptor-independent antioxidant activity by directly scavenging radicals, chelating metals, and stimulating glutathione synthesis and the activity of superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase. Its lipophilic-yet-hydrophilic character lets it cross all morphophysiological barriers and accumulate in subcellular compartments, especially mitochondria.</p>
<h2>Circadian and Sleep Physiology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO)</strong> — the evening rise is the gold-standard marker of circadian phase</li>
<li><strong>Nocturnal peak</strong> — typically 2-4 AM in entrained adults</li>
<li><strong>Light sensitivity</strong> — short-wavelength (blue) light is the most potent suppressor of nocturnal secretion</li>
<li><strong>Phase-response curve</strong> — evening administration advances the clock; morning administration delays it</li>
<li><strong>Age-related decline</strong> — peak amplitude falls markedly from childhood through older adulthood</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insomnia / sleep-onset difficulty</strong> — modestly shortens sleep latency; strongest in those with low endogenous levels</li>
<li><strong>Jet lag and shift work</strong> — re-entrains the clock to a shifted light-dark schedule</li>
<li><strong>Delayed sleep-phase syndrome</strong> — timed low-dose evening melatonin advances sleep timing</li>
<li><strong>Neurodegeneration and brain health</strong> — antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions are neuroprotective in preclinical models</li>
<li><strong>Cardiometabolic risk</strong> — supplementation modestly improves several metabolic risk factors in pooled analyses</li>
<li><strong>Cancer chronobiology</strong> — light-at-night melatonin suppression is studied as an oncogenic exposure</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety and Dosing Notes</h2>
<p>Melatonin has a wide safety margin and low toxicity. Physiological dosing (0.3-1 mg taken in the evening) often matches or exceeds the efficacy of common higher doses (3-10 mg) for circadian effects, because supraphysiological levels can blunt receptor signaling and cause next-day grogginess. Timing relative to the individual's circadian phase matters more than dose for phase-shifting. It should be used cautiously with sedatives, anticoagulants, and immunosuppressants, and is not a substitute for addressing light hygiene and sleep behavior.</p>
