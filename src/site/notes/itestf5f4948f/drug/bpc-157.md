<h1>BPC-157</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>BPC-157 (&quot;Body Protection Compound 157&quot;) is a synthetic <strong>pentadecapeptide</strong> — a chain of 15 amino acids — derived from a partial sequence of a protective protein found in human gastric juice. It is one of the most popular &quot;research peptides&quot; in fitness and biohacking circles, promoted for accelerating recovery from tendon, ligament and muscle injuries and for healing the gut. Despite this popularity, <strong>BPC-157 is not approved for human use by any major regulator</strong> (FDA, EMA, MHRA, or Poland's URPL), and the evidence base is almost entirely preclinical.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>In animal models, BPC-157 appears to accelerate tissue repair by promoting <strong>angiogenesis</strong> (new blood-vessel formation) through VEGFR2 signalling and the nitric-oxide (eNOS) system, by modulating growth-factor pathways and extracellular-matrix remodelling, and by acting as a cytoprotective agent that maintains the integrity of endothelial and epithelial cells. These mechanisms are consistently reported by a small number of research groups but have <strong>not been confirmed in controlled human studies</strong>.</p>
<h2>Clinical Evidence</h2>
<p>The evidence for BPC-157 is dominated by rodent experiments and narrative reviews. There are <strong>no large-scale randomised controlled trials in humans</strong>, no pharmacokinetic or long-term safety data in people, and much of the human &quot;evidence&quot; is anecdotal, drawn from online forums and clinics rather than trials. Reviews of therapeutic peptides classify BPC-157 as promising preclinically but investigational and unvalidated for human use. This is why its <code>evidence_level</code> here is set to <strong>1</strong> — it is included for education and mechanism, and is deliberately <strong>not</strong> eligible to be surfaced as a health recommendation.</p>
<h2>Safety</h2>
<p>The main concrete risks are practical rather than pharmacological: gray-market BPC-157 is typically supplied as a lyophilised powder that users reconstitute and self-inject, creating real risks of <strong>contamination, incorrect dosing and injection-site infection</strong>. Long-term effects in humans are simply unknown, and there is a theoretical concern that a pro-angiogenic peptide could influence tumour growth. Regulators including the FDA warn against unapproved compounded peptides; in Poland the URPL has not authorised BPC-157 and the GIF warns against self-injection of unauthorised peptides.</p>
