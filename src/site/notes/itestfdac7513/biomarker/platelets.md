<h1>Platelets</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Platelets, also called thrombocytes, are the smallest of the cellular elements of blood. They are anucleate cytoplasmic fragments (typically 2-4 micrometers in diameter) shed from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow, with roughly 10^11 platelets produced each day to maintain a stable circulating pool. Although they lack a nucleus, platelets contain mitochondria, alpha-granules, and dense granules packed with clotting factors, growth factors, adhesion molecules, and vasoactive amines. Their circulating lifespan is short — approximately 7 to 10 days — after which senescent platelets are removed primarily by the spleen and liver.</p>
<p>The platelet count measures the concentration of platelets in whole blood and is reported as part of the complete blood count (CBC). Platelets are central to primary hemostasis: at a site of vascular injury they adhere to exposed subendothelial collagen (via von Willebrand factor and glycoprotein receptors), become activated, change shape, release the contents of their granules, and aggregate to form a platelet plug. They also provide a phospholipid surface that accelerates the coagulation cascade and contribute to inflammation, immune defense, wound healing, and angiogenesis through the release of platelet-derived growth factor and other mediators. Roughly one-third of the body's platelet mass is normally sequestered in the spleen and can be released into circulation when demand rises.</p>
<p>Clinically, the platelet count is one of the most frequently ordered laboratory parameters. An abnormally low count (thrombocytopenia) raises concern for impaired production, increased destruction, or splenic sequestration and is associated with bleeding risk, whereas an abnormally high count (thrombocytosis) may be reactive to infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, or tissue damage, or may reflect a primary clonal myeloproliferative disorder. Because so many systemic illnesses perturb platelet number, the count serves as both a sensitive screening tool and a marker for monitoring disease activity, chemotherapy toxicity, and recovery of marrow function. Platelet count is almost always interpreted alongside the mean platelet volume, the white and red cell lines, and a peripheral blood film.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>The conventional adult reference interval is approximately 150-400 x10^3/uL but varies modestly by laboratory, analyzer, and population; some healthy individuals run slightly outside this band.</li>
<li>Automated analyzers may falsely lower the count when platelets clump (pseudothrombocytopenia, often EDTA-induced) or when platelets are unusually large; a peripheral smear confirms true values.</li>
<li>Counts are mildly higher in women and can fall transiently with acute infection, then rebound. Pregnancy commonly produces a benign decline (gestational thrombocytopenia).</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Platelets:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute infection and systemic inflammation (reactive thrombocytosis driven by interleukin-6 and thrombopoietin)</li>
<li>Iron deficiency (a classic cause of reactive thrombocytosis)</li>
<li>Recent surgery, trauma, or significant blood loss (rebound thrombocytosis)</li>
<li>Splenectomy or hyposplenism (loss of the normal splenic platelet pool)</li>
<li>Malignancy and chronic inflammatory disease</li>
<li>Primary myeloproliferative neoplasms such as essential thrombocythemia and polycythemia vera (clonal overproduction)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Platelets:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Bone marrow suppression or failure (chemotherapy, radiation, aplastic anemia, marrow infiltration)</li>
<li>Immune-mediated destruction (immune thrombocytopenia, drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia)</li>
<li>Splenic sequestration from splenomegaly (often due to portal hypertension in liver disease)</li>
<li>Consumption in disseminated intravascular coagulation, sepsis, and thrombotic microangiopathies</li>
<li>Nutritional deficiency of vitamin B12 or folate (ineffective megakaryopoiesis)</li>
<li>Viral infections, alcohol excess, and many medications</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Reduced levels (thrombocytopenia) are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bleeding diathesis</strong> — mucocutaneous bleeding, petechiae, and easy bruising become likely as counts fall below ~50, with serious spontaneous bleeding risk below ~10-20 x10^3/uL.</li>
<li><strong>Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)</strong> — autoimmune platelet destruction, typically a diagnosis of exclusion managed per established hematology guidelines.</li>
<li><strong>Bone marrow disorders</strong> — leukemia, myelodysplasia, aplastic anemia, and marrow infiltration suppress production, usually with concurrent anemia or leukopenia.</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease and hypersplenism</strong> — portal hypertension causes splenic sequestration and reduced hepatic thrombopoietin production.</li>
<li><strong>Sepsis and DIC</strong> — widespread platelet consumption, often with rising D-dimer and falling fibrinogen.</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional deficiency</strong> — vitamin B12 or folate deficiency impairs megakaryocyte maturation.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated levels (thrombocytosis) are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Reactive thrombocytosis</strong> — the most common cause; secondary to infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, tissue damage, or malignancy, and generally not itself prothrombotic.</li>
<li><strong>Myeloproliferative neoplasms</strong> — essential thrombocythemia and polycythemia vera produce clonal, often extreme thrombocytosis carrying both thrombotic and (paradoxically) bleeding risk.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Tracking marrow recovery and bleeding risk during and after chemotherapy</li>
<li>Surveillance of treatment response in ITP and other immune cytopenias</li>
<li>Assessing severity and trajectory in sepsis, DIC, and critical illness</li>
<li>Pre-procedural bleeding-risk assessment and management of anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying cause</strong> — the single most effective strategy; reactive thrombocytosis resolves when the infection, inflammation, or iron deficiency is corrected, and many thrombocytopenias improve once the offending drug, infection, or deficiency is addressed.</li>
<li><strong>Correct nutritional deficiencies</strong> — replete vitamin B12 (e.g., 1000 mcg) and folate (e.g., 400-800 mcg daily, higher in documented deficiency) to restore effective megakaryopoiesis; iron repletion normalizes iron-deficiency-related reactive thrombocytosis.</li>
<li><strong>Review and adjust medications</strong> — stop or substitute agents implicated in drug-induced thrombocytopenia (including heparin if HIT is suspected) under medical supervision.</li>
<li><strong>Limit alcohol</strong> — chronic heavy alcohol use directly suppresses platelet production; reduction allows recovery.</li>
<li><strong>Use evidence-based pharmacotherapy when indicated</strong> — corticosteroids, IVIG, or thrombopoietin-receptor agonists for ITP, and cytoreduction (e.g., hydroxyurea) for high-risk primary thrombocytosis, are prescribed and monitored by a clinician.</li>
<li><strong>Be cautious with antiplatelet agents</strong> — drugs such as aspirin do not lower the count but impair platelet function; their use should be weighed carefully when counts are abnormal.</li>
</ol>
