<h1>Fecal Microbiota Transplantation</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), also known as stool transplant or microbiota restoration therapy, is the transfer of processed fecal material from a healthy, rigorously screened donor into the gastrointestinal tract of a recipient. The goal is to repair a dysbiotic gut microbiome by reintroducing a diverse community of commensal microorganisms. FMT can be delivered by colonoscopy, retention enema, nasoduodenal/nasogastric tube, or—increasingly—oral freeze-dried capsules.</p>
<p>The best-established and most effective indication for FMT is recurrent <em>Clostridioides difficile</em> infection (rCDI), where it cures the great majority of patients who have failed standard antibiotic therapy. The landmark randomized trial by van Nood and colleagues was stopped early because duodenal donor-feces infusion was so much more effective than vancomycin (resolution in roughly 81-94% vs ~31%). FMT is also under active investigation for inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic disorders, and other dysbiosis-associated conditions, though evidence outside rCDI remains preliminary. Standardized, FDA-regulated microbiota products have since been approved for prevention of rCDI recurrence.</p>
<p>Evidence Level: 5/5 for recurrent C. difficile infection; investigational (1-2/5) for other indications.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>FMT works principally by restoring colonization resistance—the ability of a diverse, intact commensal microbiota to outcompete and suppress pathogens. In recurrent <em>C. difficile</em> infection, repeated antibiotic exposure depletes commensal bacteria, collapsing microbial diversity and disrupting bile acid metabolism. Healthy gut bacteria convert primary bile acids into secondary bile acids that inhibit <em>C. difficile</em> spore germination and vegetative growth; their loss permits unchecked C. difficile proliferation. Transplanting a healthy microbiota rapidly re-establishes secondary bile acid pools, competitive nutrient utilization, and short-chain fatty acid production, breaking the cycle of recurrence.</p>
<h2>Protocol Details</h2>
<ul>
<li>Donor screening: extensive infectious-disease and health questionnaire plus blood and stool testing</li>
<li>Delivery routes: oral capsules, colonoscopy, enema, or nasoduodenal infusion</li>
<li>For rCDI: typically administered after standard-of-care antibiotics (vancomycin or fidaxomicin) for active infection</li>
<li>Repeat FMT improves cure rates in the minority who recur after a first treatment</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<p>FMT is generally well tolerated. Common short-term effects include transient bloating, cramping, flatulence, and changes in bowel habits. The principal serious risk is transmission of an infectious agent from donor to recipient, which is minimized by stringent donor screening; severe immunocompromise is a relative contraindication requiring specialist assessment. Long-term safety data continue to accumulate.</p>
