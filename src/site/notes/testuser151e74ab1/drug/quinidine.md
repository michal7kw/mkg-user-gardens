<h1>Quinidine</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Quinidine is a class IA antiarrhythmic agent (Vaughan-Williams classification) and the dextrorotatory diastereomer of quinine. It was the first widely used oral antiarrhythmic and remains a prototypical sodium-channel blocker, suppressing phase 0 depolarization of cardiac myocytes while simultaneously prolonging action potential duration via potassium-channel (IKr) blockade. As an antimalarial, it is a highly potent blood schizonticide against <em>Plasmodium falciparum</em> and historically was the parenteral agent of choice for severe malaria until supplanted by artesunate.</p>
<p>Clinically, quinidine is used primarily for sinus rhythm maintenance after cardioversion of atrial fibrillation or flutter, and less commonly for ventricular tachycardia and atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardias. Its use has declined substantially because of (1) a narrow therapeutic index, (2) significant pro-arrhythmic risk (notably torsades de pointes), (3) frequent drug-drug interactions via CYP2D6 and CYP3A4, and (4) the availability of safer alternatives (flecainide, propafenone, amiodarone, dofetilide). A landmark Cochrane meta-analysis (Hood et al., 2014) confirmed that while quinidine maintains sinus rhythm more often than placebo, it increases all-cause mortality -- a sobering signal that consolidates its current third-line role.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (mechanism well characterized; clinical use constrained by safety, not efficacy).</p>
<h2>Pharmacology</h2>
<p><strong>Mechanism of Action:</strong><br />
Quinidine binds preferentially to open and fast-inactivated states of the cardiac voltage-gated sodium channel (NaV1.5), slowing phase 0 depolarization (Class I effect). Its IA subclass designation reflects intermediate dissociation kinetics -- less use-dependent block than IB (lidocaine) but more than IC (flecainide). Concomitant blockade of the rapid delayed rectifier potassium channel IKr (hERG) prolongs action-potential duration, effective refractory period, and the QT interval. Additional vagolytic (antimuscarinic) effects accelerate AV nodal conduction, an important consideration when used in atrial fibrillation (may increase ventricular response rate unless combined with an AV-nodal blocking agent).</p>
<p>Against <em>P. falciparum</em>, quinidine (like quinine) interferes with the parasite's ability to polymerize toxic free heme into inert hemozoin, leading to accumulation of membrane-damaging free heme.</p>
<p><strong>Pharmacokinetics:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Absorption: 70-80% oral bioavailability; peak serum concentration 1-3 h (sulfate), 3-4 h (gluconate SR)</li>
<li>Distribution: Vd 2-3 L/kg; 80-90% plasma protein-bound (alpha-1-acid glycoprotein)</li>
<li>Metabolism: Hepatic via CYP3A4 (major) and CYP2D6 (minor); active metabolite 3-hydroxyquinidine</li>
<li>Elimination: Renal (~20% unchanged); half-life 6-8 h (prolonged in CHF, hepatic disease, elderly)</li>
<li>Therapeutic serum range: 2-5 mcg/mL (toxicity &gt;6 mcg/mL)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Key Drug Interactions:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Digoxin: doubles digoxin levels (displacement + P-glycoprotein inhibition) -- reduce digoxin dose 50% and monitor</li>
<li>Amiodarone, verapamil, cimetidine: raise quinidine levels</li>
<li>Phenobarbital, phenytoin, rifampin: lower quinidine levels (CYP3A4 induction)</li>
<li>Diuretics: hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia markedly amplifies torsades risk</li>
<li>Azole antifungals, macrolides: CYP3A4 inhibition raises quinidine levels</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Use</h2>
<p><strong>Primary Indications:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Maintenance of sinus rhythm after pharmacological or electrical cardioversion of atrial fibrillation or flutter (third-line; flecainide/propafenone/amiodarone preferred)</li>
<li>Suppression of ventricular tachycardia (largely historical; replaced by amiodarone, sotalol, ICD)</li>
<li>Accessory pathway (Wolff-Parkinson-White) tachycardias</li>
<li>Severe <em>P. falciparum</em> malaria (IV, where artesunate unavailable)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Dosing:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Sustained-release gluconate: 324-648 mg q8-12h</li>
<li>Sulfate: 200-400 mg q6h</li>
<li>Target serum: 2-5 mcg/mL</li>
<li>Loading: rarely used today (risk of &quot;quinidine syncope&quot; / torsades)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>ECG: QTc, QRS widening (&gt;50% baseline warrants dose reduction)</li>
<li>Serum quinidine (target 2-5 mcg/mL)</li>
<li>Serum potassium, magnesium (maintain K &gt;4.0, Mg &gt;2.0)</li>
<li>Digoxin level if co-administered</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<p><strong>Contraindications:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Long QT syndrome, QTc &gt;460 ms (male) / &gt;470 ms (female)</li>
<li>Second/third-degree AV block (without pacemaker)</li>
<li>Brugada syndrome -- quinidine unmasked is diagnostic but standard therapeutic quinidine is contraindicated</li>
<li>Severe sinoatrial disease</li>
<li>Torsades de pointes history</li>
<li>Myasthenia gravis (exacerbates weakness)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Side Effects:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Torsades de pointes / polymorphic VT (the feared pro-arrhythmia, ~1-2% incidence)</li>
<li>Cinchonism: tinnitus, headache, visual disturbance, GI upset (dose-related, reversible)</li>
<li>Thrombocytopenia (immune-mediated, rare)</li>
<li>GI upset (nausea, diarrhea)</li>
<li>Hypotension (especially IV)</li>
<li>Anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, urinary retention, blurred vision)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>FDA Boxed Warning:</strong> Pro-arrhythmic death risk -- see Cochrane meta-analysis (Hood et al., 2014).</p>
