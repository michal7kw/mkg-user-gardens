<h1>Mitral Regurgitation</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Mitral regurgitation (MR) is incompetence of the mitral valve that permits retrograde flow of blood from the high-pressure left ventricle into the lower-pressure left atrium during ventricular contraction. It is among the most prevalent valvular heart diseases, and its hemodynamic burden depends on both the volume of regurgitant flow and the time course over which it develops. In chronic MR, the left atrium and ventricle dilate gradually to accommodate the extra volume, often keeping patients symptom-free for years; in acute MR (for example, after papillary muscle rupture complicating a heart attack), the unprepared chambers cannot compensate, producing sudden pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock.</p>
<p>Clinically, MR is divided into primary (organic) disease, in which the valve apparatus itself is abnormal — as in mitral valve prolapse, rheumatic disease, or endocarditis — and secondary (functional) disease, in which the leaflets are structurally normal but fail to coapt because of left ventricular remodeling or atrial/annular dilation. This distinction is central to management: primary severe MR is fundamentally a mechanical problem best corrected by valve repair, whereas secondary MR is a manifestation of underlying ventricular or atrial disease and is treated first with guideline-directed medical therapy, with intervention reserved for selected patients.</p>
<p>The importance of MR lies in its tendency to drive a self-perpetuating cycle of chamber enlargement, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary hypertension, and heart failure if left untreated. Modern echocardiographic quantification allows precise grading of severity and timing of intervention before irreversible ventricular dysfunction develops, and the advent of transcatheter edge-to-edge repair has expanded treatment to patients previously considered inoperable.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Chronic compensated MR is frequently asymptomatic and detected incidentally through a holosystolic apical murmur radiating to the axilla. As the regurgitant volume and chamber dilation increase, patients develop exertional dyspnea, fatigue, orthopnea, and palpitations, the latter often heralding new atrial fibrillation. Acute severe MR presents dramatically with rapid-onset pulmonary edema and hemodynamic collapse.</p>

<p>Severity is graded as mild, moderate, or severe using effective regurgitant orifice area, regurgitant volume, and regurgitant fraction.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Primary MR arises from mitral valve prolapse and myxomatous degeneration, rheumatic heart disease, and infective endocarditis. Secondary MR develops from ischemic papillary muscle dysfunction following myocardial infarction, left ventricular dilation in heart failure, and annular dilation accompanying long-standing atrial fibrillation.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management hinges on MR type, severity, symptoms, and ventricular function. Asymptomatic moderate disease is monitored with serial echocardiography. Severe primary MR is treated with surgical mitral valve repair when feasible (preferred over replacement) before the onset of left ventricular dysfunction. Secondary MR is managed first with guideline-directed heart failure therapy; transcatheter edge-to-edge repair is offered to selected symptomatic patients who remain limited despite optimal medical treatment. Coexisting atrial fibrillation is managed with rate or rhythm control and anticoagulation.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Untreated severe MR progresses to heart failure with pulmonary congestion, atrial fibrillation with attendant thromboembolic risk, pulmonary hypertension, and ultimately irreversible left ventricular systolic dysfunction. Infective endocarditis can both cause and complicate the disease.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Outcomes are favorable when severe MR is corrected before irreversible ventricular remodeling occurs; surgical repair of primary MR in experienced centers carries low operative risk and excellent durability. Prognosis is poorer in secondary MR, where it largely tracks the underlying cardiomyopathy, and in patients who present with established heart failure or reduced ejection fraction.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>MR is one of the most common valvular lesions, and its prevalence rises markedly with age. Degenerative (primary) disease predominates in higher-income settings with aging populations, while rheumatic MR remains an important cause in many lower-income regions.</p>
