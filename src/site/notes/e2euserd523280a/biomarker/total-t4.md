<h1>Total T4</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Total T4 measures the complete serum pool of thyroxine, the principal hormone produced and secreted by the thyroid gland. T4 is synthesized by iodination of tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin in thyroid follicular cells, then secreted into the circulation where it functions largely as a <strong>prohormone</strong>: it is transported to peripheral tissues where deiodinase enzymes (D1, D2) convert it to the more active T3, or to inactive reverse T3 (via D3). Total T4 is a broad indicator of the gland's secretory output and overall thyroid status.</p>
<p>More than 99% of circulating T4 is bound to carrier proteins, chiefly <strong>thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG; ~70%)</strong>, with smaller contributions from transthyretin (~10-15%) and albumin (~15-20%). This high degree of binding means total T4 is sensitive to changes in binding-protein concentration: <strong>pregnancy, estrogen therapy, and oral contraceptives raise TBG and therefore total T4 without changing the metabolically relevant free hormone</strong>, whereas androgens, nephrotic syndrome, severe illness, and genetic TBG variants can lower it. For this reason, total T4 alone is rarely diagnostic; it must be interpreted with <strong>Free T4 and TSH</strong> to distinguish true thyroid dysfunction from binding-protein artifacts.</p>
<p>Elevated Total T4 suggests hyperthyroidism or excessive levothyroxine dosing, while low Total T4 indicates hypothyroidism, pituitary (central) dysfunction, or euthyroid sick syndrome. Total T4 is also used to monitor <strong>pregnancy thyroid status</strong> (where TBG rises substantially; FT4 immunoassays may be unreliable), and in <strong>congenital hypothyroidism screening</strong> of newborns.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (well-validated; ATA/ETA guideline-endorsed for specific indications like pregnancy).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Total T4 reflects binding-protein status (especially TBG)</li>
<li>Pregnancy: TBG rises ~2-3x; total T4 rises correspondingly</li>
<li>Estrogen therapy / OCPs raise TBG and total T4</li>
<li>Anabolic steroids lower TBG and total T4</li>
<li>Severe illness (euthyroid sick syndrome) lowers total T4 in late stages</li>
<li>Familial dysalbuminemic hyperthyroxinemia (FDH) causes false-high total T4</li>
<li>Total T4 is rarely diagnostic alone -- always interpret with FT4 and TSH</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Total T4:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong> (Graves disease, toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma)</li>
<li><strong>Excessive levothyroxine dosing</strong> (over-replacement)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (TBG elevation; total T4 rises but FT4 unchanged)</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy and oral contraceptives</strong> (raise TBG)</li>
<li><strong>Acute hepatitis</strong> (acute TBG elevation)</li>
<li><strong>Familial dysalbuminemic hyperthyroxinemia (FDH)</strong> -- genetic; false-high total T4</li>
<li><strong>Familial elevation of TBG</strong></li>
<li><strong>Amiodarone</strong> (inhibits T4-to-T3 conversion; raises total T4)</li>
<li><strong>Acute psychiatric illness</strong> (transient)</li>
<li><strong>Heroin, methadone</strong> (raise TBG)</li>
<li><strong>Perphenazine, clofibrate</strong> (raise TBG)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Total T4:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hypothyroidism</strong> (Hashimoto thyroiditis, post-thyroidectomy, post-radioiodine)</li>
<li><strong>Secondary / central hypothyroidism</strong> (pituitary or hypothalamic disease)</li>
<li><strong>Euthyroid sick syndrome / non-thyroidal illness</strong> (severe illness lowers total T4 in late stages)</li>
<li><strong>Androgens and anabolic steroids</strong> (lower TBG)</li>
<li><strong>Nephrotic syndrome</strong> (urinary loss of TBG)</li>
<li><strong>Severe illness, protein malnutrition</strong> (reduced TBG synthesis)</li>
<li><strong>Iodine deficiency</strong></li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> lithium, amiodarone (type 2), phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampin</li>
<li><strong>Low TBG (genetic)</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated Total T4 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hyperthyroidism</strong> (with suppressed TSH; high FT4)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy-related hyperthyroidism</strong> (TBG elevation; gestational transient thyrotoxicosis)</li>
<li><strong>Exogenous thyroid hormone</strong> (over-replacement of levothyroxine)</li>
<li><strong>Binding-protein artifacts</strong> (pregnancy, estrogen, FDH) -- total T4 elevated but FT4 normal; patient euthyroid</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Low Total T4 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hypothyroidism</strong> (high TSH; low FT4)</li>
<li><strong>Central hypothyroidism</strong> (low/normal TSH; low FT4)</li>
<li><strong>Severe non-thyroidal illness</strong> (low FT4 in late stages)</li>
<li><strong>Protein-losing states</strong> (nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis)</li>
<li><strong>Low TBG (genetic)</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Total T4 is rarely diagnostic alone</strong> -- always interpret with FT4 and TSH</li>
<li><strong>Binding-protein effects</strong> are the principal confounder</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> total T4 rises physiologically; FT4 is preferred but immunoassays can be unreliable</li>
<li><strong>Familial dysalbuminemic hyperthyroxinemia</strong> causes false-high total T4 (genetic; euthyroid)</li>
<li><strong>TSH is preferred for routine screening</strong> (more sensitive due to log-linear feedback)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Comprehensive thyroid workup (with TSH, FT4, FT3)</li>
<li>Pregnancy thyroid monitoring (when FT4 immunoassays are unreliable)</li>
<li>Newborn congenital hypothyroidism screening (total T4 is the standard)</li>
<li>Levothyroxine therapy monitoring (TSH preferred; FT4 also used)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>Total T4 reflects thyroid hormone production; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For elevated Total T4 (hyperthyroidism):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Graves disease:</strong> methimazole; radioactive iodine ablation; thyroidectomy</li>
<li><strong>Toxic multinodular goiter / adenoma:</strong> radioactive iodine or surgery</li>
<li><strong>Beta-blocker</strong> (propranolol) for symptomatic control</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> levothyroxine dose</li>
<li><strong>Identify binding-protein artifact</strong> (pregnancy, estrogen, FDH) -- no treatment needed if FT4 normal</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For low Total T4 (hypothyroidism):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hypothyroidism:</strong> levothyroxine replacement (1.6 mcg/kg/day); titrate to normalize TSH</li>
<li><strong>Central hypothyroidism:</strong> levothyroxine by FT4 (not TSH); assess other pituitary axes</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying illness</strong> (euthyroid sick syndrome)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy-specific management:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Increase levothyroxine dose by 25-30% at confirmation of pregnancy</li>
<li>Monitor TSH every 4 weeks; target TSH &lt;2.5 (1st trimester), &lt;3.0 (2nd/3rd)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Adequate iodine intake</strong> (150 mcg/day; 250 mcg/day in pregnancy)</li>
<li><strong>Selenium</strong> 200 mcg daily in mild autoimmune hypothyroidism (controversial)</li>
<li><strong>Avoid excessive iodine</strong> (kelp, contrast agents) in Hashimoto's</li>
<li><strong>Address binding-protein causes:</strong> adjust estrogen therapy, treat nephrotic syndrome, monitor hepatic function</li>
</ol>
