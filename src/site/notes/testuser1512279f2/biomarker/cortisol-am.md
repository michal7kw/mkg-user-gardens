<h1>Cortisol (AM)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cortisol is the principal glucocorticoid hormone in humans, synthesized in the <strong>zona fasciculata</strong> of the adrenal cortex under the control of pituitary adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which is in turn driven by hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH). This <strong>hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis</strong> governs the body's response to physiological and psychological stress, regulates gluconeogenesis, modulates immune and inflammatory activity, supports cardiovascular tone, and influences protein and fat metabolism.</p>
<p>Cortisol secretion follows a robust <strong>circadian rhythm</strong>, with concentrations peaking within the first 30-60 minutes after awakening (the &quot;cortisol awakening response&quot;) and declining progressively through the day to a nadir near midnight. Because of this diurnal variation, a <strong>morning (AM) sample collected between 6 and 8 AM</strong> provides the most clinically interpretable snapshot of basal adrenal reserve.</p>
<p>Clinically, AM cortisol anchors the workup of two opposing disorders:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Glucocorticoid deficiency (adrenal insufficiency):</strong> A clearly normal AM cortisol (&gt;15 mcg/dL) effectively excludes adrenal insufficiency; a low AM cortisol (&lt;5 mcg/dL) with compatible symptoms (fatigue, weight loss, hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperpigmentation in primary disease) warrants urgent evaluation for adrenal insufficiency, as undiagnosed deficiency carries risk of life-threatening adrenal crisis. Confirmation with the <strong>ACTH (Synacthen) stimulation test</strong> is standard.</li>
<li><strong>Glucocorticoid excess (Cushing syndrome):</strong> A persistently elevated AM cortisol that fails to suppress on dexamethasone suppression testing suggests Cushing syndrome (pituitary Cushing disease, ectopic ACTH, adrenal cortisol-producing tumor). Confirmation requires <strong>late-night salivary cortisol, 24-h urinary free cortisol, and dexamethasone suppression testing</strong>.</li>
</ul>
<p>Interpretation must account for assay generation, concurrent estrogen (raises corticosteroid-binding globulin), acute illness/stress, and the timing of the draw. Always interpret with ACTH measurement to localize the defect to the adrenal (primary) vs the pituitary (secondary).</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational endocrine test; ATA/ETA / Endocrine Society guideline-endorsed).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Ranges are assay- and laboratory-dependent; check lab-specific cutoffs</li>
<li><strong>Evening cortisol</strong> is much lower than AM (diurnal rhythm); timing of collection is essential</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> cortisol rises progressively due to CBG elevation</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy, oral contraceptives:</strong> raise CBG and total cortisol (but free cortisol unchanged)</li>
<li><strong>Acute illness, stress, surgery, trauma:</strong> raise cortisol (stress response)</li>
<li><strong>Shift work, jet lag:</strong> disrupt diurnal rhythm</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase AM cortisol:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cushing syndrome</strong> (pituitary Cushing disease, ectopic ACTH, adrenal cortisol-producing tumor)</li>
<li><strong>Exogenous glucocorticoids</strong> (hydrocortisone, cortisone, prednisone -- immunoassays cross-react variably)</li>
<li><strong>Acute physical or psychological stress</strong> (critical illness, surgery, trauma, seizure, intense exercise)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (CBG elevation; total cortisol rises; free cortisol unchanged)</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy, oral contraceptives</strong> (raise CBG)</li>
<li><strong>Severe depression, alcoholism</strong> (&quot;pseudo-Cushing&quot;)</li>
<li><strong>Obesity</strong> (mild elevation)</li>
<li><strong>Anorexia nervosa</strong> (HPA-axis activation)</li>
<li><strong>Biotin interference</strong> (falsely elevated in streptavidin-biotin immunoassays)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease AM cortisol:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary adrenal insufficiency</strong> (Addison's disease; autoimmune, TB, bilateral adrenal hemorrhage)</li>
<li><strong>Secondary / central adrenal insufficiency</strong> (pituitary tumor, Sheehan syndrome, exogenous steroid suppression)</li>
<li><strong>Tertiary adrenal insufficiency</strong> (hypothalamic disease; chronic opioid use)</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal hemorrhage</strong> (anticoagulants, sepsis, Waterhouse-Friderichsen)</li>
<li><strong>CAH (21-hydroxylase deficiency)</strong> -- cortisol deficient</li>
<li><strong>Drugs suppressing HPA axis:</strong> chronic systemic corticosteroids, megestrol, ketoconazole, metyrapone, etomidate, mifepristone</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low AM cortisol is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease):</strong> autoimmune (most common in developed countries); TB (most common globally); presents with fatigue, weight loss, hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hyperpigmentation; <strong>life-threatening adrenal crisis</strong> if untreated</li>
<li><strong>Secondary / central adrenal insufficiency:</strong> pituitary tumor, Sheehan syndrome; presents similarly but <strong>no hyperpigmentation, no hyperkalemia</strong> (intact RAAS)</li>
<li><strong>Critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI):</strong> relative adrenal insufficiency in sepsis / critical illness</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated AM cortisol is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cushing syndrome:</strong> central obesity, moon facies, buffalo hump, purple striae, hypertension, glucose intolerance, osteoporosis, proximal myopathy, mood changes, easy bruising</li>
<li><strong>Cushing disease</strong> (pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma; ~70% of endogenous Cushing)</li>
<li><strong>Ectopic ACTH</strong> (small cell lung cancer, carcinoid)</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal cortisol-producing tumor</strong> (adenoma, carcinoma)</li>
<li><strong>Exogenous steroid use</strong></li>
<li><strong>Pseudo-Cushing</strong> (severe depression, alcoholism, obesity)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Single AM cortisol is non-diagnostic</strong> for Cushing -- requires dynamic testing (DST, late-night salivary cortisol, 24-h UFC)</li>
<li><strong>Borderline low values</strong> require ACTH stimulation test (250 mcg or 1 mcg) for confirmation of adrenal insufficiency</li>
<li><strong>Stress, illness</strong> raise cortisol; repeat when stable</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy</strong> raises CBG and total cortisol (false positive)</li>
<li><strong>Biotin interference</strong> is common</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Adrenal insufficiency workup (with ACTH, electrolytes, glucose, ACTH stim test)</li>
<li>Cushing syndrome workup (with ACTH, late-night salivary cortisol, 24-h UFC, DST)</li>
<li>Stress-response monitoring in critical illness</li>
<li>Functional medicine: HPA-axis assessment</li>
<li>Treatment monitoring for adrenal insufficiency (hydrocortisone replacement)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>AM cortisol reflects HPA-axis function; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For low cortisol (adrenal insufficiency):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hydrocortisone</strong> 15-25 mg/day (split: 10-15 mg AM, 5-10 mg afternoon) -- the standard glucocorticoid replacement</li>
<li><strong>Fludrocortisone</strong> 0.05-0.2 mg/day -- mineralocorticoid replacement (only for primary adrenal insufficiency)</li>
<li><strong>Patient education:</strong> stress-dose steroids for illness/surgery; medical alert bracelet; emergency hydrocortisone injection kit</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying cause:</strong> TB, autoimmune disease, pituitary tumor</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For elevated cortisol (Cushing syndrome):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pituitary surgery</strong> (transsphenoidal) for Cushing disease</li>
<li><strong>Adrenalectomy</strong> for adrenal tumors</li>
<li><strong>Medical therapy:</strong> ketoconazole, metyrapone, mifepristone (select cases)</li>
<li><strong>Ectopic ACTH:</strong> treat underlying tumor</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For chronic stress / HPA-axis dysregulation (functional medicine):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Stress-reduction practices:</strong> mindfulness, meditation, breathwork, yoga</li>
<li><strong>Consistent sleep-wake timing</strong> (regulates circadian rhythm)</li>
<li><strong>Adaptogenic herbs:</strong> Ashwagandha 300-600 mg daily (KSM-66) -- meta-analyses show cortisol reduction in chronically stressed adults</li>
<li><strong>Phosphatidylserine</strong> 400-600 mg before bed -- lowers evening cortisol</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium glycinate</strong> 200-400 mg evening -- supports HPA-axis recovery</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> 1-4 g/day -- anti-inflammatory; modest cortisol effect</li>
<li><strong>Reduce caffeine after noon</strong></li>
<li><strong>Reduce alcohol</strong></li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Avoid unnecessary exogenous steroids</strong> (long-term prednisone, dexamethasone suppress HPA axis)</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying comorbidities:</strong> depression, anxiety, OSA, chronic pain</li>
</ol>
