<h1>Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D, also abbreviated HDRS or HRSD) is a clinician-administered instrument for rating the severity of depressive symptoms in patients already diagnosed with a depressive disorder. Introduced by Max Hamilton in 1960, it became—and largely remains—the historical reference standard for measuring depression severity and treatment response in antidepressant clinical trials.</p>
<p>The most widely used version contains 17 items rated during a semi-structured interview, covering depressed mood, guilt, suicidal ideation, insomnia, work and activities, psychomotor change, anxiety, somatic symptoms, and insight. Items are scored on either a 0-2 or 0-4 scale depending on the symptom. Unlike self-report measures such as the BDI-II, the HAM-D depends on trained clinician judgment, which improves consistency in research settings but requires rater training to maintain reliability.</p>

<h2>Scoring &amp; Interpretation</h2>
<p>Item scores on the 17-item version are summed for a total range of 0-52 (higher = more severe). Commonly cited severity cutoffs:</p>

<p>In treatment trials, a ≥50% reduction from baseline is the conventional definition of treatment <strong>response</strong>, and a score ≤7 is often used to define <strong>remission</strong>.</p>
<h2>Psychometrics</h2>
<p>The HAM-D was developed in hospitalized adults with depressive illness. It shows good inter-rater reliability when raters are trained and acceptable validity against global clinical impressions. Recognized limitations include heavy weighting of insomnia and somatic items (which can inflate scores in medically ill patients), redundancy among items, and the multi-decade evolution of variant versions (HAM-D-6, -17, -21, -24), which complicates cross-study comparison. Despite critiques, it remains a benchmark outcome measure in depression research.</p>
