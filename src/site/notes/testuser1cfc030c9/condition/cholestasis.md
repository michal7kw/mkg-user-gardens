<h1>Cholestasis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cholestasis is defined as impairment of bile formation or bile flow, resulting in the retention of <strong>bile acids, bilirubin, cholesterol, and other biliary constituents</strong> in the blood and liver. It is broadly categorized as <strong>intrahepatic</strong> (defect in bile formation at the hepatocyte or canalicular level) or <strong>extrahepatic</strong> (mechanical obstruction of the biliary tree).</p>
<p>The clinical hallmark is <strong>jaundice with elevated alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)</strong>, disproportionate to ALT/AST elevation. <strong>Pruritus</strong> is a characteristic and often debilitating symptom, caused by deposition of bile acids in the skin (though the exact pruritogen is debated; lysophosphatidic acid and autotaxin may be more important than bile acids themselves). <strong>Fat malabsorption</strong> leads to steatorrhea and deficiencies of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).</p>
<p>Common causes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Extrahepatic:</strong> choledocholithiasis (gallstones in common bile duct), pancreatic head tumor, cholangiocarcinoma, biliary stricture, primary sclerosing cholangitis</li>
<li><strong>Intrahepatic:</strong> primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), drug-induced (antibiotics, anabolic steroids, chlorpromazine), viral hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), sepsis-associated, TPN</li>
</ul>
<p>Chronic cholestasis (PBC, PSC) can lead to <strong>biliary cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and hepatocellular carcinoma</strong>.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Jaundice</strong> (yellow discoloration of skin and sclera)</li>
<li><strong>Pruritus</strong> (often severe; worse at night; can be debilitating)</li>
<li><strong>Fatigue</strong> (common, non-specific)</li>
<li><strong>Dark urine</strong> (bilirubinuria)</li>
<li><strong>Pale or clay-colored stools</strong> (acholia; absent stercobilin)</li>
<li><strong>Right upper quadrant pain</strong> (if biliary obstruction)</li>
<li><strong>Steatorrhea</strong> (fatty, foul-smelling stools)</li>
<li><strong>Easy bruising</strong> (vitamin K deficiency)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p><strong>Liver function tests:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Elevated <strong>ALP</strong> (&gt;3x ULN) and <strong>GGT</strong> (most characteristic pattern)</li>
<li>Elevated <strong>direct (conjugated) bilirubin</strong></li>
<li>Mildly elevated ALT/AST (usually &lt;5x ULN; higher in hepatitis)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Imaging (to distinguish intra- vs extrahepatic):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ultrasound</strong> -- first-line; identifies biliary dilation, gallstones, masses</li>
<li><strong>MRCP</strong> (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography) -- gold standard non-invasive biliary imaging</li>
<li><strong>CT abdomen</strong> -- for pancreatic masses, staging</li>
<li><strong>ERCP</strong> -- diagnostic and therapeutic (stone removal, stent placement, biopsy)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Specific tests:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>AMA (anti-mitochondrial antibody)</strong> -- positive in ~95% of PBC</li>
<li><strong>p-ANCA</strong> -- positive in ~65% of PSC</li>
<li><strong>IgM</strong> -- elevated in PBC</li>
<li><strong>Viral hepatitis panel</strong> (HBV, HCV)</li>
<li><strong>Drug history</strong> -- critical for drug-induced cholestasis</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Treat underlying cause:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Choledocholithiasis:</strong> ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction</li>
<li><strong>Pancreatic head tumor:</strong> Whipple procedure or palliative biliary stenting</li>
<li><strong>PBC:</strong> ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) 13-15 mg/kg/day; obeticholic acid for non-responders</li>
<li><strong>PSC:</strong> no specific medical therapy; manage complications; ERCP for dominant strictures; liver transplant for end-stage</li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced:</strong> discontinue offending agent</li>
<li><strong>ICP (pregnancy):</strong> UDCA; delivery if severe</li>
<li><strong>Sepsis-associated:</strong> treat underlying infection</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Symptomatic management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pruritus:</strong> cholestyramine (first-line), rifampin (second-line), sertraline, naltrexone</li>
<li><strong>Fat-soluble vitamin supplementation:</strong> vitamins A, D, E, K (monitor levels)</li>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis prevention:</strong> calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonate if needed</li>
</ul>
