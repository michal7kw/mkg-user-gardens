<h1>Zinc</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Zinc is an essential trace mineral that participates as a cofactor or structural component in over 300 enzymatic reactions and approximately 2,000 transcription factors (zinc finger proteins). It is the second most abundant trace mineral in the body after iron, with approximately 60% stored in skeletal muscle, 30% in bone, and the remainder distributed across the liver, skin, kidneys, and prostate. Unlike iron, zinc has no dedicated storage system, making regular dietary intake essential.</p>
<p>Zinc is critical for immune function at multiple levels: it is required for the development and activation of T-lymphocytes, natural killer cells, and neutrophils; it supports the physical integrity of skin and mucosal barriers; and it inhibits viral replication (particularly rhinoviruses). Zinc deficiency is the fifth leading risk factor for disease burden in developing countries, affecting an estimated 2 billion people worldwide, primarily due to diets high in phytate-rich grains and low in animal protein.</p>
<p>Zinc homeostasis is tightly regulated by zinc transporters (ZnT and ZIP families) and metallothioneins. Supplemental forms differ significantly in bioavailability: zinc picolinate and zinc bisglycinate are well-absorbed, zinc citrate and gluconate are moderately absorbed, and zinc oxide is poorly absorbed but inexpensive. Importantly, zinc and copper share intestinal absorption pathways, so chronic zinc supplementation above 30 mg/day can induce copper deficiency. A 15:1 zinc-to-copper ratio is commonly recommended when supplementing.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Serves as a cofactor for 300+ enzymes including carbonic anhydrase and alkaline phosphatase</li>
<li>Structural component of zinc finger transcription factors regulating gene expression</li>
<li>Essential for innate and adaptive immune cell development and function</li>
<li>Required for wound healing, tissue repair, and collagen synthesis</li>
<li>Supports DNA synthesis and cell division (critical during growth and pregnancy)</li>
<li>Maintains taste and smell perception via gustin (carbonic anhydrase VI)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Oysters (richest food source by far)</li>
<li>Red meat (beef, lamb)</li>
<li>Poultry (chicken, turkey dark meat)</li>
<li>Pumpkin seeds and hemp seeds</li>
<li>Lentils, chickpeas, and beans</li>
<li>Cashews and almonds</li>
<li>Crab and lobster</li>
<li>Fortified cereals and whole grains</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Zinc deficiency impairs immune function (increased susceptibility to infections, particularly diarrheal and respiratory), delays wound healing, causes hair loss, skin lesions (acrodermatitis enteropathica in severe genetic deficiency), loss of taste and smell (hypogeusia, hyposmia), growth retardation in children, and hypogonadism in males. Marginal zinc deficiency is common in vegetarians, the elderly, pregnant women, and individuals with gastrointestinal disorders (Crohn's disease, celiac disease).</p>
<p>Excess zinc from chronic supplementation (&gt;40 mg/day) induces copper deficiency by upregulating intestinal metallothionein, which preferentially binds copper and prevents its absorption. Copper deficiency from zinc excess manifests as microcytic anemia, neutropenia, and neurological symptoms resembling B12 deficiency. Acute zinc toxicity (150-450 mg) causes nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea. Paradoxically, very high zinc intake can suppress immune function, the opposite of its beneficial effect at optimal doses.</p>
