<h1>Selenium</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Selenium is an essential trace mineral that is incorporated into selenoproteins as the amino acid selenocysteine (the &quot;21st amino acid&quot;). The human selenoproteome comprises 25 selenoproteins, including glutathione peroxidases (GPx1-4, antioxidant defense), thioredoxin reductases (redox regulation), and iodothyronine deiodinases (thyroid hormone conversion of T4 to active T3). Selenium is thus indispensable for both antioxidant protection and thyroid function.</p>
<p>Selenium status varies dramatically worldwide depending on soil selenium content, which directly determines concentrations in plant foods and the animals that consume them. Regions with selenium-poor soils (parts of China, New Zealand, Europe) have historically experienced selenium deficiency disorders. The RDA is 55 mcg/day for adults, with the tolerable upper limit set at 400 mcg/day. Brazil nuts are the richest food source, with 1-2 nuts providing the daily requirement, though selenium content varies widely by growing region.</p>
<p>Selenium demonstrates a U-shaped dose-response relationship: both deficiency and excess are harmful. At optimal levels (serum selenium 100-150 ng/mL), selenium supports robust immune function, reduces oxidative stress, protects against certain cancers (particularly prostate, colorectal, and lung), and is essential for male fertility (selenoprotein P in testes protects sperm from oxidative damage). Selenomethionine is the best-absorbed organic form in supplements, while selenite is the most common inorganic form.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Enables antioxidant defense through glutathione peroxidase enzymes</li>
<li>Converts thyroid hormone T4 to active T3 via deiodinase enzymes</li>
<li>Supports immune cell function and enhances viral defense</li>
<li>Protects sperm from oxidative damage and supports male fertility</li>
<li>Regulates cellular redox state through thioredoxin reductase system</li>
<li>May reduce cancer risk through DNA repair and apoptosis regulation</li>
</ul>

<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Selenium deficiency impairs thyroid hormone metabolism (contributing to hypothyroidism), reduces antioxidant capacity, and weakens immune function. Severe deficiency causes Keshan disease (endemic cardiomyopathy) and Kashin-Beck disease (osteoarthropathy). Moderate deficiency is associated with cognitive decline, increased cancer risk, male infertility, and increased susceptibility to viral infections (viruses mutate to more virulent strains in selenium-deficient hosts).</p>
<p>Selenosis (chronic selenium toxicity) occurs at intakes exceeding 400 mcg/day and manifests as garlic breath odor, brittle hair and nails, nail loss, skin rashes, nausea, diarrhea, and peripheral neuropathy. Acute selenium poisoning can cause respiratory distress, myocardial infarction, and renal failure. The SELECT trial raised concern that selenium supplementation in selenium-replete individuals may increase type 2 diabetes risk, underscoring the importance of testing selenium status before supplementing.</p>
