<h1>Diet, hs-CRP and Inflammation</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p><a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/biomarker/hs-crp/" class="internal-link">hs-CRP</a> (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein) is a number most people never think about until a doctor circles it. It is not a marker of any single disease — it is a thermostat reading of <em>low-grade, chronic inflammation</em> simmering throughout the body. That background heat accelerates <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/condition/atherosclerosis/" class="internal-link">Atherosclerosis</a>, worsens <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/condition/insulin-resistance/" class="internal-link">Insulin Resistance</a>, and tracks closely with <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/condition/cardiovascular-disease/" class="internal-link">Cardiovascular Disease</a> risk. The good news: hs-CRP is strongly diet-responsive, so what you eat is one of the most direct ways to turn the thermostat down.</p>
<h2>The Story</h2>
<p>C-reactive protein is made by the <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/organ/liver/" class="internal-link">Liver</a> in response to inflammatory signals — chiefly the cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6) — released by immune and fat tissue. When CRP is high during an infection, it spikes into the tens or hundreds. But <em>high-sensitivity</em> CRP measures the low range (roughly under 10 mg/L), where small persistent elevations reveal smoldering inflammation rather than acute illness. A value under ~1 mg/L is considered low risk, 1-3 mg/L average, and above 3 mg/L higher risk.</p>
<p>Where does the chronic signal come from? A large part is metabolic. Excess <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/labtest/vldl/" class="internal-link">VLDL</a> and <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/labtest/triglycerides/" class="internal-link">Triglycerides</a>, visceral fat, and post-meal blood-sugar spikes all generate reactive oxygen species and activate immune pathways. This is why inflammation, lipids, and glucose control are not separate problems — they share the same fire. A diet heavy in refined starch, sugar, and processed meat keeps feeding it; in studies, such &quot;Western&quot; eating patterns are consistently associated with higher CRP.</p>
<p>The reverse is also measurable. In a randomized trial in people with <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/clinicalpattern/metabolic-syndrome/" class="internal-link">Metabolic Syndrome</a>, a Mediterranean-style diet rich in vegetables, whole grains, nuts, and olive oil significantly lowered CRP, IL-6, and other vascular inflammation markers compared with a control diet — alongside improved endothelial function (PMID: 15383514). Two mechanisms do the heavy lifting. First, <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/nutrient/dietary-fiber/" class="internal-link">Dietary Fiber</a> feeds gut bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids, which calm immune signaling and strengthen the gut barrier so fewer inflammatory molecules leak into circulation. Second, the monounsaturated fat and polyphenols in olive oil, plus the omega-3s <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/nutrient/omega-3-epa/" class="internal-link">Omega-3 (EPA)</a> and <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/nutrient/omega-3-dha/" class="internal-link">Omega-3 (DHA)</a>, shift the body toward producing anti-inflammatory rather than pro-inflammatory lipid mediators. Replacing <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/nutrient/saturated-fat/" class="internal-link">Saturated Fat</a> with these unsaturated fats reinforces the effect.</p>
<h2>What This Means For You</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Treat hs-CRP as a pattern marker, not a diagnosis.</strong> A single elevated value can also reflect a recent cold, injury, or intense workout — so it should be rechecked when you are well before drawing conclusions. Persistent elevation, especially alongside high <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/labtest/triglycerides/" class="internal-link">Triglycerides</a>, low <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/labtest/hdl-cholesterol/" class="internal-link">HDL Cholesterol</a>, or rising <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/biomarker/glucose-fasting/" class="internal-link">Glucose Fasting</a>, points to metabolic inflammation that diet can address.</li>
<li><strong>What pushes it up:</strong> refined carbohydrates and added sugar, excess saturated and processed-meat fat, very low fiber intake, and excess visceral fat.</li>
<li><strong>What pulls it down:</strong> an <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/intervention/anti-inflammatory-diet/" class="internal-link">Anti-Inflammatory Diet</a> or <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/intervention/mediterranean-diet/" class="internal-link">Mediterranean Diet</a> built on vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, fish, and olive oil; the <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/intervention/dash-diet/" class="internal-link">DASH Diet</a> shows similar benefits. More <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/nutrient/dietary-fiber/" class="internal-link">Dietary Fiber</a> and more omega-3 fats are the two most reproducible single changes.</li>
<li><strong>Pair food with the basics:</strong> <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/intervention/exercise/" class="internal-link">Exercise</a> lowers CRP independently of weight loss, and losing visceral fat is one of the strongest CRP-lowering interventions of all.</li>
<li><strong>Supportive nutrients:</strong> <a href="/garden/testuser16cba4daa/supplement/omega-3-fatty-acids/" class="internal-link">Omega-3 Fatty Acids</a> modestly lower CRP in many trials, particularly when baseline inflammation is high.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>hs-CRP is a thermostat for low-grade, body-wide inflammation, not a marker of one disease.</li>
<li>The inflammatory signal is largely metabolic — shared with high triglycerides, visceral fat, and glucose spikes.</li>
<li>Mediterranean-style and anti-inflammatory eating lowers CRP and IL-6 in randomized trials.</li>
<li>Fiber (via gut short-chain fatty acids) and omega-3 fats are the two most reproducible food levers.</li>
<li>Recheck an isolated high CRP when well — infection, injury, or hard exercise can transiently raise it.</li>
</ul>
