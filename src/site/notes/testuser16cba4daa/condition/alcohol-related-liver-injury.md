<h1>Alcohol-Related Liver Injury</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Alcohol-related liver injury (also called alcohol-associated or alcoholic liver disease, ALD) is a progressive spectrum of hepatic damage driven by chronic excessive alcohol consumption. It begins with hepatic steatosis (fatty liver), which develops in the majority of heavy drinkers and is largely reversible with abstinence. With continued drinking, a subset of patients progress to alcoholic steatohepatitis (alcoholic hepatitis), hepatic fibrosis, and ultimately cirrhosis, with its attendant risks of portal hypertension, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma.</p>
<p>The disease is one of the most common causes of liver-related death globally, and its burden is rising in many regions. Crucially, ALD is largely preventable and modifiable: the dominant determinant of outcome at every stage is the patient's drinking trajectory, with sustained abstinence improving survival even in advanced disease. Because liver injury is often clinically silent until late, a high proportion of cases present only once cirrhosis or decompensation has developed.</p>
<p>Pathophysiologically, ethanol metabolism generates acetaldehyde and reactive oxygen species, depletes hepatic glutathione, and promotes mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid accumulation, and inflammatory signaling. Gut-derived endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide) reaching the liver via a more permeable intestinal barrier activates Kupffer cells and amplifies cytokine-driven hepatocyte injury and fibrogenesis. This convergence of oxidative stress, steatosis, and innate immune activation underlies the rationale for both antioxidant adjuncts and anti-inflammatory therapy in severe disease.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Early ALD (simple steatosis) is typically asymptomatic and discovered incidentally through abnormal liver enzymes or imaging. As injury advances, patients may report fatigue, right-upper-quadrant discomfort, nausea, anorexia, and weight loss. Severe alcoholic hepatitis presents acutely with jaundice, tender hepatomegaly, fever, and signs of systemic inflammation, often superimposed on chronic liver disease. Decompensated cirrhosis manifests with ascites, hepatic encephalopathy (confusion, asterixis), variceal bleeding, jaundice, and stigmata such as spider angiomata, palmar erythema, and gynecomastia.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis rests on a documented history of significant alcohol intake combined with a compatible biochemical and imaging picture, after excluding viral, autoimmune, and metabolic causes. The transaminase pattern is characteristic: AST and ALT are usually only modestly elevated, with an AST:ALT ratio greater than 2:1, while gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) is frequently markedly raised. Rising bilirubin, falling albumin, and a prolonged INR signal impaired synthetic function and worse prognosis. Non-invasive elastography and scoring systems (e.g., discriminant function and MELD for alcoholic hepatitis) help stage disease and guide therapy; liver biopsy is reserved for diagnostic uncertainty.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The quantity and duration of alcohol exposure are the principal drivers, but susceptibility varies widely. Women develop ALD at lower cumulative doses than men. Obesity and coexisting metabolic dysfunction act synergistically with alcohol to accelerate steatohepatitis and fibrosis. Genetic polymorphisms—most notably PNPLA3 (I148M) and TM6SF2—modulate individual risk, and concurrent viral hepatitis or other hepatotoxin exposure compounds injury.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>The cornerstone of management at every stage is complete alcohol abstinence, paired with structured treatment of alcohol use disorder through behavioral support and, where appropriate, pharmacotherapy (naltrexone, acamprosate, or baclofen). Nutritional rehabilitation—adequate protein and calories, plus repletion of thiamine and folate—is essential given the high prevalence of malnutrition. For severe alcoholic hepatitis, corticosteroids (prednisolone) remain the principal evidence-based intervention to reduce short-term mortality in appropriately selected patients, though benefit is modest and response is assessed early (e.g., by the Lille model); patient stratification and emerging anti-inflammatory and antioxidant strategies are active areas of research. Complications of cirrhosis are managed with diuretics for ascites, lactulose for encephalopathy, non-selective beta-blockers for variceal prophylaxis, and prompt antibiotics for infection. Liver transplantation, increasingly offered to carefully selected patients with severe disease, can be life-saving when other measures fail.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Progressive ALD can culminate in cirrhosis with portal hypertension, ascites, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal hemorrhage, and hepatorenal syndrome. Long-standing cirrhosis carries a sustained risk of hepatocellular carcinoma, warranting surveillance. Severe alcoholic hepatitis is itself a medical emergency with high 28- and 90-day mortality in its most severe forms.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Outcomes are strongly stage- and behavior-dependent. Simple steatosis is largely reversible with abstinence, whereas alcoholic hepatitis and cirrhosis carry substantial mortality. Continued drinking is the strongest predictor of progression and death, while sustained abstinence improves survival at every stage and is often a prerequisite for transplant candidacy.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Alcohol-related liver disease is a leading global contributor to liver-related deaths, and its incidence has risen in many countries alongside increasing alcohol consumption. The burden falls disproportionately on working-age adults and is amplified where harmful drinking patterns and metabolic risk factors coexist, underscoring the public-health value of population-level alcohol policy.</p>
