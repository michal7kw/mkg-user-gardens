<h1>Pneumonia</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Pneumonia is an <strong>acute infection of the pulmonary parenchyma</strong> (alveoli and interstitial tissue) that produces <strong>cough, fever, dyspnea, radiographic infiltrates</strong>, and impaired gas exchange. It is the <strong>leading infectious cause of death worldwide</strong> and a leading cause of hospitalization in all age groups. Classification by acquisition setting guides empiric therapy: <strong>community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)</strong> develops outside the hospital or within 48 hours of admission; <strong>hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP)</strong> develops after 48 hours; <strong>ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)</strong> develops after 48 hours of mechanical ventilation; and <strong>aspiration pneumonia</strong> results from inhalation of oropharyngeal or gastric contents.</p>
<p>The microbiology of CAP is dominated by <strong>Streptococcus pneumoniae</strong> (~20-60% of cases; the pneumococcus), followed by <strong>Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis</strong> (especially in COPD), the <strong>atypical pathogens</strong> (<em>Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila</em>), and <strong>respiratory viruses</strong> (influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2, adenovirus). HAP and VAP shift toward <strong>gram-negative bacilli</strong> (<em>Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter, E. coli</em>) and <strong>Staphylococcus aureus</strong> (including MRSA). The introduction of <strong>pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV)</strong> has substantially altered the epidemiology, reducing invasive pneumococcal disease and shifting serotypes.</p>
<p>The clinical presentation varies by pathogen and patient. <strong>Typical pneumonia</strong> (pneumococcus): acute onset with <strong>high fever, productive purulent cough, pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, lobar consolidation</strong> on imaging. <strong>Atypical pneumonia</strong> (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia): more gradual onset, <strong>dry cough, low-grade fever, headache, myalgias, diffuse interstitial infiltrates</strong>, often younger patients (&quot;walking pneumonia&quot;). <strong>Aspiration pneumonia</strong>: dependent lobe (right lower in upright, posterior upper in supine), foul-smelling sputum, polymicrobial. <strong>Elderly patients often present atypically</strong> -- <strong>confusion, falls, weakness, anorexia, no fever</strong> -- making a high index of suspicion essential. Diagnosis requires <strong>clinical features plus radiographic infiltrate</strong>; <strong>CURB-65</strong> (Confusion, Urea &gt;19 mg/dL, Respiratory rate &gt;=30, Blood pressure systolic &lt;90 or diastolic &lt;=60, age &gt;=65) or <strong>PSI</strong> (Pneumonia Severity Index) guide the site-of-care decision.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Assess severity and site of care:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>CURB-65 score</strong> -- 0-1: outpatient; 2: inpatient (general ward); &gt;=3: ICU consideration</li>
<li><strong>PSI</strong> -- more complex; integrates demographics, comorbidities, vitals, labs</li>
<li>Hospitalize for: hypoxemia (SpO2 &lt;92%), respiratory distress, hemodynamic instability, inability to tolerate oral intake, social factors</li>
<li>ICU for: septic shock requiring vasopressors, respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Empiric antibiotic therapy (start promptly, within 4 hours):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Outpatient (no comorbidities, no MRSA/Pseudomonas risk factors):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Amoxicillin 1 g TID</strong> (preferred in regions with low pneumococcal resistance)</li>
<li><strong>Doxycycline 100 mg BID</strong></li>
<li><strong>Macrolide</strong> (azithromycin, clarithromycin) -- only in regions with low macrolide resistance</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Outpatient (with comorbidities: heart, lung, liver, renal disease; diabetes; alcoholism; malignancy; asplenia; immunosuppression):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Beta-lactam + macrolide</strong> -- amoxicillin/clavulanate 875/125 BID OR cefpodoxime + azithromycin 500 mg day 1, then 250 mg daily</li>
<li><strong>Respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy</strong> -- levofloxacin 750 mg daily, moxifloxacin 400 mg daily (avoid in QT prolongation, aortic disease, myasthenia)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Inpatient (non-ICU):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily + azithromycin 500 mg IV/PO daily</strong> (beta-lactam + macrolide)</li>
<li>OR <strong>respiratory fluoroquinolone</strong> monotherapy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Inpatient (ICU):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Beta-lactam (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or ampicillin-sulbactam) + macrolide</strong> OR <strong>beta-lactam + respiratory fluoroquinolone</strong></li>
<li>Add <strong>MRSA coverage</strong> (vancomycin or linezolid) if risk factors</li>
<li>Add <strong>Pseudomonas coverage</strong> (piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, or meropenem) if risk factors (bronchiectasis, recent antibiotics, recent hospitalization)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Aspiration pneumonia:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ampicillin-sulbactam</strong> OR <strong>clindamycin</strong> (anaerobic coverage)</li>
<li>Avoid extended anaerobic coverage if uncomplicated aspiration without abscess</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Duration:</strong> 5-7 days for most CAP (shorter courses are non-inferior and reduce resistance); longer for <em>S. aureus</em>, <em>Pseudomonas</em>, Legionella, complications</p>
<p><strong>Adjunctive and supportive therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Oxygen</strong> to maintain SpO2 &gt;=92% (&gt;=94% if critical illness, pregnancy, critical perfusion)</li>
<li><strong>IV fluids</strong> for hypovolemia; avoid overload (worsens gas exchange)</li>
<li><strong>Early mobilization</strong> once stable</li>
<li><strong>Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis</strong> for hospitalized patients</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroids</strong> -- short course (hydrocortisone 200 mg/day x 4-7 days, or prednisone 40 mg x 5 days) for severe CAP with septic shock or high inflammatory burden; mixed evidence but commonly used</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Vaccination (prevention):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pneumococcal</strong> -- <strong>PCV20</strong> (single dose; preferred) or <strong>PCV15 followed by PPSV23</strong> in immunocompromised, all adults &gt;=65, and high-risk younger adults</li>
<li><strong>Influenza</strong> annually (reduces influenza pneumonia and post-influenza bacterial superinfection)</li>
<li><strong>COVID-19</strong> per current guidelines</li>
<li><strong>RSV</strong> for adults &gt;=60, pregnant women, and infants</li>
<li><strong>Hib</strong> (for specific indications: asplenia, complement deficiency)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Complications to monitor:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pleural effusion and empyema</strong> -- drain if infected</li>
<li><strong>Lung abscess</strong> -- prolonged antibiotics; surgical drainage if large</li>
<li><strong>ARDS</strong> -- lung-protective ventilation</li>
<li><strong>Septic shock</strong> -- early goal-directed therapy, vasopressors, source control</li>
</ul>
