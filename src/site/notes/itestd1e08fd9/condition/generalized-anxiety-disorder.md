<h1>Generalized Anxiety Disorder</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) is a chronic psychiatric condition defined by <strong>excessive and difficult-to-control worry</strong> about a range of everyday concerns - health, finances, work, family - that persists more days than not for at least six months. The worry is disproportionate to actual circumstances and is accompanied by physical and cognitive symptoms including restlessness, fatigue, muscle tension, irritability, impaired concentration, and disturbed sleep. Unlike fear, which is a response to an immediate threat, the anxiety of GAD is anticipatory and diffuse.</p>
<p>GAD is among the most common anxiety disorders in primary care, with a lifetime prevalence in the population of several percent and roughly double the prevalence in women compared with men. It typically follows a chronic, fluctuating course and is highly comorbid: a majority of patients also meet criteria for depression or another anxiety disorder over their lifetime, which complicates diagnosis and treatment. The disorder arises from an interaction of genetic predisposition, temperament (behavioral inhibition, neuroticism), and environmental stressors, with dysregulation of fear and worry circuits involving the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and the serotonergic, noradrenergic, and GABAergic neurotransmitter systems.</p>
<p>Clinically, GAD matters because it is frequently underrecognized despite causing substantial functional impairment, and because it responds well to treatment. The brief, validated <strong>GAD-7</strong> questionnaire is widely used for screening and monitoring severity, while first-line management combines cognitive behavioral therapy with serotonergic pharmacotherapy. Because of the strong overlap with depression, the <strong>PHQ-9</strong> is often administered alongside it.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Patients describe persistent, uncontrollable worry that jumps from topic to topic, often accompanied by a sense of impending misfortune. The somatic burden is prominent: muscle tension and tension headaches, restlessness, fatigue, gastrointestinal upset, and sleep disturbance - particularly difficulty falling asleep because the mind will not &quot;switch off.&quot; Concentration problems and irritability are common and may be the chief complaint. Many patients present first to primary care with physical symptoms rather than naming anxiety.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is clinical, based on DSM-5 criteria: excessive anxiety and worry occurring more days than not for at least six months, difficulty controlling the worry, and at least three of six associated symptoms (restlessness, easy fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance), causing significant distress or impairment, and not better explained by another disorder, substance, or medical condition. The <strong>GAD-7</strong> rating scale quantifies severity and tracks treatment response; thyroid disease, arrhythmia, and substance/caffeine effects should be excluded.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>GAD reflects gene-environment interaction. Risk is increased by female sex, a family history of anxiety or mood disorders, childhood adversity or trauma, temperamental behavioral inhibition and high neuroticism, chronic stress, and the presence of comorbid depression or another anxiety disorder.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>First-line treatment is <strong>cognitive behavioral therapy</strong>, which targets the cognitive distortions and avoidance that maintain worry. First-line pharmacotherapy comprises SSRIs (e.g., escitalopram, sertraline) and SNRIs (e.g., venlafaxine, duloxetine); buspirone and, for short-term use, pregabalin are alternatives, while benzodiazepines are generally avoided for long-term management because of dependence. Mindfulness-based interventions, regular aerobic exercise, sleep optimization, and reducing caffeine and alcohol are valuable adjuncts.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Untreated or undertreated GAD frequently progresses to or co-occurs with <strong>major depressive disorder</strong> and other anxiety disorders such as panic disorder and social anxiety disorder. Chronic insomnia, substance misuse (self-medication), and persistent functional impairment with reduced quality of life are common.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>GAD tends to be chronic and relapsing, but the majority of patients improve meaningfully with adequate treatment. Outcomes are better with early recognition, combined psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy, and management of comorbid depression; residual symptoms and comorbidity predict a more persistent course.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>GAD affects roughly 3-6% of adults over their lifetime and is about twice as common in women as in men. It is one of the most frequently encountered anxiety disorders in primary care, often beginning in adulthood and following a waxing-and-waning course, with high rates of comorbid depression and other anxiety disorders.</p>
