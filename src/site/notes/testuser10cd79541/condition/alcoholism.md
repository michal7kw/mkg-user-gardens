<h1>Alcoholism</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) -- colloquially &quot;alcoholism&quot; -- is a <strong>chronic relapsing brain disease</strong> defined by the <strong>DSM-5</strong> as a problematic pattern of alcohol use leading to clinically significant impairment, with <strong>11 criteria</strong> spanning impaired control, social impairment, risky use, and pharmacological indicators (tolerance and withdrawal). Meeting <strong>&gt;=2 criteria within 12 months</strong> establishes the diagnosis, with severity graded as mild (2-3), moderate (4-5), or severe (&gt;=6). Globally, AUD affects an estimated <strong>283 million people</strong> (5.1% of adults) and causes approximately <strong>3 million deaths annually</strong> -- roughly 5% of all deaths worldwide -- through intoxication-related injury, organ toxicity, cancer, and withdrawal.</p>
<p>The neurobiology of AUD centers on <strong>dopaminergic reward circuits</strong> (ventral tegmental area to nucleus accumbens) and progressive <strong>neuroadaptation</strong> in stress (CRF, dynorphin) and executive (prefrontal cortex) systems. Acute alcohol enhances <strong>GABA</strong> and inhibits <strong>NMDA glutamate</strong> receptors, producing the characteristic anxiolysis, disinhibition, and sedation. With chronic use the brain counter-regulates -- <strong>downregulating GABA receptors and upregulating NMDA</strong> -- so abrupt cessation unmasks CNS hyperexcitability (anxiety, tremor, seizures, delirium tremens). The stress system is recruited (CRF in the amygdala drives the <strong>negative reinforcement</strong> of continued drinking), and prefrontal cortex atrophy impairs decision-making and inhibitory control, perpetuating compulsive use despite harm.</p>
<p>The medical complications of AUD are protean: <strong>Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome</strong> (thiamine deficiency; acute Wernicke = confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia; chronic Korsakoff = irreversible confabulatory amnesia), <strong>alcoholic liver disease</strong> (steatosis -&gt; hepatitis -&gt; cirrhosis -&gt; hepatocellular carcinoma), <strong>acute and chronic pancreatitis</strong>, <strong>alcoholic cardiomyopathy</strong> and <strong>atrial arrhythmias</strong> (&quot;holiday heart&quot;), <strong>peripheral and autonomic neuropathy</strong>, <strong>gastritis and peptic ulcer</strong>, <strong>immune suppression</strong> (predisposing to pneumonia, TB), and <strong>cancers</strong> of the oropharynx, esophagus, liver, breast, and colon. AUD also drives <strong>trauma, violence, family disruption, and productivity loss</strong>, making it one of the leading contributors to global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Screening and diagnosis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>AUDIT-C</strong> (3-item) or full <strong>AUDIT</strong> (10-item) in primary care; <strong>&gt;=4</strong> (men) or <strong>&gt;=3</strong> (women) on AUDIT-C warrants full evaluation</li>
<li>DSM-5 criteria for severity</li>
<li>Comprehensive medical and psychiatric assessment (look for liver disease, neuropathy, depression, anxiety, trauma)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Acute withdrawal management (detoxification):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Assess severity with <strong>CIWA-Ar</strong> (Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, revised)</li>
<li><strong>Benzodiazepines</strong> are the cornerstone -- <strong>symptom-triggered</strong> dosing (CIWA-Ar-guided) is superior to fixed schedule; long-acting (diazepam, chlordiazepoxide) preferred; lorazepam/oxazepam if liver disease</li>
<li><strong>Thiamine 100 mg IV/IM daily for 3-5 days BEFORE any glucose</strong> -- prevents Wernicke encephalopathy</li>
<li>Fluid and electrolyte correction (especially magnesium, potassium, phosphate)</li>
<li>Seizure precautions; IV lorazepam for withdrawal seizures</li>
<li><strong>Delirium tremens</strong> (DTs): ICU admission, high-dose benzodiazepines, supportive care; mortality 5-15% untreated</li>
<li>Address comorbid medical issues (infection, GI bleed, pancreatitis)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>FDA-approved pharmacotherapy for AUD maintenance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Naltrexone</strong> (50 mg/day oral or 380 mg IM monthly -- Vivitrol) -- opioid antagonist; reduces heavy-drinking days and craving; first-line. Avoid in opioid use or acute hepatitis</li>
<li><strong>Acamprosate</strong> (666 mg TID) -- modulates glutamate; supports maintenance of abstinence; best for patients committed to abstinence</li>
<li><strong>Disulfiram</strong> (250 mg/day) -- aversive therapy; acetaldehyde accumulation produces nausea, flushing, hypotension if alcohol consumed; adherence-dependent, best with supervised use</li>
<li>Off-label: <strong>topiramate</strong> (200-300 mg/day), <strong>gabapentin</strong> (300-1800 mg/day), <strong>ondansetron</strong> in early-onset AUD</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Behavioral therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cognitive behavioral therapy</strong> (CBT) -- identify triggers, develop coping skills</li>
<li><strong>Motivational interviewing</strong> -- enhances intrinsic motivation to change</li>
<li><strong>12-step facilitation</strong> and <strong>mutual-help groups</strong> (Alcoholics Anonymous, SMART Recovery)</li>
<li><strong>Contingency management</strong> and <strong>community reinforcement approach</strong></li>
<li><strong>Family therapy</strong> and couples-based treatment</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Treat comorbidities:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Depression and anxiety (SSRIs; avoid bupropion in withdrawal seizure risk)</li>
<li>PTSD (trauma-focused therapy; SSRIs)</li>
<li>Sleep disorders (CBT-I; avoid benzodiazepines long-term)</li>
<li>Other substance use disorders (opioids, tobacco, cannabis)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Long-term monitoring and relapse prevention:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Regular follow-up; AUD is chronic and relapsing -- relapse is common and not failure</li>
<li>Continue pharmacotherapy for at least 6-12 months; indefinite in many</li>
<li>Build recovery capital (social support, employment, housing, meaning)</li>
<li>Address psychosocial barriers (housing, employment, legal, family)</li>
</ul>
