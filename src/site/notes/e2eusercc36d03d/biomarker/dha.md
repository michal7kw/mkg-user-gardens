<h1>Dha</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; 22:6n-3) is a 22-carbon, long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid that is the most abundant omega-3 fatty acid in the brain and retina. DHA comprises approximately <strong>40% of the polyunsaturated fatty acids in the brain</strong> and <strong>60% of the polyunsaturated fatty acids in retinal photoreceptors</strong> -- reflecting its critical structural role in these tissues. DHA is obtained primarily from fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring), shellfish, and <strong>algal oil supplements</strong> (the original plant source of DHA in the marine food web). DHA can be endogenously synthesized from alpha-linolenic acid (ALA; the plant omega-3), but this conversion is <strong>extremely inefficient (&lt;5%) in humans</strong>, making dietary intake critical.</p>
<p>While EPA (the companion marine omega-3) is predominantly anti-inflammatory via eicosanoid modulation, DHA's primary roles are <strong>structural and neuroprotective</strong>. DHA maintains membrane fluidity in neurons and photoreceptors, supports synaptic plasticity, promotes neurogenesis, and is the precursor to specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) called <strong>protectins, maresins, and resolvins of the D-series</strong> that actively resolve neuroinflammation. DHA is especially critical during the <strong>third trimester of pregnancy and the first two years of life</strong> when rapid brain growth occurs; maternal DHA status directly influences infant neurocognitive outcomes and visual acuity.</p>
<p>In adults, low DHA levels are associated with <strong>accelerated brain aging, cognitive decline, increased Alzheimer's disease risk</strong>, and visual impairment. DHA supplementation has shown promise in some (but not all) clinical trials for cognitive aging, mood disorders (especially perinatal depression), and dry eye syndrome. Combined EPA+DHA supplementation is well-established for cardiovascular risk reduction (REDUCE-IT for EPA; mixed trials for EPA+DHA).</p>
<p>DHA blood levels are measured as part of an omega-3 fatty acid panel, either as a plasma/serum concentration (reflecting recent 2-4 week intake) or within the <strong>Omega-3 Index</strong> (EPA+DHA in RBC membranes, reflecting 3-4 months of intake; the longer-term marker). The Omega-3 Index (&gt;=8% cardioprotective) is the most clinically validated long-term marker.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (well-validated biomarker; extensive research on brain, cardiovascular, and pregnancy outcomes).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>DHA levels tend to be higher in women than men due to estrogen-enhanced conversion from ALA</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy recommendations:</strong> DHA intake of at least 200-300 mg/day; many experts recommend 600-1000 mg/day</li>
<li>DHA is more variable than EPA in response to supplementation; individuals with higher baseline omega-6 intake show smaller increases</li>
<li>Reference range is assay-specific (gas chromatography, LC-MS/MS)</li>
<li><strong>Vegetarian / vegan</strong> populations typically have very low DHA unless supplemented with algal DHA</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase DHA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dietary fatty fish intake</strong> (salmon 2-3x/week raises DHA 30-50%)</li>
<li><strong>Algal oil supplementation</strong> (vegan source of DHA; 200-1000 mg/day)</li>
<li><strong>Fish oil or krill oil supplementation</strong> (1-4 g/day EPA+DHA; most supplements contain more EPA than DHA)</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean, Japanese, plant-based whole-food diets</strong></li>
<li><strong>Flaxseed, chia, walnut</strong> (ALA precursor; conversion to DHA ~2-5% -- inefficient)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease DHA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Western diet</strong> (low fish intake, high omega-6 from processed seed oils)</li>
<li><strong>Vegetarian / vegan diet without DHA supplementation</strong> (very low DHA; ALA conversion too inefficient)</li>
<li><strong>Alcohol excess</strong></li>
<li><strong>Smoking</strong> (impaired omega-3 metabolism)</li>
<li><strong>Aging</strong> (decreased conversion of ALA to DHA)</li>
<li><strong>FADS1/FADS2 polymorphisms</strong> (reduced conversion)</li>
<li><strong>Renal failure, dialysis</strong> (altered lipid metabolism)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low DHA is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cognitive decline and Alzheimer's disease:</strong> low brain and plasma DHA associated with faster cognitive decline; some RCTs show benefit of DHA supplementation (especially in APOE4 non-carriers)</li>
<li><strong>Poor visual acuity and retinal function:</strong> DHA deficiency impairs retinal development and function</li>
<li><strong>Perinatal and postpartum depression:</strong> low DHA linked to perinatal mood disorders</li>
<li><strong>Impaired infant neurodevelopment</strong> if maternal DHA low during pregnancy/lactation</li>
<li><strong>ADHD symptom severity</strong> in children (mixed evidence)</li>
<li><strong>Dry eye syndrome, age-related macular degeneration</strong></li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular disease</strong> (as part of overall low omega-3)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adequate/High DHA is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Better cognitive performance, memory, processing speed</strong></li>
<li><strong>Reduced risk of age-related macular degeneration</strong></li>
<li><strong>Better mood stability and lower depression risk</strong> (especially perinatal)</li>
<li><strong>Healthy pregnancy outcomes and infant brain/eye development</strong></li>
<li><strong>Reduced cardiovascular risk</strong> (as part of overall omega-3 status; REDUCE-IT used pure EPA but DHA co-supplementation trials also positive)</li>
<li>Very high levels are generally well-tolerated; mild GI effects or fishy aftertaste possible with high-dose supplementation</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>DHA alone vs. EPA alone vs. EPA+DHA:</strong> clinical trials have produced mixed results; the relative benefit of each remains debated</li>
<li><strong>REDUCE-IT used pure EPA</strong> -- DHA was lowered in the treatment group; suggests EPA may be the CV-benefit component</li>
<li><strong>Cognitive trials of DHA</strong> have produced mixed results; benefit may depend on baseline status, APOE genotype, and timing</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy supplementation</strong> trials show reduced preterm birth but inconsistent cognitive benefit in offspring</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Cognitive aging risk assessment (with Omega-3 Index)</li>
<li>Pregnancy omega-3 adequacy assessment (especially vegan/vegetarian)</li>
<li>Cardiovascular risk stratification (as part of Omega-3 Index)</li>
<li>Anti-inflammatory dietary intervention monitoring</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>DHA responds within 2-4 weeks of dietary or supplemental intervention:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Fatty fish intake</strong> 2-3 servings/week (salmon, mackerel, sardines, anchovies, herring) -- the most natural source</li>
<li><strong>Algal oil supplementation</strong> (vegan source of DHA; 200-1000 mg/day) -- suitable for vegetarians/vegans</li>
<li><strong>Fish oil or krill oil supplementation</strong> 1-4 g/day EPA+DHA (most products contain EPA and DHA)</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong> rich in olive oil, fish, vegetables</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> DHA supplementation 200-1000 mg/day from low-mercury fish or algal oil; especially important for vegans</li>
<li><strong>Lactation:</strong> continue DHA supplementation 200-1000 mg/day to support infant brain/eye development via breast milk</li>
<li><strong>Reduce omega-6 intake</strong> (processed seed oils -- corn, soybean, sunflower, cottonseed) -- improves AA:EPA:DHA balance</li>
<li><strong>Stop smoking</strong> and <strong>reduce alcohol</strong></li>
<li><strong>Plant-based ALA sources</strong> (flaxseed, chia, walnut) -- modest conversion (~2-5% to DHA); insufficient alone for vegans</li>
</ol>
