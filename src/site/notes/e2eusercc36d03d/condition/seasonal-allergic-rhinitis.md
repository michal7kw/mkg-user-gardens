<h1>Seasonal Allergic Rhinitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Seasonal allergic rhinitis (SAR), commonly known as <strong>hay fever</strong>, is an IgE-mediated allergic inflammatory response of the nasal mucosa to seasonal aeroallergens -- primarily <strong>tree pollen</strong> (spring), <strong>grass pollen</strong> (late spring/summer), and <strong>weed pollen</strong> (fall; especially ragweed). It affects approximately <strong>10-30% of adults and 40% of children</strong> worldwide, with increasing prevalence attributed to environmental changes, urbanization, and climate change (which extends pollen seasons and increases pollen production).</p>
<p>The pathophysiology involves a <strong>Type I (immediate) hypersensitivity reaction</strong>:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Sensitization:</strong> allergen exposure -&gt; B-cell class switching -&gt; IgE production -&gt; IgE binds to mast cells and basophils in the nasal mucosa</li>
<li><strong>Early-phase response (minutes):</strong> repeat allergen exposure -&gt; cross-links IgE on mast cells -&gt; degranulation -&gt; release of histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes -&gt; sneezing, rhinorrhea, pruritus</li>
<li><strong>Late-phase response (4-8 hours):</strong> recruitment of inflammatory cells (eosinophils, basophils, Th2 lymphocytes) -&gt; sustained congestion and inflammation</li>
</ol>
<p>The &quot;<strong>United Airway Disease</strong>&quot; concept recognizes the strong link between allergic rhinitis and asthma: <strong>up to 40% of patients with allergic rhinitis have asthma</strong>, and up to 80% of asthmatics have allergic rhinitis. Treating allergic rhinitis reduces asthma exacerbations. The &quot;<strong>allergic march</strong>&quot; describes the typical progression from atopic dermatitis (infancy) -&gt; food allergy (toddler) -&gt; allergic rhinitis (childhood) -&gt; asthma (later childhood/adulthood).</p>
<p>Despite being a non-life-threatening condition, SAR has significant quality-of-life impact: impaired sleep, reduced cognitive performance, school absenteeism, and decreased work productivity (&quot;presenteeism&quot;). The annual economic burden in the US exceeds $10 billion.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Stepwise approach:</strong></p>
<p><strong>Step 1: Allergen avoidance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Monitor pollen counts; stay indoors when high</li>
<li>Keep windows closed during pollen season; use air conditioning with HEPA filter</li>
<li>Shower and change clothes after outdoor activities</li>
<li>Avoid outdoor activities in early morning (peak pollen)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Step 2: Pharmacotherapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Intranasal corticosteroids</strong> (first-line; most effective single therapy):
<ul>
<li>Fluticasone propionate 50 mcg, 2 sprays each nostril daily</li>
<li>Mometasone furoate 50 mcg, 2 sprays each nostril daily</li>
<li>Triamcinolone acetonide 55 mcg, 2 sprays each nostril daily</li>
<li>Budesonide 32 mcg, 2-4 sprays each nostril daily</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Oral second-generation antihistamines</strong> (non-sedating; for itching/sneezing):
<ul>
<li>Cetirizine 10 mg daily</li>
<li>Loratadine 10 mg daily</li>
<li>Fexofenadine 180 mg daily</li>
<li>Bilastine 20 mg daily</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Intranasal antihistamines</strong> (azelastine, olopatadine; rapid onset; for acute symptoms)</li>
<li><strong>Intranasal combination</strong> (azelastine + fluticasone; Dymista; more effective than either alone)</li>
<li><strong>Intranasal cromolyn</strong> 4% (mast cell stabilizer; OTC; less effective; prophylactic use)</li>
<li><strong>Leukotriene receptor antagonist</strong> (montelukast 10 mg; less effective than INS; black box warning for neuropsychiatric effects)</li>
<li><strong>Intranasal ipratropium</strong> 0.06% (for rhinorrhea; anticholinergic)</li>
<li><strong>Oral decongestants</strong> (pseudoephedrine; short-term only; caution: hypertension, insomnia)</li>
<li><strong>Ocular antihistamines</strong> (olopatadine, ketotifen eye drops; for allergic conjunctivitis)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Step 3: Allergen immunotherapy (for moderate-severe or refractory disease):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT):</strong> weekly injections during build-up, then monthly maintenance for 3-5 years; highly effective; risk of anaphylaxis</li>
<li><strong>Sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT):</strong> daily tablets or drops under the tongue (ragweed, grass, tree); safer than SCIT (lower anaphylaxis risk); FDA-approved products available</li>
<li><strong>Disease-modifying effect:</strong> immunotherapy is the only treatment that can permanently alter the immune response and potentially cure the allergy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Omalizumab</strong> (anti-IgE): for severe refractory allergic rhinitis (especially with concomitant asthma)</p>
