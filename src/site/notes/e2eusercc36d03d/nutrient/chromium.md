<h1>Chromium</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Chromium is an essential trace mineral that plays a central role in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism by enhancing insulin signaling. It functions as part of the chromodulin complex (also known as low-molecular-weight chromium-binding substance), which amplifies insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity, thereby improving glucose uptake into cells.</p>
<p>Research suggests that chromium supplementation may benefit individuals with impaired glucose tolerance or type 2 diabetes by improving insulin sensitivity and reducing fasting blood glucose. The most studied supplemental form is chromium picolinate, though chromium polynicotinate and chromium-enriched yeast are also used. Bioavailability from food sources is low, typically 0.4-2.5%.</p>
<p>Chromium also participates in lipid metabolism, with some evidence showing reductions in total cholesterol and triglycerides. Vitamin C and niacin enhance chromium absorption, while high intakes of simple sugars, phytates, and zinc may impair it. Modern food processing and high-sugar diets may increase chromium excretion, contributing to marginal deficiency.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Enhances insulin receptor signaling via the chromodulin complex</li>
<li>Improves cellular glucose uptake and glycemic control</li>
<li>Supports lipid metabolism and may reduce total cholesterol</li>
<li>Participates in macronutrient metabolism (carbohydrates, fats, proteins)</li>
<li>May reduce carbohydrate cravings and support appetite regulation</li>
<li>Supports lean body mass maintenance during weight management</li>
</ul>

<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Chromium deficiency manifests as impaired glucose tolerance, insulin resistance, elevated fasting blood glucose, and weight gain. It is more common in individuals consuming highly refined diets, in the elderly, and during pregnancy. Marginal deficiency is believed to be widespread but difficult to diagnose due to lack of reliable biomarkers.</p>
<p>Trivalent chromium (the dietary form) has low toxicity, with the upper limit not firmly established. However, hexavalent chromium (industrial exposure) is carcinogenic and nephrotoxic. Very high supplemental doses of chromium picolinate have been associated with kidney damage, liver toxicity, and potential DNA damage in isolated reports.</p>
