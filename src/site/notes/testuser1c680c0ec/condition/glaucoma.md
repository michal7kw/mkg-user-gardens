<h1>Glaucoma</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Glaucoma is an <strong>optic neuropathy</strong> defined by the progressive loss of <strong>retinal ganglion cells</strong> and their axons, producing characteristic <strong>cupping of the optic disc</strong> and <strong>visual field loss</strong>. It is the <strong>leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide</strong>, affecting an estimated <strong>80 million people</strong> globally (projected 111 million by 2040). The major types are <strong>primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)</strong> -- chronic, painless, insidious loss of peripheral vision that may progress to tunnel vision and blindness -- and <strong>primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG)</strong> -- which can present as an acute, painful emergency requiring immediate intervention.</p>
<p>The pathophysiology centers on <strong>aqueous humor dynamics</strong>. Aqueous humor is produced by the <strong>ciliary body</strong> and drains through the <strong>trabecular meshwork</strong> and Schlemm's canal into the episcleral veins. In <strong>open-angle glaucoma</strong> the angle is open but the trabecular meshwork has increased outflow resistance, raising <strong>intraocular pressure (IOP)</strong>. In <strong>angle-closure glaucoma</strong> the iris obstructs the angle (often in hyperopic eyes with shallow anterior chambers), causing acute IOP elevation. Elevated IOP is the <strong>only modifiable risk factor</strong> and the target of all current therapy, though up to one-third of patients have <strong>normal-tension glaucoma</strong> (NTG), implicating <strong>vascular insufficiency</strong>, <strong>autoimmune mechanisms</strong>, and <strong>nocturnal hypotension</strong> in pathogenesis.</p>
<p>The clinical importance is the <strong>silent, irreversible progression</strong> of open-angle glaucoma: patients are asymptomatic until significant optic nerve damage has occurred, and vision already lost cannot be recovered. This underscores the critical role of <strong>screening</strong> (tonometry, ophthalmoscopy, OCT, perimetry) in at-risk populations -- particularly <strong>African, Hispanic, and Asian individuals</strong>, <strong>those over 60</strong>, <strong>diabetics</strong>, <strong>myopes</strong>, and those with <strong>family history</strong>. <strong>Acute angle-closure glaucoma</strong>, by contrast, is a dramatic emergency: severe unilateral eye pain, headache, nausea/vomiting, halos around lights, a mid-dilated fixed pupil, corneal edema, and IOP often &gt;50 mmHg; <strong>irreversible vision loss can occur within hours</strong> without urgent <strong>laser peripheral iridotomy</strong>.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Goal:</strong> reduce IOP by 20-50% from baseline; individualize target based on severity, age, and life expectancy.</p>
<p><strong>Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Medical (first-line for most patients):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prostaglandin analogs</strong> -- <strong>latanoprost 0.005%</strong> nightly, <strong>bimatoprost 0.01%</strong>, <strong>travoprost 0.004%</strong>, <strong>tafluprost</strong>. Reduce IOP 25-35% by increasing uveoscleral outflow. Once-daily; minimal systemic side effects</li>
<li><strong>Beta-blockers</strong> -- <strong>timolol 0.25-0.5%</strong> BID; reduce aqueous production. <strong>Contraindicated in asthma/COPD, bradycardia</strong></li>
<li><strong>Alpha-2 agonists</strong> -- <strong>brimonidine 0.1-0.2%</strong> TID; reduce production and increase uveoscleral outflow</li>
<li><strong>Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors</strong> -- <strong>dorzolamide 2%</strong> TID (topical), acetazolamide (oral; short-term severe cases)</li>
<li><strong>Rho kinase inhibitors</strong> -- <strong>netarsudil</strong>; increase trabecular outflow</li>
<li><strong>Combination drops</strong> improve adherence (latanoprost + timolol, brimonidine + brinzolamide)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Laser:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT)</strong> -- increasingly <strong>first-line</strong> (LiGHT trial showed SLT non-inferior to eye drops with better adherence); repeatable; low risk</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Surgical (advanced or refractory disease):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS)</strong> -- iStent, Kahook Dual Blade, Xen gel stent; often combined with cataract surgery</li>
<li><strong>Trabeculectomy</strong> with mitomycin C or <strong>tube shunt</strong> (Ahmed, Baerveldt) -- for refractory or advanced disease</li>
<li><strong>Sustained-release implants</strong> (bimatoprost implant, Durysta)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Acute angle-closure crisis (EMERGENCY):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>IV acetazolamide</strong> 500 mg, topical pilocarpine 2% (miosis to open angle), topical beta-blocker and alpha-agonist, IV mannitol if IOP very high</li>
<li><strong>Laser peripheral iridotomy</strong> (definitive; creates hole in iris to equalize pressure and open the angle) -- <strong>perform as soon as IOP controlled; in both eyes</strong> (prophylactic iridotomy in the fellow eye)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Chronic angle-closure:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Laser iridotomy, lens extraction (cataract surgery often resolves), goniosynechialysis</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Normal-tension glaucoma (NTG):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Lower IOP by 30% (best evidence); prostaglandin analogs first-line</li>
<li>Treat <strong>vascular risk factors</strong> (nocturnal hypotension, sleep apnea, anemia)</li>
<li>Consider <strong>calcium channel blockers</strong> (limited evidence)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive / supportive:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Treat systemic vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, sleep apnea); avoid excessive nocturnal BP dips</li>
<li><strong>Ginkgo biloba</strong> (improves ocular blood flow; limited RCT evidence), <strong>omega-3</strong>, <strong>vitamin C</strong> (antioxidant, collagen support)</li>
<li>Patient education on adherence -- drops are lifelong</li>
</ul>
