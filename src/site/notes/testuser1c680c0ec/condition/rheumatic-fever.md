<h1>Rheumatic Fever</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Acute rheumatic fever (ARF) is a multisystem inflammatory disorder that arises as a <strong>delayed, immune-mediated sequela of group A streptococcal (GAS) pharyngitis</strong>. It is not a direct bacterial infection of the affected tissues; rather, antibodies and T cells directed against streptococcal antigens cross-react with human tissues—a process termed <strong>molecular mimicry</strong>—producing inflammation in the heart, joints, brain, skin, and subcutaneous tissues. The disease characteristically follows a sore throat by one to five weeks and predominantly affects children aged 5 to 15.</p>
<p>The clinical hallmark and source of long-term morbidity is <strong>carditis</strong>, which can inflame all three layers of the heart but is most consequential when it scars the valves. Repeated or severe episodes lead to <strong>rheumatic heart disease (RHD)</strong>, a chronic condition characterized by mitral and aortic valve damage that can progress over years to heart failure, arrhythmia, and premature death. Other manifestations—migratory polyarthritis, Sydenham chorea, subcutaneous nodules, and the distinctive rash erythema marginatum—are largely self-limiting but are diagnostically important.</p>
<p>ARF and RHD are diseases of poverty and overcrowding and have become rare in high-income countries but remain a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality among children and young adults in low- and middle-income regions. Because the entire disease cascade is triggered by a treatable bacterial infection, prompt treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis (primary prevention) and long-term antibiotic prophylaxis after a first episode (secondary prevention) are the cornerstones of control.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>ARF presents with a combination of major and minor manifestations, classically codified in the <strong>Jones criteria</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Carditis</strong>: a new or changed heart murmur, tachycardia, pericardial rub, or signs of heart failure; may be subclinical and detectable only by echocardiography</li>
<li><strong>Migratory polyarthritis</strong>: painful, swollen large joints with inflammation &quot;migrating&quot; from one joint to another</li>
<li><strong>Sydenham chorea</strong>: involuntary, purposeless movements with muscle weakness and emotional lability, often appearing late</li>
<li><strong>Subcutaneous nodules</strong>: firm, painless nodules over extensor surfaces and tendons</li>
<li><strong>Erythema marginatum</strong>: a transient, non-pruritic, serpiginous pink rash on the trunk and proximal limbs</li>
</ul>

<p>Echocardiography is now central to detecting subclinical carditis, and updated World Heart Federation criteria formalize the echocardiographic definition of rheumatic valvulitis.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The essential prerequisite is <strong>untreated or inadequately treated GAS pharyngitis</strong>. Risk is amplified by young age (5-15 years), crowded living conditions and limited access to health care, and a prior episode of ARF, which markedly increases recurrence risk. Host genetic susceptibility, including certain HLA associations, also contributes.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management has three goals: eradicate the streptococcus, suppress inflammation, and prevent recurrence. <strong>Penicillin</strong> (or a suitable alternative in allergic patients) eradicates residual GAS. <strong>Aspirin or other NSAIDs</strong> treat the arthritis and mild carditis, while <strong>corticosteroids</strong> are used for severe carditis with heart failure. Critically, all patients require <strong>long-term secondary antibiotic prophylaxis</strong> (typically intramuscular benzathine penicillin every 3-4 weeks) for years to prevent recurrent attacks and cumulative valve damage. Heart failure and chorea are managed supportively.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The dominant complication is <strong>rheumatic heart disease</strong>, with chronic mitral and aortic valve damage that can progress to congestive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, thromboembolic stroke, and infective endocarditis on the damaged valves. Recurrent episodes compound the cardiac injury, underscoring the importance of secondary prophylaxis.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>The arthritis, chorea, and skin manifestations resolve without lasting harm, but the prognosis is driven by the presence and severity of carditis. Mild carditis may resolve, whereas severe or recurrent carditis frequently leaves permanent valvular disease. With reliable secondary prophylaxis and, when needed, valve surgery, long-term outcomes improve substantially.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>ARF and RHD have nearly disappeared from high-income countries owing to improved living conditions and prompt antibiotic treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis, but they remain endemic in many low- and middle-income countries and among disadvantaged Indigenous populations. RHD continues to affect tens of millions of people worldwide and is a leading cause of cardiovascular death in young people in these settings.</p>
