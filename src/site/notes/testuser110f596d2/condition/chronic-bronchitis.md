<h1>Chronic Bronchitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Chronic bronchitis is clinically defined as a <strong>productive cough lasting &gt;=3 months per year for at least two consecutive years</strong>, occurring in the absence of other lung diseases. It is one of the two principal phenotypes of <strong>chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)</strong>, alongside emphysema; in practice the two overlap. COPD affects <strong>&gt;200 million people worldwide</strong> and is the <strong>third leading cause of death globally</strong>. The dominant driver is <strong>cigarette smoking</strong> (~80-90% of cases), though indoor biomass-fuel smoke is a major cause in low- and middle-income countries, where women are disproportionately affected.</p>
<p>The pathophysiology begins with chronic exposure to cigarette smoke, which activates neutrophils, macrophages, and CD8+ T cells in the airway wall. Persistent inflammation drives <strong>hypertrophy of submucosal glands</strong> and hyperplasia of <strong>goblet cells</strong>, thickening the airway wall and producing the characteristic <strong>mucus hypersecretion</strong>. Ciliary dysfunction, small-airway fibrosis, and luminal narrowing progressively limit airflow; in advanced disease alveolar destruction (emphysema) reduces elastic recoil, further trapping air and causing hyperinflation. The net result is expiratory airflow limitation -- the spirometric hallmark (<strong>post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC &lt;0.70</strong>) -- and impaired gas exchange (hypoxemia and hypercapnia).</p>
<p>Clinically, chronic bronchitis presents with <strong>chronic productive cough</strong> (worse in the mornings), <strong>dyspnea on exertion</strong>, <strong>wheezing</strong>, and <strong>recurrent exacerbations</strong> -- acute worsenings typically triggered by viral or bacterial infection. Acute exacerbations accelerate the decline in lung function, impair quality of life, and are a leading cause of hospitalization. Diagnosis requires <strong>spirometry</strong> (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC &lt;0.70 confirms persistent airflow limitation). Disease severity is graded by GOLD spirometric grades (GOLD 1-4 based on FEV1 percent predicted).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Reduce exposure:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong> -- the only intervention proven to slow disease progression (5-15% sustained quit rate with varenicline, nicotine replacement, and counseling)</li>
<li>Avoid occupational and indoor air pollution</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pharmacotherapy (GOLD-aligned):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bronchodilators</strong> are the cornerstone of symptom relief -- <strong>LAMA</strong> (tiotropium 18 mcg daily) preferred for moderate disease; <strong>LABA+LAMA</strong> combination (umeclidinium/vilanterol, indacaterol/glycopyrrolate) for greater bronchodilation</li>
<li><strong>Inhaled corticosteroids</strong> (ICS) added for patients with exacerbations and <strong>eosinophil count &gt;=300/uL</strong> (evidence of &quot;T2 inflammation&quot;); ICS increases pneumonia risk, so use selectively</li>
<li><strong>Roflumilast</strong> (PDE-4 inhibitor) -- for severe chronic bronchitis with frequent exacerbations; reduces exacerbation rate</li>
<li><strong>Mucolytics</strong> (N-acetylcysteine 600 mg BID, carbocysteine) -- modest reduction in exacerbations, especially in patients not on ICS</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Non-pharmacologic:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pulmonary rehabilitation</strong> -- exercise training, education, and nutritional support; improves exercise capacity, dyspnea, and quality of life more than any single drug</li>
<li><strong>Long-term oxygen therapy</strong> (LTOT) for chronic hypoxemia (PaO2 &lt;=55 mmHg or O2 sat &lt;=88%); improves survival</li>
<li><strong>Non-invasive ventilation</strong> for chronic hypercapnia in selected patients</li>
<li><strong>Vaccinations</strong> -- annual influenza, pneumococcal (PCV20), COVID-19, RSV</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Acute exacerbation management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Bronchodilators (albuterol + ipratropium nebulized)</li>
<li><strong>Systemic corticosteroids</strong> (prednisone 40 mg x 5 days) -- shorten recovery</li>
<li><strong>Antibiotics</strong> (amoxicillin-clavulanate, doxycycline, azithromycin) for increased sputum purulence or Anthonisen criteria</li>
<li><strong>NIV</strong> for impending respiratory failure</li>
</ul>
