<h1>Omega3 Index</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The Omega-3 Index is defined as the percentage of <strong>EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) + DHA (docosahexaenoic acid)</strong> in red blood cell (RBC) membranes, expressed as a proportion of total RBC fatty acids. It was developed by <strong>William Harris and Clemens von Schacky in 2004</strong> as a standardized, reproducible biomarker of long-term omega-3 fatty acid status. Because red blood cells circulate for approximately 120 days, the Omega-3 Index reflects average omega-3 intake over the preceding <strong>3-4 months</strong> -- substantially more stable and clinically meaningful than plasma/serum omega-3 levels, which fluctuate with recent meals over 2-4 weeks.</p>
<p>The Omega-3 Index has been extensively studied as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease. An index of <strong>&gt;=8% is associated with a 90% reduction in the risk of sudden cardiac death</strong> compared to an index below 4% (Harris &amp; Von Schacky 2004). Beyond cardiovascular protection, higher Omega-3 Index values are associated with reduced all-cause mortality, lower rates of cognitive decline and dementia, improved mood and reduced depression risk, better inflammatory profiles, reduced risk of age-related macular degeneration, and improved pregnancy outcomes (especially preterm birth prevention). The Omega-3 Index is increasingly recognized as a <strong>modifiable risk factor</strong> akin to LDL cholesterol or blood pressure.</p>
<p>Average Omega-3 Index values vary dramatically by population: <strong>Western / US populations</strong> (low fish intake) average ~4-5%, well below the cardioprotective threshold. <strong>Japanese populations</strong> (high fish intake) average 8-12%. <strong>Korean, Mediterranean, and Scandinavian</strong> populations are intermediate. The Omega-3 Index is particularly valuable because the response to omega-3 supplementation is highly individual -- influenced by genetics (FADS1/FADS2 polymorphisms affecting ALA-to-EPA conversion), absorption, metabolism, background diet (especially omega-6:omega-3 ratio), age, sex, and BMI. Without testing, it is impossible to predict who will reach the cardioprotective range with a given dose.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (extensively validated; Harris &amp; Von Schacky foundational work; REDUCE-IT and JELIS trials support cardiovascular benefit).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Ranges apply to both sexes and all adult age groups</li>
<li>Average Omega-3 Index in US/Western populations: ~4-5% (well below cardioprotective threshold)</li>
<li>Japanese populations with high fish consumption average 8-12%</li>
<li>The HS-Omega-3 Index methodology (Harris Scientific) is the reference standard; results vary slightly across labs that use different methodologies</li>
<li>Extremely high values (&gt;16%) are rare and not associated with adverse effects in clinical studies (though theoretical bleeding concern exists)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Omega-3 Index:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fatty fish intake</strong> (salmon, mackerel, sardines, anchovies, herring) 2-3 servings/week raises Index ~2-3 percentage points</li>
<li><strong>Fish oil, krill oil, or algal oil supplementation</strong> (1-4 g/day EPA+DHA) raises Index ~4-5 percentage points over 3-4 months</li>
<li><strong>Icosapent ethyl (Vascepa)</strong> 4 g/day pure EPA -- raises Index dramatically (~3-5x baseline)</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean, Japanese, plant-based whole-food diets</strong> with adequate omega-3</li>
<li><strong>ALA-rich plant sources</strong> (flaxseed, chia, walnut) -- modest conversion ~5-10% to EPA+DHA</li>
<li><strong>Reduced omega-6 intake</strong> (processed seed oils) -- improves omega-6:omega-3 ratio</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Omega-3 Index:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Western diet</strong> (low fish, high processed seed oils)</li>
<li><strong>Vegetarian / vegan diet</strong> without omega-3 supplementation</li>
<li><strong>Alcohol excess</strong></li>
<li><strong>Smoking</strong> (impaired omega-3 metabolism)</li>
<li><strong>Aging</strong> (decreased conversion of ALA to EPA/DHA)</li>
<li><strong>FADS1/FADS2 polymorphisms</strong> (reduced conversion)</li>
<li><strong>Atorvastatin</strong> (modestly lowers via altered lipid metabolism)</li>
<li><strong>Renal failure, dialysis</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low Omega-3 Index (&lt;4%) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sudden cardiac death:</strong> ~90% increased risk vs index &gt;8% (Harris 2004)</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular events:</strong> each SD decrease roughly doubles CV event risk</li>
<li><strong>All-cause mortality:</strong> low index predicts higher all-cause mortality in elderly cohorts</li>
<li><strong>Cognitive decline and dementia:</strong> low index associated with faster decline</li>
<li><strong>Depression:</strong> low index predicts depression risk</li>
<li><strong>Inflammation:</strong> low index correlates with elevated hsCRP and pro-inflammatory eicosanoids</li>
<li><strong>Dry eye syndrome, age-related macular degeneration</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Optimal Omega-3 Index (&gt;=8%) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cardioprotection:</strong> reduced risk of sudden cardiac death, MI, stroke, heart failure</li>
<li><strong>Cognitive preservation:</strong> slower cognitive decline in elderly</li>
<li><strong>Mood stability:</strong> reduced depression risk</li>
<li><strong>Reduced inflammation:</strong> lower hsCRP, IL-6</li>
<li><strong>Better pregnancy outcomes:</strong> reduced preterm birth (especially very preterm)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Standardization across labs still incomplete</strong> -- HS-Omega-3 Index (Harris Scientific) is the reference method</li>
<li><strong>Cost and insurance coverage</strong> often limit access (~$50-150 out of pocket)</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 Index is one component of CV risk</strong> -- interpret with lipid panel, blood pressure, traditional risk factors</li>
<li><strong>REDUCE-IT (icosapent ethyl) trial</strong> raised EPA but the Omega-3 Index was not the primary endpoint; however, the resulting index values are consistent with cardioprotection</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Cardiovascular risk stratification (especially patients with elevated triglycerides, prior ASCVD, or strong family history)</li>
<li>Guide omega-3 supplementation dose (highly individual response)</li>
<li>Monitor dietary intervention response</li>
<li>Pre-pregnancy optimization</li>
<li>Cognitive aging assessment</li>
<li>Anti-inflammatory dietary intervention monitoring</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>Omega-3 Index responds within 3-4 months of dietary or supplemental intervention:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Fatty fish intake</strong> 2-3 servings/week (salmon, mackerel, sardines, anchovies, herring) -- the most natural source</li>
<li><strong>Fish oil, krill oil, or algal oil supplementation</strong> 1-4 g/day EPA+DHA (look for products with verified third-party testing; IFOS certification)</li>
<li><strong>Algal oil</strong> (vegan source) for vegetarians and vegans</li>
<li><strong>Icosapent ethyl (Vascepa)</strong> 4 g/day -- FDA-approved for elevated triglycerides in statin-treated patients with ASCVD or diabetes + risk factors (REDUCE-IT population)</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong> rich in olive oil, fish, vegetables, whole grains</li>
<li><strong>Plant-based ALA sources</strong> (flaxseed, chia, walnut) -- modest conversion ~5-10%</li>
<li><strong>Reduce omega-6 intake</strong> (processed seed oils -- corn, soybean, sunflower, cottonseed) -- improves AA:EPA ratio</li>
<li><strong>Stop smoking</strong> and <strong>reduce alcohol</strong></li>
<li><strong>Re-test Omega-3 Index after 3-4 months</strong> to confirm response; adjust dose as needed (individual response varies widely)</li>
</ol>
