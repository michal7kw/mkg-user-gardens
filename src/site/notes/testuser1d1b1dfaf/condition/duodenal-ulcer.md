<h1>Duodenal Ulcer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>A duodenal ulcer is a mucosal break penetrating the <strong>muscularis mucosae</strong> of the duodenum, most commonly the first part (duodenal bulb). Together with gastric ulcer, it constitutes <strong>peptic ulcer disease (PUD)</strong>. The duodenum is the most common site for peptic ulcers (~60-70% of PUD cases).</p>
<p>The two principal causes are:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Helicobacter pylori infection</strong> (~70-90% of duodenal ulcers): a spiral, urease-producing gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucus layer, causes chronic gastritis, and disrupts the mucosal barrier. H. pylori is a <strong>Class I carcinogen</strong> (gastric cancer and MALT lymphoma).</li>
<li><strong>NSAID use</strong> (~10-20% of cases): nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX-1), reducing protective gastric prostaglandins and mucosal bicarbonate secretion.</li>
</ol>
<p>The classic clinical feature distinguishing duodenal from gastric ulcer is the <strong>temporal relationship of pain to meals</strong>: duodenal ulcer pain is <strong>relieved by eating</strong> and recurs 2-3 hours later (including nocturnal pain), whereas gastric ulcer pain is <strong>worsened by eating</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Complications</strong> include upper GI bleeding (most common; melena, hematemesis), perforation (surgical emergency presenting as sudden severe pain and free air under diaphragm), gastric outlet obstruction (from edema/scarring), and penetration into adjacent organs (pancreas).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>H. pylori eradication (first-line for H. pylori-positive ulcers):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Quadruple therapy:</strong> PPI BID + bismuth subsalicylate + metronidazole + tetracycline x 14 days</li>
<li><strong>Triple therapy:</strong> PPI BID + clarithromycin + amoxicillin (or metronidazole if penicillin-allergic) x 14 days</li>
<li><strong>Confirm eradication</strong> 4+ weeks after completion (urea breath test or stool antigen)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Acid suppression (for healing and NSAID-induced ulcers):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PPI</strong> (omeprazole 40 mg, esomeprazole 40 mg, pantoprazole 40 mg) daily x 4-8 weeks</li>
<li>Continue PPI until endoscopic confirmation of healing (for complicated ulcers)</li>
<li><strong>H2 blockers</strong> (famotidine 40 mg) -- second-line</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>NSAID management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Discontinue NSAIDs</strong> if possible</li>
<li>If NSAIDs must continue: co-prescribe PPI; switch to COX-2 selective (celecoxib) if low CV risk</li>
<li><strong>Test for H. pylori</strong> before starting long-term NSAIDs (eradicate if positive)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Complicated ulcer (bleeding):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Endoscopic hemostasis:</strong> epinephrine injection + thermal or mechanical therapy (clips)</li>
<li><strong>High-dose PPI infusion:</strong> pantoprazole 80 mg bolus then 8 mg/hr x 72 hours</li>
<li><strong>Transfusion</strong> for hemodynamic instability</li>
<li><strong>Surgery</strong> for refractory bleeding or perforation</li>
</ul>
