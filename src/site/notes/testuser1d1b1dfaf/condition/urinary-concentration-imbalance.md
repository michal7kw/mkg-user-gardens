<h1>Urinary Concentration Imbalance</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Urinary concentration imbalance refers to the kidney's inability to appropriately regulate the <strong>osmolality</strong> of urine -- producing urine that is either <strong>inappropriately dilute</strong> (low specific gravity, &lt;1.005) or <strong>inappropriately concentrated</strong> (high specific gravity, &gt;1.025) relative to the body's water and osmolar status. This ability depends on the integrated function of the <strong>hypothalamus (ADH synthesis)</strong>, the <strong>posterior pituitary (ADH release)</strong>, and the <strong>renal collecting duct (ADH response via V2 receptors and aquaporin-2 channels)</strong>. Dysfunction at any level produces one of three clinical syndromes: <strong>central diabetes insipidus, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, or SIADH</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Diabetes insipidus (DI)</strong> is defined by <strong>excretion of large volumes of dilute urine</strong> despite dehydration or elevated serum osmolality, reflecting either <strong>deficient ADH</strong> (central DI) or <strong>renal resistance to ADH</strong> (nephrogenic DI). <strong>Central DI</strong> results from damage to the hypothalamus or posterior pituitary -- <strong>pituitary surgery</strong> (transsphenoidal), <strong>traumatic brain injury</strong>, <strong>pituitary or hypothalamic tumors</strong>, <strong>Sheehan syndrome</strong> (postpartum necrosis), <strong>histiocytosis</strong>, <strong>tuberculosis</strong>, or <strong>idiopathic</strong> (often autoimmune). <strong>Nephrogenic DI</strong> most commonly results from <strong>lithium therapy</strong> (~50% of patients on long-term lithium have impaired concentrating ability), <strong>hypercalcemia</strong>, <strong>hypokalemia</strong>, <strong>CKD</strong>, and <strong>genetic</strong> causes (AVPR2 X-linked, AQP2 autosomal recessive). Patients present with <strong>polyuria</strong> (3-20 L/day), <strong>polydipsia</strong>, <strong>nocturia</strong>, and -- if thirst mechanism intact -- maintain near-normal serum sodium through driven water intake; if thirst impaired (elderly, hypothalamic lesions), <strong>hypernatremia</strong> and <strong>hyperosmolar coma</strong> develop.</p>
<p><strong>SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone)</strong> is the opposite extreme -- <strong>excessive ADH action</strong> produces <strong>concentrated urine despite low serum osmolality</strong>, causing <strong>dilutional hyponatremia</strong>. Causes include <strong>malignancy</strong> (small cell lung cancer with ectopic ADH, pancreatic, lymphoma), <strong>pulmonary disease</strong> (pneumonia, mechanical ventilation), <strong>CNS disease</strong> (stroke, meningitis, trauma), and <strong>medications</strong> (SSRIs, carbamazepine, vincristine, MDMA). Patients present with <strong>euvolemic hyponatremia</strong> (no edema, no dehydration), and symptoms reflect cerebral edema from hypoosmolality: headache, nausea, confusion, seizures, coma. <strong>Chronic kidney disease</strong> impairs concentrating ability at multiple levels, producing <strong>isosthenuria</strong> (fixed urine osmolality near plasma, ~300 mOsm/kg) and predisposing to both nocturia and volume overload.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Diagnosis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Simultaneous serum and urine osmolality and sodium</strong> -- the cornerstone</li>
<li><strong>Central or nephrogenic DI</strong>: high serum sodium and osmolality (&gt;295 mOsm/kg) with inappropriately dilute urine (&lt;300 mOsm/kg)</li>
<li><strong>SIADH</strong>: low serum sodium (&lt;135 mEq/L) and osmolality (&lt;275 mOsm/kg) with inappropriately concentrated urine (&gt;100 mOsm/kg, often &gt;300) and urine sodium &gt;40 mEq/L</li>
<li><strong>Water deprivation test</strong> (for DI) -- withhold water; central DI fails to concentrate urine, then responds to desmopressin; nephrogenic DI fails to respond</li>
<li><strong>Hypertonic saline infusion test</strong> for equivocal cases</li>
<li><strong>Copeptin</strong> (stable ADH precursor surrogate) -- emerging as superior diagnostic marker</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Central diabetes insipidus:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Desmopressin (DDAVP)</strong> -- synthetic ADH analog; routes: intranasal 10-40 mcg daily, oral 0.05-1.2 mg BID, sublingual 60-120 mcg BID, or IV/SC for acute</li>
<li><strong>Titrate to control polyuria and thirst, avoid hyponatremia</strong></li>
<li>Address the underlying cause (pituitary tumor, etc.)</li>
<li>Patients must have <strong>access to water</strong> -- desmopressin without water intake causes water intoxication and hyponatremia</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Nephrogenic DI:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Discontinue offending drug</strong> (lithium if possible; demeclocycline)</li>
<li><strong>Correct hypercalcemia and hypokalemia</strong></li>
<li><strong>Low-sodium diet and thiazide diuretics</strong> -- paradoxical; mild volume depletion triggers proximal sodium and water reabsorption, reducing distal delivery and polyuria</li>
<li><strong>Indomethacin or ibuprofen</strong> (NSAIDs) -- adjunct; inhibit prostaglandins that antagonize ADH</li>
<li><strong>Amiloride</strong> for lithium-induced DI -- blocks lithium entry into collecting duct cells</li>
<li>Genetic forms -- manage with low-solute diet and adequate hydration</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>SIADH:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fluid restriction</strong> (800-1000 mL/day) -- first-line; slow but effective</li>
<li><strong>Salt tablets and high-protein diet</strong> -- increases solute load and water excretion</li>
<li><strong>Loop diuretics</strong> if fluid restriction insufficient</li>
<li><strong>Vasopressin V2 receptor antagonists</strong> (aquaretics) -- <strong>tolvaptan 15-60 mg/day</strong>, <strong>conivaptan IV</strong>, <strong>satavaptan</strong>; produce solute-free water diuresis. Caution: rapid sodium correction risk</li>
<li><strong>Urea</strong> (15-60 g/day) -- osmotic diuresis, less expensive alternative to vaptans</li>
<li><strong>Hypertonic (3%) saline</strong> for severe symptomatic hyponatremia (seizures, coma) -- emergency; correct 4-6 mEq/L in 4-6 hours, then stop; <strong>avoid over-rapid correction</strong> (risk of osmotic demyelination)</li>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying cause</strong> (resect tumor, treat pneumonia, discontinue drug)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Chronic kidney disease-related concentration impairment:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Manage <strong>fluid balance</strong> carefully -- patients cannot concentrate or dilute urine effectively</li>
<li><strong>Nocturia</strong> is common; address by timing diuretics and fluid intake</li>
<li>Avoid extremes of fluid intake</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>General principles:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Correct sodium gradually</strong> -- not more than 8-10 mEq/L in 24 hours (or 18 in 48 hours) to avoid <strong>osmotic demyelination syndrome</strong> (central pontine myelinolysis)</li>
<li><strong>Monitor</strong> serum sodium, urine output and osmolality, weight, fluid balance</li>
<li>Patient education on <strong>water intake, recognizing dehydration</strong>, and medication adherence</li>
</ul>
