<h1>Kynurenine Pathway</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The kynurenine pathway (KP) is the principal catabolic route for the essential amino acid tryptophan, consuming roughly 95% of the tryptophan not committed to protein synthesis. It serves two physiologically distinct purposes that make it a unique metabolic crossroads:</p>
<ol>
<li>It is the <strong>de novo source of <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/metabolite/nad/" class="internal-link">NAD+</a></strong>, synthesizing the cofactor from tryptophan through quinolinic acid.</li>
<li>It generates a family of <strong>neuroactive metabolites</strong> with opposing actions — the neurotoxic, excitotoxic <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/metabolite/quinolinic-acid/" class="internal-link">Quinolinic Acid</a> and the neuroprotective <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/metabolite/kynurenic-acid/" class="internal-link">Kynurenic Acid</a>.</li>
</ol>
<p>Because the rate-limiting enzymes are induced by inflammation (IDO1 by IFN-gamma and pro-inflammatory cytokines) and stress hormones (TDO2 by cortisol), the KP is the molecular conduit through which systemic immune activation and metabolic state are translated into altered brain chemistry. This positions it at the intersection of longevity (NAD+), metabolic health, and mental health.</p>
<h2>Key Steps</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Tryptophan → N-formylkynurenine → Kynurenine</strong>, catalyzed by TDO2 (liver, cortisol-induced) or IDO1/IDO2 (extrahepatic, cytokine-induced). This is the rate-limiting and most heavily regulated step.</li>
<li><strong>Branch point at <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/metabolite/kynurenine/" class="internal-link">Kynurenine</a>:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Neurotoxic/NAD+ branch:</strong> KMO → 3-hydroxykynurenine → KYNU → 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid → (3-HAO) → <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/metabolite/quinolinic-acid/" class="internal-link">Quinolinic Acid</a> → (QPRT) → NAD+. Microglia preferentially express KMO, favoring this branch under neuroinflammation.</li>
<li><strong>Neuroprotective branch:</strong> kynurenine aminotransferases (KAT I-IV), expressed in astrocytes, divert kynurenine to <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/metabolite/kynurenic-acid/" class="internal-link">Kynurenic Acid</a>.</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ol>
<h2>Regulation</h2>
<p>The balance between branches is determined by the cellular distribution and induction of KMO (neurotoxic) versus KATs (neuroprotective). Inflammation shifts flux toward quinolinic acid; this &quot;neurotoxic shift&quot; is observed in depression, neurodegeneration, and infection. The first step also competes with <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/pathway/serotonin-synthesis/" class="internal-link">Serotonin Synthesis</a> for the shared tryptophan substrate, so KP activation can lower serotonin availability.</p>
<h2>Physiological Role</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>NAD+ biosynthesis:</strong> supplies the de novo arm that complements the salvage pathway (see <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/pathway/de-novo-nad-synthesis-pathway/" class="internal-link">De Novo NAD+ Synthesis Pathway</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Glutamatergic modulation:</strong> quinolinic acid is an NMDA-receptor agonist (excitotoxic); kynurenic acid is an NMDA and alpha7-nicotinic antagonist (neuroprotective, but cognition-impairing when excessive).</li>
<li><strong>Immune tolerance:</strong> tryptophan depletion and kynurenine signaling through the aryl hydrocarbon receptor modulate T-cell responses.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Relevance</h2>
<p>KP dysregulation is implicated in <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/condition/depression/" class="internal-link">Depression</a> and <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/condition/treatment-resistant-depression/" class="internal-link">Treatment-Resistant Depression</a> (inflammation-driven neurotoxic shift), <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/condition/alzheimers-disease/" class="internal-link">Alzheimer's Disease</a> and <a href="/garden/itestc7a960c6/condition/parkinsons-disease/" class="internal-link">Parkinson's Disease</a> (quinolinic acid accumulation, NAD+ insufficiency), and schizophrenia (elevated kynurenic acid). KMO and IDO inhibitors are under investigation as neuropsychiatric therapeutics.</p>
