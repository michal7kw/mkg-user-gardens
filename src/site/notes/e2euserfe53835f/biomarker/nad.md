<h1>NAD+</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is a dinucleotide coenzyme found in every living cell and one of the most central small molecules in metabolism. It exists in two interconverting forms: the oxidized NAD+ and the reduced NADH. By accepting and donating a pair of electrons, the NAD+/NADH couple links the catabolism of carbohydrates, fats and amino acids (glycolysis, the citric acid cycle and fatty-acid oxidation) to the electron transport chain, where the energy stored in NADH is used to generate ATP via oxidative phosphorylation. A closely related phosphorylated pair, NADP+/NADPH, supports reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defense, but the bare &quot;NAD+&quot; measured as a biomarker refers chiefly to the unphosphorylated redox pool.</p>
<p>Beyond its classical redox role, NAD+ is consumed as a substrate by three families of signaling enzymes: the sirtuins (NAD+-dependent deacylases such as SIRT1 and SIRT3 that regulate metabolism, stress resistance and chromatin state), the poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs, which use NAD+ for DNA-damage repair), and the cyclic ADP-ribose synthases (CD38 and CD157, which generate calcium-mobilizing second messengers). Because these enzymes cleave NAD+ rather than recycle it, cells must continuously regenerate NAD+ through the de novo pathway from tryptophan, the Preiss-Handler pathway from nicotinic acid, and—most importantly in mammals—the salvage pathway that recycles nicotinamide via NAMPT. This dual identity as both an electron carrier and a consumable signaling substrate is what couples cellular energy status to gene expression, genome maintenance and circadian timing.</p>
<p>Tissue and circulating NAD+ levels decline substantially with age, in part through increased NAD+ consumption by CD38 and chronic PARP activation, and through reduced salvage capacity. This decline is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired DNA repair and metabolic disease, which has driven intense interest in NAD+ as both a biomarker of cellular health and a therapeutic target. NAD+ is not part of routine clinical chemistry; it is measured in research settings (whole blood, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, or tissue biopsy) typically by enzymatic cycling assays, HPLC or LC-MS/MS, and is most often used to confirm that NAD+ precursor supplements (NMN, nicotinamide riboside, niacin) are raising the intracellular pool.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>There is <strong>no universally standardized clinical reference range</strong> for NAD+. Absolute values depend heavily on the sample matrix (whole blood, PBMCs, plasma, tissue), collection and quenching method, and assay (enzymatic cycling vs. LC-MS/MS).</li>
<li>NAD+ is metabolically labile; rapid sample quenching is required because NAD+ is consumed within seconds of cell lysis. Poor pre-analytical handling is a major source of variability.</li>
<li>Most actionable data come from <strong>within-subject changes</strong> (before vs. after an intervention) rather than from comparison to a population range.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase NAD+:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>NAD+ precursor supplementation — NMN, nicotinamide riboside (NR), niacin/nicotinic acid, nicotinamide (provide salvage and Preiss-Handler substrate)</li>
<li>Caloric restriction and fasting (upregulate NAMPT and the salvage pathway)</li>
<li>Exercise and increased energy demand (raise NAD+ turnover and biosynthesis)</li>
<li>Healthy circadian rhythm (NAMPT and NAD+ oscillate on a daily cycle)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease NAD+:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Aging (reduced salvage capacity, increased CD38 expression)</li>
<li>Chronic DNA damage and inflammation (excess PARP and CD38 NAD+ consumption)</li>
<li>High-fat diet, obesity and metabolic syndrome</li>
<li>Excess alcohol and oxidative stress</li>
<li>Mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic disease states</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Aging and cellular senescence</strong> — declining NAD+ limits sirtuin activity and mitochondrial quality control, a recurring theme in geroscience.</li>
<li><strong>Mitochondrial dysfunction</strong> — low NAD+/NADH impairs oxidative phosphorylation and ATP output, contributing to fatigue and metabolic disease.</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 Diabetes and metabolic dysfunction</strong> — impaired NAD+ metabolism is linked to insulin resistance and disordered fuel handling.</li>
<li><strong>Heart Failure and cardiovascular disease</strong> — the failing heart shows a shifted NAD+/NADH redox state, and NAD+ repletion is under active investigation.</li>
<li><strong>Neurodegeneration</strong> — reduced NAD+ and DNA-repair capacity are implicated in age-related neuronal decline.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Confirming that an NAD+ precursor regimen (NMN, NR, niacin) is actually raising the intracellular NAD+ pool.</li>
<li>Tracking the NAD+ metabolome (NAD+, NADH, NMN, NR, nicotinamide, methylated metabolites) in research and longevity studies.</li>
<li>Investigating mitochondrial and metabolic disease where NAD+ redox balance is altered.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Interpretation caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>A single absolute NAD+ value is rarely interpretable; trends and the full NAD+ metabolome are more informative.</li>
<li>Plasma NAD+ may not reflect the intracellular pool that matters most for sirtuin and PARP activity.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>NAD+ precursor supplementation</strong> — Nicotinamide riboside (typically 250-1000 mg/day) and NMN (250-1000 mg/day) reliably raise the blood NAD+ metabolome in controlled trials; niacin/nicotinic acid is an older, low-cost precursor (flushing is a common side effect).</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction and time-restricted eating</strong> — Activate NAMPT and the salvage pathway, supporting endogenous NAD+ synthesis.</li>
<li><strong>Regular exercise</strong> — Endurance and resistance training increase NAD+ biosynthetic enzymes and turnover.</li>
<li><strong>CD38 modulation</strong> — Polyphenols such as apigenin and quercetin inhibit CD38, the major age-related NAD+-consuming enzyme (preclinical/emerging evidence).</li>
<li><strong>Protect against excess NAD+ drain</strong> — Limit alcohol, manage chronic inflammation, and avoid sustained oxidative/DNA-damage stress that over-activates PARPs.</li>
<li><strong>Support mitochondrial health</strong> — Adequate B-vitamin status (NAD+ precursors derive from niacin equivalents and tryptophan) and a nutrient-dense diet sustain the biosynthetic pathways.</li>
</ol>
