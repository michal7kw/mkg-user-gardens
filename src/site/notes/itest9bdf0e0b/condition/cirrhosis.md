<h1>Cirrhosis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cirrhosis is the common end stage of most chronic liver diseases. Persistent hepatocellular injury and inflammation activate hepatic stellate cells, which deposit excess collagen and extracellular matrix. Over years, this fibrosis bridges between portal tracts and central veins and, together with nodular hepatocyte regeneration, distorts the normal lobular architecture. The structural derangement has two major consequences: loss of functioning liver mass (impairing synthesis of albumin and clotting factors, bilirubin clearance, and detoxification) and increased resistance to blood flow through the liver, producing portal hypertension.</p>
<p>The principal causes worldwide are chronic hepatitis B and C, alcohol-related liver disease, and metabolic dysfunction-associated (non-alcoholic) fatty liver disease; less common causes include hemochromatosis, autoimmune hepatitis, and primary biliary cholangitis. Early (compensated) cirrhosis may be asymptomatic, whereas decompensation is marked by ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, and jaundice.</p>
<h2>Diagnosis and Monitoring</h2>
<p>Cirrhosis is increasingly diagnosed non-invasively by elastography (liver stiffness) combined with serum markers, reserving biopsy for unclear cases. Laboratory abnormalities reflect both injury and failing synthetic function: aminotransferases (ALT, AST) and GGT may be elevated, while a falling albumin, rising bilirubin, and prolonged prothrombin time (raised INR) indicate decompensation. Severity and prognosis are graded by the Child-Pugh and MELD scores. Surveillance for hepatocellular carcinoma and esophageal varices is standard.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management centers on treating the underlying cause (antiviral therapy, alcohol cessation, weight loss for metabolic disease), preventing and treating complications (diuretics for ascites, beta-blockers and band ligation for varices, lactulose/rifaximin for encephalopathy), and, in advanced disease, liver transplantation. Fibrosis is potentially reversible in early stages if the driving injury is removed.</p>
