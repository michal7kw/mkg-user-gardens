<h1>Testosterone Total</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Total testosterone quantifies the entire pool of testosterone in the bloodstream, encompassing the fraction bound tightly to <strong>sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG; ~60%, biologically inactive)</strong>, the fraction loosely bound to <strong>albumin (~38%, bioavailable)</strong>, and the small <strong>biologically active free fraction (~1-2%)</strong>. As the primary androgen, testosterone drives the development and maintenance of male reproductive tissues, secondary sexual characteristics, muscle mass, bone density, erythropoiesis, libido, and mood, and is present at lower but physiologically important levels in women.</p>
<p>Total testosterone is the <strong>recommended first-line test for evaluating androgen status</strong> (Endocrine Society). In men it is used to screen for <strong>hypogonadism</strong> (low testosterone with symptoms: low libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue, reduced muscle mass, depressed mood, osteoporosis). In women it helps assess <strong>hyperandrogenism</strong> (PCOS, androgen-secreting tumors) and rule out androgen-secreting tumors. Because testosterone follows a <strong>diurnal rhythm</strong> that peaks in the morning, samples should be drawn in a fasting state in the early AM, and any abnormal result should be <strong>confirmed on a repeat morning sample</strong> before a diagnosis is made.</p>
<p>A key interpretive limitation is that total testosterone reflects both bound and free hormone, so it can mislead when <strong>SHBG is abnormal</strong>. A high SHBG (aging, hyperthyroidism, estrogen therapy, liver disease) can produce a &quot;normal&quot; total despite a functionally low free testosterone; a low SHBG (insulin resistance, obesity, hypothyroidism) can do the reverse. For this reason, total testosterone is often interpreted together with SHBG and, when needed, free or calculated free testosterone.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (Endocrine Society guideline-endorsed; foundational androgen biomarker).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals are sex-specific, age-dependent, and vary by assay (LC-MS/MS preferred over immunoassay at low concentrations) and laboratory</li>
<li><strong>Morning (before 10 AM) fasting sample</strong> required for accurate assessment</li>
<li><strong>Confirm abnormal results</strong> on a repeat morning sample (significant inter-day variability)</li>
<li>African American populations have ~10-15% higher total testosterone</li>
<li>Aging: total T declines by ~1-2%/year after age 30</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase total testosterone:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Men:</strong> anabolic-androgenic steroid use (exogenous testosterone, nandrolone, oxandrolone); testicular tumors (Leydig cell); adrenal tumors; congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency); hCG therapy; clomiphene citrate (selective estrogen receptor modulator)</li>
<li><strong>Women:</strong> PCOS (the most common cause); congenital adrenal hyperplasia; androgen-secreting tumors (ovarian, adrenal); stromal hyperthecosis</li>
<li><strong>Both sexes:</strong> high-dose DHEA supplementation; hyperthyroidism (raises SHBG and total T but lowers free T)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease total testosterone:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary hypogonadism</strong> -- Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), cryptorchidism, orchitis (mumps), chemotherapy/radiation, anabolic steroid withdrawal</li>
<li><strong>Secondary / central hypogonadism</strong> -- pituitary tumors, Kallmann syndrome, hemochromatosis, hyperprolactinemia, opioids, glucocorticoids</li>
<li><strong>Late-onset hypogonadism (LOH)</strong> -- age-related decline (~1-2%/year after age 30)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic illness</strong> -- CKD, HIV, COPD, cirrhosis, type 2 diabetes, obesity, OSA</li>
<li><strong>Obesity</strong> (reduces SHBG; lowers total T but may not lower free T)</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome</strong> (associated with low T)</li>
<li><strong>Malnutrition, severe weight loss</strong></li>
<li><strong>Anorexia nervosa</strong> (hypothalamic suppression)</li>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency</strong></li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> opioids (chronic), glucocorticoids, ketoconazole, spironolactone, cimetidine, marijuana (chronic)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated total testosterone is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Men:</strong> anabolic steroid use (most common); testicular tumors; adrenal tumors; CAH</li>
<li><strong>Women:</strong> PCOS (the most common cause); CAH; androgen-secreting tumors (ovarian, adrenal); hyperthecosis</li>
<li><strong>Symptoms:</strong> hirsutism, acne, virilization (voice deepening, clitoromegaly in women; gynecomastia, low LH/FSH in men from exogenous steroids)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced total testosterone is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Men: hypogonadism symptoms:</strong> low libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue, reduced muscle mass, depressed mood, cognitive impairment, osteoporosis, anemia</li>
<li><strong>Women:</strong> reduced libido, fatigue, bone loss</li>
<li><strong>Causes:</strong> primary (Klinefelter, orchitis), secondary (pituitary tumor, Kallmann), late-onset (aging), chronic illness, opioids, glucocorticoids, obesity</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Always interpret with SHBG and free testosterone</strong> (especially when SHBG is abnormal)</li>
<li><strong>Diurnal variation</strong> requires morning fasting sample</li>
<li><strong>Confirm abnormal results</strong> on repeat sample (significant variability)</li>
<li><strong>LC-MS/MS preferred</strong> at low concentrations (women, children, postmenopausal)</li>
<li><strong>African American populations have higher total T</strong> (population differences)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Hypogonadism workup (with SHBG, LH, FSH, prolactin, free T)</li>
<li>PCOS evaluation (with SHBG, LH, FSH, DHEA-S, free T)</li>
<li>TRT monitoring (target mid-normal range; q3-6 months after dose change)</li>
<li>Anti-androgen therapy monitoring (prostate cancer; target castrate-range)</li>
<li>Functional medicine: androgen deficiency symptoms despite &quot;normal&quot; total</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>For men with low total testosterone attributable to lifestyle factors:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> (5-10% body weight if obese) -- the most impactful intervention; raises total and free T</li>
<li><strong>Resistance training</strong> 2-3x/week -- increases testosterone production</li>
<li><strong>Adequate sleep</strong> (7-9 h/night) -- sleep deprivation lowers testosterone 10-15%</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> 2000-5000 IU D3 daily (in deficient patients) -- vitamin D deficiency linked to low T</li>
<li><strong>Zinc</strong> 15-30 mg daily (in deficient patients) -- zinc is essential for testosterone synthesis</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium</strong> 200-400 mg daily (in deficient patients) -- supports metabolic health and T</li>
<li><strong>Boron</strong> 3-10 mg daily -- small studies suggest modest T increase via SHBG reduction</li>
<li><strong>Tongkat Ali (Eurycoma longifolia)</strong> 200-400 mg daily -- small RCTs show T improvement in stressed men</li>
<li><strong>Ashwagandha</strong> 300-600 mg daily (KSM-66) -- reduces cortisol; supports T in chronically stressed men</li>
<li><strong>Fenugreek</strong> 500-600 mg daily -- small studies suggest modest effect</li>
<li><strong>Reduce alcohol</strong> (chronic excess lowers T)</li>
<li><strong>Stop smoking</strong> (chronic smoking lowers T)</li>
<li><strong>Address comorbidities:</strong> type 2 diabetes, OSA, depression, chronic illness</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> T-lowering medications (opioids, glucocorticoids) when possible</li>
<li><strong>Confirmed pathological hypogonadism</strong> -- refer for TRT (testosterone cypionate, gel, patch, pellet); monitor q3-6 months</li>
</ol>
