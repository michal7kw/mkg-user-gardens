<h1>Total T3</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Total T3 quantifies the entire circulating pool of triiodothyronine, the most metabolically active thyroid hormone. It encompasses both the small free fraction (~0.3%) and the large fraction bound to carrier proteins -- thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG; ~70%), albumin (~25%), and transthyretin (~5%). Although the thyroid gland secretes mostly T4, roughly <strong>80% of circulating T3 is produced peripherally</strong> by deiodination of T4 in the liver, kidney, and skeletal muscle via type 1 and type 2 deiodinase enzymes (selenium-containing).</p>
<p>T3 binds nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (TR-alpha, TR-beta) with high affinity to regulate basal metabolic rate, thermogenesis, cardiac output, gut motility, protein turnover, and neurodevelopment. T3 is the principal driver of thyroid hormone action at the tissue level, despite being present at much lower concentrations than T4.</p>
<p>Elevated Total T3 most often indicates hyperthyroidism (Graves disease, toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma), including &quot;T3-predominant thyrotoxicosis&quot; where T3 is disproportionately elevated relative to T4. It can also arise from excessive thyroid hormone supplementation (liothyronine, desiccated thyroid) or iodine-induced hyperthyroidism. Low Total T3 may reflect hypothyroidism, but it is <strong>very commonly reduced in euthyroid sick syndrome (non-thyroidal illness)</strong>, malnutrition, and chronic disease, where T4-to-T3 conversion is suppressed. Because &gt;99% of T3 is protein-bound, total T3 is sensitive to changes in binding-protein concentration -- pregnancy and estrogen therapy raise TBG (and thus total T3) without affecting the active free hormone.</p>
<p>Total T3 is interpreted in conjunction with Free T3, Free T4, and TSH. It is particularly valuable in confirming and characterizing hyperthyroidism and in monitoring response to antithyroid therapy (methimazole, PTU).</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (foundational thyroid biomarker; ATA/ETA guideline-endorsed for specific indications).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Total T3 reflects binding-protein status (TBG, albumin, transthyretin)</li>
<li>Pregnancy: total T3 rises with TBG; FT3 falls slightly</li>
<li>Estrogen therapy raises total T3 (TBG elevation)</li>
<li>Significant inter-assay variability</li>
<li><strong>Free T3 preferred</strong> for most clinical indications (more reflective of active hormone)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Total T3:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong> (Graves disease, toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma)</li>
<li><strong>T3 thyrotoxicosis</strong> (elevated T3 with normal T4; can be earliest sign of recurrent Graves)</li>
<li><strong>Excessive thyroid hormone supplementation</strong> (liothyronine, desiccated thyroid)</li>
<li><strong>Iodine-induced hyperthyroidism</strong> (Jod-Basedow)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy and estrogen therapy</strong> (TBG elevation; total T3 rises but FT3 unchanged)</li>
<li><strong>Adequate selenium and iodine status</strong> (support T4-to-T3 conversion)</li>
<li><strong>Thyroiditis</strong> (transient elevation from gland destruction)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Total T3:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong> (primary or central)</li>
<li><strong>Euthyroid sick syndrome / non-thyroidal illness</strong> (the most common cause of low Total T3 in hospitalized patients)</li>
<li><strong>Malnutrition, severe caloric restriction, anorexia nervosa</strong> (adaptive response)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic illness</strong> (CKD, cirrhosis, malignancy)</li>
<li><strong>Selenium deficiency</strong> (impairs deiodinase function)</li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> amiodarone, high-dose propranolol, corticosteroids, propylthiouracil, lithium</li>
<li><strong>Conditions lowering binding proteins:</strong> nephrotic syndrome, androgens</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated Total T3 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hyperthyroidism</strong> (Graves disease, toxic multinodular goiter): elevated Total T3 with suppressed TSH and elevated Free T4</li>
<li><strong>T3 thyrotoxicosis:</strong> elevated T3 with normal T4 and suppressed TSH -- can be earliest sign of recurrent Graves disease</li>
<li><strong>Thyroid storm:</strong> markedly elevated T3/T4 with severe clinical features</li>
<li><strong>Exogenous thyroid hormone</strong> (factitious hyperthyroidism)</li>
<li><strong>Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (type 1)</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Low Total T3 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong> (advanced disease; T3 is preferentially preserved until late)</li>
<li><strong>Non-thyroidal illness (euthyroid sick syndrome):</strong> the most common context in hospitalized patients; self-resolves with recovery</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction, malnutrition</strong></li>
<li><strong>Chronic illness</strong> (CKD, cirrhosis, malignancy)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Total T3 reflects binding-protein status</strong> -- FT3 preferred for most clinical indications</li>
<li><strong>Not for routine screening</strong> (TSH and FT4 are preferred)</li>
<li><strong>Confounded by non-thyroidal illness</strong> in hospitalized patients</li>
<li><strong>T3 is preferentially preserved in early hypothyroidism</strong> -- a poor screening test for hypothyroidism</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Hyperthyroidism diagnosis and severity assessment (with TSH, FT4, FT3)</li>
<li>T3 thyrotoxicosis detection</li>
<li>Antithyroid therapy monitoring (methimazole, PTU)</li>
<li>Thyroid storm assessment</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>Total T3 reflects thyroid hormone action; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For elevated Total T3 (hyperthyroidism):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Graves disease:</strong> methimazole (preferred over PTU except in 1st trimester pregnancy); radioactive iodine ablation; thyroidectomy</li>
<li><strong>Toxic multinodular goiter / adenoma:</strong> radioactive iodine or surgery</li>
<li><strong>Beta-blocker</strong> (propranolol) for symptomatic control</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> exogenous thyroid hormone</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For low Total T3 (hypothyroidism or non-thyroidal illness):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism:</strong> levothyroxine (T4) replacement; dose-adjusted to normalize TSH</li>
<li><strong>Combination T4/T3 therapy:</strong> controversial; some clinicians add liothyronine (T3)</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying illness</strong> (euthyroid sick syndrome resolves with recovery)</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional support</strong> for caloric restriction / anorexia</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Selenium</strong> 200 mcg daily in deficient patients -- cofactor for deiodinase; supports T4-to-T3 conversion</li>
<li><strong>Adequate iodine intake</strong> (150 mcg/day; 250 mcg/day in pregnancy)</li>
<li><strong>Adaptogenic support</strong> (Ashwagandha) for chronic stress-related thyroid dysfunction (research-stage)</li>
<li><strong>Reduce goitrogens</strong> (raw cruciferous vegetables, soy) in susceptible individuals</li>
<li><strong>Avoid excessive iodine</strong> (kelp, contrast agents) in autoimmune thyroid disease</li>
</ol>
