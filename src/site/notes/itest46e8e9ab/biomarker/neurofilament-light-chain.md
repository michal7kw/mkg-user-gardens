<h1>Neurofilament Light Chain</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Neurofilament light chain (NfL) is the smallest of the three neurofilament subunits (light, medium, and heavy) that together form the intermediate-filament scaffold of the neuronal cytoskeleton. Neurofilaments are highly abundant in large myelinated axons, where they provide structural support and regulate axonal caliber and conduction velocity. When axons are injured—whether through neurodegeneration, inflammation, demyelination, ischemia, or trauma—neurofilament proteins are released into the interstitial fluid and reach the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and, in smaller quantities, the peripheral blood. Because release reflects the integrity of the axon itself rather than any single disease mechanism, NfL is regarded as a sensitive but disease-nonspecific marker of neuroaxonal damage.</p>
<p>For decades NfL could only be measured reliably in CSF because blood concentrations are roughly 40-100 times lower. The advent of ultrasensitive single-molecule array (Simoa) immunoassays and other high-sensitivity platforms transformed NfL into a practical blood biomarker, enabling minimally invasive, repeatable sampling. Blood NfL correlates strongly with CSF NfL, which has driven rapid adoption across research and, increasingly, clinical practice. Concentrations rise physiologically with age and are influenced by body mass index and renal function, so age- and BMI-adjusted reference models (expressed as percentiles or Z-scores) are now preferred over fixed thresholds.</p>
<p>Clinically, NfL is valued for three complementary roles: detection of subclinical or ongoing neuroaxonal injury, longitudinal monitoring of disease activity and treatment response, and prognostication of future decline. It is elevated in multiple sclerosis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal dementia, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, traumatic brain injury, stroke, and many other conditions. Because it lacks disease specificity, NfL is most powerful when interpreted alongside the clinical picture and disease-specific markers (e.g., phosphorylated tau and amyloid-beta ratios in Alzheimer's disease).</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Values are assay- and platform-dependent (Simoa, Ella, Elecsys); there is no single universally standardized cutoff, and results should not be compared across platforms without harmonization.</li>
<li>NfL increases substantially with age and is inversely associated with body mass index; age- and BMI-adjusted percentile/Z-score reference databases (e.g., Benkert et al.) outperform fixed thresholds for individual interpretation.</li>
<li>Reduced renal clearance (lower eGFR) raises blood NfL independently of brain pathology and must be considered.</li>
<li>CSF NfL concentrations are roughly 40-100x higher than blood and are reported in separate, method-specific ranges.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Neurofilament Light Chain:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Active neurodegeneration (ALS, Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal dementia, Huntington's disease)</li>
<li>Demyelinating disease activity and relapses (multiple sclerosis)</li>
<li>Acute brain injury (traumatic brain injury, stroke, hypoxic-ischemic injury)</li>
<li>Advancing age (physiological axonal turnover)</li>
<li>Reduced renal clearance / chronic kidney disease (lower eGFR raises blood NfL)</li>
<li>Peripheral nerve injury and certain peripheral neuropathies</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Neurofilament Light Chain:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Resolution of an acute injury or relapse over weeks to months</li>
<li>Effective disease-modifying therapy (e.g., high-efficacy MS treatments lower serum NfL)</li>
<li>Lower body mass relative to blood volume effects (higher BMI is associated with lower measured NfL)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Multiple Sclerosis</strong> — reflects ongoing neuroaxonal damage; predicts relapses, new MRI lesions, brain atrophy, and long-term disability, and declines with effective treatment.</li>
<li><strong>Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis</strong> — markedly elevated; supports diagnosis, correlates with progression rate, and is prognostic for survival.</li>
<li><strong>Alzheimer's Disease</strong> — rises years before symptom onset in familial forms and tracks neurodegeneration and clinical decline; complements amyloid and tau markers.</li>
<li><strong>Frontotemporal Dementia</strong> — among the highest NfL elevations in dementia; aids differentiation and prognostication.</li>
<li><strong>Parkinson's Disease and atypical parkinsonism</strong> — helps distinguish Parkinson's disease from atypical syndromes (which show higher NfL).</li>
<li><strong>Huntington's Disease</strong> — rises before and tracks with clinical onset and progression.</li>
<li><strong>Traumatic Brain Injury and Stroke</strong> — acute, often large elevations proportional to injury severity and outcome.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Recovery from acute neuroaxonal injury and successful suppression of disease activity by therapy.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Tracking disease activity and treatment response in multiple sclerosis.</li>
<li>Prognostication and progression monitoring in ALS and other neurodegenerative diseases.</li>
<li>Detecting subclinical neuroaxonal injury in at-risk or presymptomatic individuals.</li>
<li>Drug-development endpoint as a pharmacodynamic biomarker of neuroprotection.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>NfL reflects the extent of underlying neuroaxonal injury rather than a directly modifiable lifestyle variable, so levels fall primarily when the driving pathology is controlled. Practical strategies center on treating the underlying disease and protecting brain health:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying disease</strong> — initiating high-efficacy disease-modifying therapy in multiple sclerosis reliably lowers serum NfL; appropriate management of any neurological condition is the principal lever.</li>
<li><strong>Optimize vascular and metabolic risk factors</strong> — controlling hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia reduces ongoing vascular axonal injury that contributes to elevated NfL.</li>
<li><strong>Avoid neurotrauma</strong> — protective measures against head injury (helmets, fall prevention) limit acute spikes in NfL.</li>
<li><strong>Support renal function</strong> — because reduced clearance raises blood NfL, maintaining kidney health aids accurate interpretation and avoids spurious elevation.</li>
<li><strong>Interpret with an age/BMI model</strong> — apply percentile/Z-score reference data rather than fixed cutoffs to avoid over- or under-calling abnormal values.</li>
</ol>
