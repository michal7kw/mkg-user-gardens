<h1>Vitamin B12</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a cobalt-containing, water-soluble vitamin that serves as an essential cofactor for two enzymes in human metabolism: methionine synthase, which converts homocysteine to methionine and regenerates the active folate pool, and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, which channels propionate-derived substrates into the citric acid cycle. Because the first reaction links cobalamin to one-carbon (methylation) metabolism and the second to fatty-acid and amino-acid catabolism, deficiency simultaneously impairs DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing cells and the maintenance of myelin in the central and peripheral nervous systems. The serum B12 test measures total circulating cobalamin, most of which is bound to the transport proteins haptocorrin and transcobalamin.</p>
<p>Dietary B12 is obtained almost exclusively from animal-derived foods (meat, fish, eggs, dairy); plants do not synthesize it, which is why strict vegans and vegetarians are at elevated risk. Absorption is a multi-step process requiring gastric acid and pepsin to release B12 from food, gastric intrinsic factor to bind it, and specific receptors in the terminal ileum to internalize the intrinsic-factor complex. Disruption at any step — atrophic gastritis, autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor (pernicious anemia), ileal disease or resection, or drugs such as metformin and proton-pump inhibitors — produces deficiency despite adequate intake. Hepatic stores are large, so deficiency from reduced intake develops over years.</p>
<p>Clinically, low B12 is a common and treatable cause of macrocytic (megaloblastic) anemia and of neuropsychiatric syndromes ranging from peripheral neuropathy and subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord to cognitive impairment and depression. Total serum B12 in the low-normal range (200-350 pg/mL) can mask true tissue deficiency, so the metabolic markers homocysteine and methylmalonic acid (both elevated early in functional deficiency) are used to confirm or exclude deficiency in equivocal cases.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals vary by assay platform; conversion to SI units: 1 pg/mL ≈ 0.738 pmol/L.</li>
<li>Total serum B12 has limited sensitivity in the low-normal range. When clinical suspicion is high but B12 is 200-400 pg/mL, measure methylmalonic acid and homocysteine, which rise before total B12 falls.</li>
<li>High-dose biotin supplementation can interfere with some immunoassays and produce spuriously normal or elevated results.</li>
<li>Pregnancy and oral contraceptives can lower total B12 without true deficiency.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Vitamin B12:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Oral or injectable B12 supplementation (cyanocobalamin, methylcobalamin)</li>
<li>Liver disease (release of stored cobalamin and haptocorrin)</li>
<li>Myeloproliferative disorders (elevated haptocorrin)</li>
<li>Renal insufficiency (reduced clearance)</li>
<li>High-dose biotin (assay interference, not a true increase)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Vitamin B12:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Strict vegan or vegetarian diet (minimal dietary intake)</li>
<li>Pernicious anemia (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor)</li>
<li>Atrophic gastritis and reduced gastric acid (impaired release from food)</li>
<li>Metformin use (dose- and duration-dependent malabsorption)</li>
<li>Proton-pump inhibitors and H2 blockers (reduced acid-dependent absorption)</li>
<li>Ileal disease, resection, or bariatric surgery (loss of absorption site)</li>
<li>Pancreatic insufficiency and small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Megaloblastic / macrocytic anemia</strong> — impaired DNA synthesis enlarges red cell precursors, raising mean corpuscular volume and lowering hemoglobin.</li>
<li><strong>Subacute combined degeneration and peripheral neuropathy</strong> — demyelination of the dorsal and lateral spinal columns and peripheral nerves causes paresthesia, gait ataxia, and sensory loss, which may become irreversible if untreated.</li>
<li><strong>Cognitive impairment and depression</strong> — B12 deficiency contributes to elevated homocysteine and is linked to accelerated brain atrophy and neuropsychiatric symptoms in older adults.</li>
<li><strong>Elevated homocysteine and methylmalonic acid</strong> — the earliest and most sensitive biochemical signatures of functional cobalamin deficiency.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Liver disease, myeloproliferative disorders, and renal failure</strong> — high total B12 can be a paradoxical marker of serious underlying disease rather than good nutritional status.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Screening vegans/vegetarians, older adults, and patients on long-term metformin or acid-suppressing drugs.</li>
<li>Investigating unexplained macrocytosis, anemia, neuropathy, or cognitive decline.</li>
<li>Confirming response to repletion therapy (rising B12, falling homocysteine and methylmalonic acid).</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying cause first</strong> — confirm whether deficiency is dietary or due to malabsorption (e.g., pernicious anemia), as the latter requires lifelong therapy.</li>
<li><strong>Oral cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin</strong> — high-dose oral B12 (1,000-2,000 mcg/day) is effective even in many malabsorption cases via passive diffusion.</li>
<li><strong>Intramuscular B12</strong> — preferred for severe deficiency, neurological involvement, or proven malabsorption; typical loading then maintenance dosing.</li>
<li><strong>Dietary sources</strong> — increase intake of meat, fish, eggs, and dairy; vegans should use fortified foods or routine supplementation.</li>
<li><strong>Co-correct folate</strong> — replete folate alongside B12 when both are low, but never give folate alone in B12 deficiency, as it can mask anemia while neurological damage progresses.</li>
<li><strong>Review medications</strong> — reassess long-term metformin and proton-pump inhibitor use and monitor B12 in chronic users.</li>
</ol>
