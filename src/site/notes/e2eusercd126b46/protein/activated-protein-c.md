<h1>Activated Protein C</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Activated protein C (APC) is the active form of protein C, a vitamin K-dependent zymogen synthesized in the liver. Its activation occurs on the surface of intact endothelium: thrombin, captured by the membrane receptor thrombomodulin, loses its procoagulant activity and instead cleaves protein C — a reaction greatly accelerated when protein C is presented by the endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR). The result is a built-in, surface-localized brake on coagulation.</p>
<p>Once generated, APC proteolytically degrades the activated cofactors factor Va and factor VIIIa, sharply reducing further thrombin generation. This anticoagulant function is amplified by its cofactor protein S. Beyond hemostasis, APC signaling through EPCR and protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR-1) produces cytoprotective effects: it stabilizes endothelial tight junctions, reduces vascular permeability, and suppresses inflammatory and apoptotic signaling.</p>
<h2>Structure and Function</h2>
<p>APC is a two-chain serine protease derived from protein C by cleavage of an activation peptide. It requires vitamin K-dependent gamma-carboxylation of its Gla domain for calcium-mediated membrane binding. The catalytic serine protease domain confers its proteolytic activity against factors Va and VIIIa. The dual anticoagulant and cytoprotective roles are partly separable, which has motivated engineered APC variants that retain endothelial protection while minimizing bleeding risk.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>The protein C anticoagulant pathway is initiated when thrombin binds thrombomodulin on the endothelium. This complex activates protein C, with EPCR enhancing the rate. APC, together with protein S on a phospholipid surface, cleaves and inactivates factors Va and VIIIa, the rate-limiting cofactors of the prothrombinase and tenase complexes. By removing these cofactors, APC down-regulates thrombin production in a feedback loop. Concurrently, EPCR-bound APC cleaves PAR-1 to trigger barrier-protective and anti-inflammatory intracellular signaling.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Deficiency of protein C or resistance to APC (most commonly the factor V Leiden mutation) causes hereditary thrombophilia with increased venous thromboembolism risk. The protein C system is also pivotal in <strong>Sepsis</strong>, where consumption and impaired activation of protein C contribute to disseminated intravascular coagulation, microvascular thrombosis, and <strong>Inflammation</strong>. Recombinant human activated protein C (drotrecogin alfa) was studied as a sepsis therapy and initially licensed before being withdrawn after later trials failed to confirm a mortality benefit; protein C levels nonetheless remain of interest as a marker and potential supplementation target in sepsis.</p>
