<h1>Lecithin Cholesterol Acyltransferase Deficiency</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Lecithin cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT) deficiency is a rare <strong>autosomal recessive disorder of reverse cholesterol transport</strong> caused by mutations in the <strong>LCAT gene</strong> on chromosome 16. LCAT is the plasma enzyme that catalyzes the transfer of a fatty acid from lecithin (phosphatidylcholine) to free cholesterol, forming <strong>cholesteryl ester</strong> on <strong>HDL particles</strong> -- the central step of <strong>reverse cholesterol transport</strong>, by which peripheral cholesterol is returned to the liver for excretion. Without LCAT, free cholesterol accumulates in plasma and tissues, particularly in the <strong>cornea, kidney glomeruli, and red blood cell membranes</strong>, producing the clinical triad of <strong>corneal opacities, hemolytic anemia, and progressive renal failure</strong>.</p>
<p>Two clinical phenotypes exist. <strong>Classic familial LCAT deficiency</strong> (complete enzyme deficiency) is the more severe form, presenting in childhood with <strong>corneal opacities</strong> (diffuse, dot-like, &quot;fish-eye&quot; appearance that does not usually impair vision significantly), <strong>normochromic normocytic anemia</strong> (from cholesterol accumulation in RBC membranes causing target cells and hemolysis), and <strong>proteinuria</strong> progressing to <strong>renal failure</strong> typically by the 4th-5th decade. <strong>Fish-eye disease</strong> (also called partial LCAT deficiency or alpha-LCAT deficiency) is a milder variant in which LCAT activity on HDL (alpha-LCAT activity) is selectively deficient while activity on LDL (beta-LCAT) is preserved; patients develop <strong>corneal opacities</strong> but <strong>typically lack the anemia and renal disease</strong>. The biochemical hallmark of both is <strong>markedly low HDL cholesterol</strong> (&lt;10-15 mg/dL) with low apoA-I.</p>
<p>Diagnosis is suggested by <strong>very low HDL cholesterol</strong> on routine lipid panel, confirmed by <strong>LCAT enzyme activity assay</strong> (in plasma) and <strong>LCAT gene sequencing</strong>. The differential diagnosis includes other inherited low-HDL syndromes (<strong>Tangier disease / ABCA1 deficiency</strong>, <strong>apoA-I deficiencies</strong>, <strong>CETP deficiency</strong>), which can be distinguished by their clinical and biochemical features. Of note, despite the profoundly low HDL (the &quot;good cholesterol&quot;), <strong>patients with LCAT deficiency do not have the markedly accelerated atherosclerosis one might predict</strong> -- a paradox that challenged the simple &quot;HDL protects against atherosclerosis&quot; hypothesis and clarified that <strong>HDL function</strong> (not just HDL cholesterol mass) is what matters for atheroprotection.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>There is <strong>no specific disease-modifying therapy</strong> for LCAT deficiency. Management is supportive, focused on <strong>slowing renal disease</strong>, <strong>managing anemia</strong>, and <strong>addressing corneal involvement</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Renal disease management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ACE inhibitor or ARB</strong> -- first-line for proteinuria and hypertension; renoprotective</li>
<li><strong>Strict blood pressure control</strong> (&lt;130/80 mmHg)</li>
<li><strong>Avoid nephrotoxic drugs</strong> (NSAIDs, contrast media)</li>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Renal replacement therapy</strong> -- dialysis or renal transplantation for end-stage renal disease; <strong>disease does not recur in the transplanted kidney</strong> (the enzyme defect is systemic, but the donor kidney has normal glomeruli; however, systemic deposition continues and may affect other organs)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Anemia management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron supplementation</strong> if iron deficiency present</li>
<li><strong>Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs)</strong> for anemia of CKD</li>
<li><strong>Blood transfusion</strong> for severe symptomatic anemia (consider iron chelation if chronically transfused)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Corneal management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Corneal transplant</strong> (penetrating keratoplasty) for severe vision impairment -- but opacities may recur as systemic deposition continues</li>
<li><strong>Lubricating eye drops</strong> for symptomatic relief</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Lipid management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Statins</strong> have limited effect on HDL (typically &lt;5-10% increase) and are not clearly beneficial in LCAT deficiency</li>
<li><strong>No established role for CETP inhibitors, niacin, or fibrates</strong></li>
<li><strong>Plasma infusion</strong> (a source of LCAT) has shown transient biochemical improvement in case reports but is not standard therapy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Investigational and future therapies:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Recombinant LCAT enzyme replacement</strong> -- ACP-501 (rhLCAT) has shown early promise in preclinical studies and phase 1; not yet approved</li>
<li><strong>Gene therapy</strong> -- AAV-mediated LCAT gene transfer; preclinical</li>
<li><strong>ApoA-I Milano / reconstituted HDL infusions</strong> -- conceptually appealing but unproven</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Genetic counseling:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Autosomal recessive inheritance; siblings of affected individuals have 25% risk</li>
<li><strong>Prenatal diagnosis</strong> (CVS or amniocentesis with LCAT gene sequencing) is possible for at-risk couples</li>
<li><strong>Preimplantation genetic testing</strong> available for families with known mutations</li>
<li><strong>Cascade screening</strong> of family members (low-cost serum lipid panel + LCAT activity in suspected carriers)</li>
</ul>
