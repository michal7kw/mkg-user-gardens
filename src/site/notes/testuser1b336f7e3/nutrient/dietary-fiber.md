<h1>Dietary Fiber</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Dietary fiber consists of indigestible plant carbohydrates and lignin that resist enzymatic digestion in the human small intestine. It is broadly classified into soluble fiber (which dissolves in water to form viscous gels) and insoluble fiber (which adds bulk to stool and promotes intestinal motility). Both types are essential for optimal gastrointestinal health and metabolic regulation.</p>
<p>Soluble fiber, found in oats, beans, and psyllium, lowers LDL cholesterol by binding bile acids, slows glucose absorption to improve glycemic control, and serves as a fermentable substrate for beneficial gut bacteria producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as butyrate. Insoluble fiber, found in wheat bran, vegetables, and whole grains, promotes regular bowel movements and prevents constipation.</p>
<p>The recommended daily intake is 25-30 grams for adults, yet average consumption in Western diets is only 15 grams. Adequate fiber intake is consistently associated with reduced risk of colorectal cancer, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and all-cause mortality. Fiber intake should be increased gradually and accompanied by adequate hydration to prevent gastrointestinal discomfort.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Promotes regular bowel movements and prevents constipation</li>
<li>Lowers LDL cholesterol by binding bile acids in the intestine</li>
<li>Slows carbohydrate absorption, improving postprandial glycemic control</li>
<li>Feeds beneficial gut microbiota, producing health-promoting SCFAs</li>
<li>Enhances satiety and supports weight management</li>
<li>Reduces colorectal cancer risk through stool bulking and reduced transit time</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Whole grains (oats, barley, whole wheat)</li>
<li>Legumes (lentils, chickpeas, black beans)</li>
<li>Vegetables (broccoli, Brussels sprouts, artichokes)</li>
<li>Fruits (raspberries, pears, apples with skin)</li>
<li>Nuts and seeds (chia seeds, flaxseed, almonds)</li>
<li>Psyllium husk</li>
<li>Sweet potatoes</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Low fiber intake is associated with constipation, diverticular disease, elevated LDL cholesterol, blood sugar dysregulation, and increased risk of colorectal cancer. Populations consuming high-fiber diets consistently show lower rates of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes.</p>
<p>Excessive fiber intake, particularly when increased rapidly, can cause bloating, gas, abdominal cramping, and diarrhea. Very high fiber consumption may also impair mineral absorption (calcium, iron, zinc) by binding these minerals with phytates, though this is typically only significant in the context of otherwise poor mineral intake.</p>
