<h1>IGF-1</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1), historically called Somatomedin C, is a 70-amino-acid peptide hormone structurally homologous to proinsulin. Measured in serum, it functions as a biomarker rather than only a hormone: roughly 75% of circulating IGF-1 is synthesized by hepatocytes in response to pituitary Growth Hormone (GH), so a single fasting or non-fasting blood draw provides a stable, integrated readout of GH activity over the preceding hours to days. This is clinically valuable because GH itself is secreted in pulses and has a half-life of minutes, making direct GH measurement unreliable; IGF-1, by contrast, circulates almost entirely bound to IGF-binding proteins (principally IGFBP-3) in a ternary complex with the acid-labile subunit, giving it a half-life of 12-16 hours and a flat diurnal profile.</p>
<p>Physiologically, IGF-1 mediates most of the anabolic and growth-promoting actions of GH by binding the IGF-1 receptor (a receptor tyrosine kinase) and activating the PI3K-Akt and Ras-MAPK cascades, which converge on mTORC1 to drive protein synthesis, cell proliferation, and suppression of autophagy. IGF-1 also provides negative feedback to the hypothalamus and pituitary, restraining further GH release. Serum levels are strongly age-dependent — peaking during puberty, then declining throughout adult life (the &quot;somatopause&quot;) — and are sensitive to nutritional status: protein-energy intake and insulin permit hepatic GH-receptor signaling, so malnutrition, fasting, and caloric restriction lower IGF-1 even when GH is high.</p>
<p>Clinically, serum IGF-1 is the first-line screening test for both GH deficiency and GH excess. Elevated, age-adjusted values raise suspicion of acromegaly or gigantism and are confirmed with an oral glucose tolerance test; low values prompt evaluation for hypopituitarism, severe systemic illness, hepatic failure, or malnutrition. Beyond the pituitary axis, IGF-1 has become a focus of aging and oncology research: reduced GH/IGF-1 signaling extends lifespan across model organisms, while higher circulating IGF-1 in humans is epidemiologically associated with increased risk of several common cancers — embodying the central growth-versus-longevity trade-off.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>IGF-1 is <strong>strongly age- and sex-dependent</strong>; raw values are uninterpretable without an age-stratified reference. Best practice is to report a standard deviation score (SDS / Z-score) against the assay's own population.</li>
<li>Reference intervals are <strong>assay-specific</strong> and not fully harmonized between platforms; the IGFBP-3 ternary complex and heterophilic antibodies can interfere. Serial monitoring should use the same laboratory and method.</li>
<li>Pregnancy, puberty stage (Tanner stage), and estrogen status shift the distribution and should be factored into interpretation.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase IGF-1:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Growth hormone excess — pituitary somatotroph adenoma (acromegaly/gigantism), the dominant pathological cause</li>
<li>Exogenous GH or recombinant IGF-1 administration (mecasermin); performance-enhancing GH misuse</li>
<li>Adequate-to-high protein and energy intake (permits hepatic GH-receptor signaling)</li>
<li>Puberty and pregnancy (physiologic surges)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease IGF-1:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Aging (progressive somatopause from the third decade onward)</li>
<li>GH deficiency / hypopituitarism and GH-receptor resistance (Laron syndrome)</li>
<li>Caloric restriction, fasting, malnutrition, and anorexia (nutrient-sensing downregulation)</li>
<li>Hepatic failure (loss of synthetic capacity), poorly controlled diabetes, hypothyroidism, and chronic systemic illness</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acromegaly / Gigantism</strong> — GH-secreting pituitary tumors raise IGF-1; age-adjusted IGF-1 is the recommended screening and treatment-monitoring marker, and chronic excess drives cardiovascular, metabolic, and arthropathic complications.</li>
<li><strong>Cancer risk</strong> — Mendelian-randomization and epidemiological studies link higher circulating IGF-1 to increased risk of several cancers (notably prostate, breast, and colorectal), consistent with its pro-proliferative, anti-apoptotic mTOR signaling.</li>
<li><strong>Anabolic / well-fed state</strong> — higher IGF-1 reflects favorable protein-energy status and is leveraged in nutrition and athletic-performance research.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>GH deficiency and hypopituitarism</strong> — low age-adjusted IGF-1 is the first screening step before dynamic GH testing.</li>
<li><strong>Malnutrition, anorexia, and hepatic failure</strong> — IGF-1 falls as a marker of catabolic or synthetic-failure states.</li>
<li><strong>Sarcopenia and frailty</strong> — low IGF-1 in older adults tracks with reduced muscle mass and impaired anabolic response; it is an intervention target in sarcopenia research.</li>
<li><strong>Longevity signal</strong> — paradoxically, genetically or pharmacologically reduced GH/IGF-1 signaling extends lifespan in model organisms and is enriched in some human centenarian cohorts.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>First-line screening for both GH excess (acromegaly) and GH deficiency</li>
<li>Titrating GH-replacement or somatostatin-analog therapy to an age-appropriate target</li>
<li>Tracking nutritional rehabilitation and anabolic response in cachexia, sarcopenia, and critical illness</li>
<li>Research surrogate for caloric restriction and longevity-intervention studies</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>For most clinical contexts the goal is to bring IGF-1 into the <strong>age-appropriate range</strong>, not simply to raise it — higher is not universally better given the cancer trade-off.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Optimize protein-energy nutrition</strong> — adequate total energy and high-quality protein restore hepatic GH-receptor signaling; correcting undernutrition is the most reliable way to raise a pathologically low IGF-1.</li>
<li><strong>Resistance and aerobic exercise</strong> — structured training, especially combined with adequate protein intake, improves the IGF-1 response and downstream muscle anabolism in sarcopenia (network meta-analyses favor nutrition plus exercise).</li>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying endocrine cause</strong> — GH replacement for documented deficiency (raises IGF-1) or somatostatin analogs / surgery for acromegaly (lowers IGF-1 toward target).</li>
<li><strong>Correct hepatic and metabolic dysfunction</strong> — managing diabetes, hypothyroidism, and liver disease normalizes synthetic capacity.</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction / intermittent fasting (to deliberately lower IGF-1)</strong> — for longevity-oriented goals, sustained caloric restriction lowers the insulin-IGF-1 nutrient-sensing axis; note that weight regain reverses these benefits (CALERIE-2 data).</li>
<li><strong>Avoid unsupervised exogenous GH/IGF-1</strong> — performance use raises IGF-1 with meaningful cardiovascular and oncologic risk and is not a legitimate optimization strategy.</li>
</ol>
