<h1>Anti-dsDNA Antibody</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies are highly specific IgG autoantibodies directed against native (double-stranded) DNA. They are a hallmark of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and constitute one of the most clinically useful biomarkers in all of rheumatology. Anti-dsDNA positivity is part of the ACR/EULAR 2019 Classification Criteria for SLE (entry criterion: ANA positive ≥1:80; weighted additive criteria including anti-dsDNA as an &quot;SLE-specific antibody&quot;). The 2019 criteria assign anti-dsDNA a weighted score of 6 points (out of 10 required for classification), reflecting its diagnostic weight.</p>
<p>Clinically, anti-dsDNA is used for two principal purposes: (1) <strong>diagnosis</strong> -- in a patient with positive ANA and clinical suspicion of SLE, anti-dsDNA positivity substantially raises the probability of SLE (specificity &gt;95%); (2) <strong>disease activity monitoring</strong> -- rising titers often precede clinical flares by weeks to months, particularly lupus nephritis. High-titer anti-dsDNA (&gt;200 IU/mL) correlates strongly with active proliferative lupus nephritis (Class III/IV), and serial measurements alongside complement C3/C4 (which fall during flares) provide the most informative serological assessment of SLE activity.</p>
<p>Three main assay types are used clinically, each with different sensitivity/specificity trade-offs: (1) <strong>ELISA</strong> -- most common, highest sensitivity (~60-80%), variable specificity (90-95%) depending on manufacturer; (2) <strong>Farr radioimmunoassay</strong> -- measures high-avidity anti-dsDNA binding, considered gold standard for specificity, but requires radioactive reagents; (3) <strong>Crithidia luciliae indirect immunofluorescence test (CLIFT)</strong> -- highest specificity (~98%) but lower sensitivity (~50%), useful as a confirmatory test. Discordance between methods is common, and clinicians should use the same assay serially when monitoring.</p>
<p>Anti-dsDNA antibodies contribute directly to SLE pathogenesis through immune complex formation. Circulating anti-dsDNA-DNA immune complexes deposit in glomeruli, activate complement, and drive lupus nephritis. They also deposit in skin, synovium, and the choroid plexus, contributing to malar rash, arthritis, and neuropsychiatric lupus respectively.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational SLE biomarker; ACR/EULAR classification criteria component; universal clinical use).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range is assay-specific (ELISA varies by manufacturer; Farr and CLIFT have different units/cutoffs)</li>
<li>A positive result in a patient with clinical SLE features is highly informative; a positive result in isolation without ANA or clinical features is rarely diagnostic</li>
<li>Titers fluctuate with disease activity; serial changes are more useful than single values</li>
<li>20-40% of SLE patients are persistently anti-dsDNA negative -- a negative result does not exclude SLE</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase anti-dsDNA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>SLE flare</strong> -- rising titers often precede clinical flares by weeks to months (especially lupus nephritis)</li>
<li><strong>Lupus nephritis</strong> (Class III/IV proliferative) -- typically high titers &gt;200 IU/mL</li>
<li><strong>Active SLE in any organ</strong> -- cutaneous, musculoskeletal, hematologic, neurologic</li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced lupus</strong> (anti-TNF agents, hydralazine, procainamide, minocycline) -- usually low titers, mostly anti-histone rather than anti-dsDNA</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease anti-dsDNA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Treatment response</strong> -- B-cell depletion (rituximab, obinutuzumab), belimumab (anti-BAFF), immunosuppression (mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide) lower titers as disease activity improves</li>
<li><strong>Disease remission</strong> -- quiescent disease often has undetectable or low anti-dsDNA</li>
<li><strong>Long-standing SLE</strong> -- some patients &quot;burn out&quot; and become serologically negative</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Confounding factors:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Different assay methods yield different absolute values (don't compare across methods)</li>
<li>Assay lots and manufacturers have batch variability</li>
<li>Hemolysis or lipemia can interfere with ELISA</li>
<li>Pregnancy (anti-dsDNA may transiently fall; complement rises physiologically -- use pregnancy-specific interpretation)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Positive anti-dsDNA is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>SLE diagnosis:</strong> ACR/EULAR 2019 weighted criteria component (6 points); specificity 90-95% in patients with positive ANA and clinical SLE features</li>
<li><strong>Lupus nephritis:</strong> high-titer anti-dsDNA (&gt;200 IU/mL) correlates with active proliferative lupus nephritis (Class III/IV); kidney biopsy typically shows immune complex deposition</li>
<li><strong>SLE disease activity:</strong> serial rising titers predict clinical flares by weeks to months; falling titers suggest treatment response</li>
<li><strong>Cutaneous SLE:</strong> malar rash, discoid lesions (lower titer associations than nephritis)</li>
<li><strong>Hematologic SLE:</strong> autoimmune hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia (moderate titer associations)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sensitivity ~60-80%</strong> -- 20-40% of SLE patients are persistently anti-dsDNA negative; does not exclude SLE</li>
<li><strong>False positives</strong> in: autoimmune hepatitis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Sjögren's syndrome, infectious mononucleosis, chronic active hepatitis (usually low titers)</li>
<li><strong>Assay variability</strong> across methods and manufacturers</li>
<li><strong>Not a screening test</strong> -- anti-dsDNA should be ordered only in patients with clinical suspicion of SLE (or with positive ANA + SLE features)</li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced lupus</strong> typically has anti-histone antibodies, not anti-dsDNA (anti-dsDNA positivity in DIL is rare and suggests true SLE)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>SLE diagnosis (alongside ANA, anti-Smith antibody, complement C3/C4)</li>
<li>Disease activity monitoring every 3-6 months in active SLE</li>
<li>Lupus nephritis activity monitoring (q1-3 months)</li>
<li>Treatment response assessment (rituximab, belimumab, mycophenolate, voclosporin)</li>
<li>Pre-pregnancy counseling and pregnancy monitoring (titers help assess flare risk)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>Anti-dsDNA titers reflect active SLE disease state and are managed through SLE-specific therapies:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Hydroxychloroquine 200-400 mg daily (5 mg/kg)</strong> -- the anchor of SLE therapy for ALL patients; reduces flares, improves survival; anti-dsDNA titers may decline gradually</li>
<li><strong>Belimumab (Benlysta)</strong> 10 mg/kg IV monthly (or 200 mg SC weekly) -- anti-BAFF monoclonal antibody; FDA-approved for active SLE; reduces anti-dsDNA titers and improves complement</li>
<li><strong>Anifrolumab (Saphnelo)</strong> 300 mg IV monthly -- anti-IFN-alpha receptor monoclonal antibody; FDA-approved for active SLE; reduces flares and anti-dsDNA titers</li>
<li><strong>Rituximab</strong> 1 g IV x 2 doses (2 weeks apart) -- anti-CD20 B-cell depletion; off-label for refractory SLE; profound anti-dsDNA reduction</li>
<li><strong>Obinutuzumab</strong> 1000 mg IV x 2 -- newer anti-CD20; FDA-approved for lupus nephritis 2024</li>
<li><strong>Voclosporin</strong> 23.7 mg BID -- calcineurin inhibitor; FDA-approved for lupus nephritis 2021; rapid proteinuria reduction</li>
<li><strong>Mycophenolate mofetil</strong> 2-3 g/day -- first-line for lupus nephritis (with corticosteroids); reduces disease activity and anti-dsDNA</li>
<li><strong>Cyclophosphamide</strong> -- IV pulse for severe lupus nephritis or neurologic SLE; reserved for refractory disease</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroids</strong> -- acute flare management; minimize chronic use due to side effects</li>
<li><strong>Sun protection</strong> -- UV exposure triggers SLE flares including anti-dsDNA elevation</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy planning</strong> -- quiescent disease (anti-dsDNA stable, complement normal) for 6+ months before conception; hydroxychloroquine continued throughout pregnancy</li>
</ol>
