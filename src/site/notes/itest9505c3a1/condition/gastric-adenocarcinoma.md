<h1>Gastric Adenocarcinoma</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Gastric adenocarcinoma is the dominant histologic form of stomach cancer, accounting for the large majority of gastric malignancies. It originates in the glandular epithelium of the gastric mucosa and is most commonly classified by the Lauren system into an <strong>intestinal type</strong>, which forms gland-like structures and tends to arise in older patients via a defined precancerous sequence, and a <strong>diffuse type</strong>, characterized by poorly cohesive (often signet-ring) cells that infiltrate the wall, present at a younger age, and carry a worse prognosis. A diffuse tumor infiltrating the entire stomach produces the rigid &quot;leather-bottle&quot; appearance known as linitis plastica.</p>
<p>The intestinal type is the prototypical example of inflammation-driven carcinogenesis. Chronic <strong>Helicobacter pylori</strong> infection initiates a cascade — chronic gastritis, to atrophic gastritis, to intestinal metaplasia, to dysplasia, and finally to invasive carcinoma — over the course of decades. Molecular profiling (TCGA) has further refined the disease into subtypes including Epstein-Barr virus-positive, microsatellite-unstable, genomically stable (enriched for diffuse, CDH1-altered tumors), and chromosomally unstable cancers, each with distinct therapeutic implications.</p>
<p>Clinically, gastric adenocarcinoma is challenging because early disease is usually silent. Symptoms such as weight loss, persistent dyspepsia, and anemia tend to appear only once the tumor is locally advanced, so outside of countries with organized screening programs many cases are diagnosed late. Outcomes therefore hinge heavily on stage at diagnosis, the feasibility of complete resection, and the availability of targeted and immune therapies for biomarker-defined subgroups.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Presentation is frequently insidious. Early tumors may cause only vague dyspepsia or be entirely asymptomatic. As disease advances, patients develop epigastric pain, anorexia, early satiety, and unintentional weight loss. Proximal and gastroesophageal-junction tumors may cause dysphagia, while distal tumors can produce gastric outlet obstruction with vomiting. Chronic occult blood loss commonly manifests as iron-deficiency anemia, and overt bleeding (hematemesis or melena) may occur. Advanced disease can present with a palpable mass, ascites, or characteristic metastatic nodes (e.g., left supraclavicular Virchow node).</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is established by upper endoscopy with biopsy of suspicious lesions, confirmed histologically as adenocarcinoma and classified by Lauren/WHO criteria. Staging combines contrast CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with endoscopic ultrasound for local T and N assessment; staging laparoscopy is often added to detect occult peritoneal disease. Predictive biomarker testing — HER2 amplification, PD-L1 combined positive score, and mismatch-repair/MSI status — is now standard and directs the use of targeted and immunotherapeutic agents.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The leading modifiable risk factor is chronic H. pylori infection, and its eradication reduces incidence. Additional contributors include atrophic gastritis with intestinal metaplasia, diets high in salt and smoked or preserved foods, tobacco use, male sex, and older age. A minority of cases are hereditary, most notably hereditary diffuse gastric cancer caused by germline CDH1 mutations, which warrants genetic counseling and consideration of prophylactic gastrectomy.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is stage-driven and multidisciplinary. Selected early cancers confined to the mucosa can be cured by endoscopic resection (EMR/ESD). Resectable locally advanced disease is treated with gastrectomy and D2 lymphadenectomy, usually combined with perioperative chemotherapy such as the FLOT regimen. For advanced or metastatic disease, systemic therapy is individualized: HER2-targeted trastuzumab for HER2-positive tumors, immune checkpoint inhibitors for PD-L1-high or MSI-high tumors, and palliative chemotherapy with supportive care otherwise.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Local complications include gastric outlet or proximal obstruction, bleeding, and perforation. Progressive disease produces malnutrition and cachexia. Metastatic spread occurs to regional nodes, the liver, lungs, bone, and the peritoneum, where carcinomatosis can cause malignant ascites and bowel obstruction.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis is dominated by stage at diagnosis. Early, node-negative cancers — particularly those amenable to endoscopic or surgical cure — have favorable long-term survival, whereas advanced and metastatic disease carries a poor prognosis with low five-year survival. The diffuse histologic type and signet-ring morphology are associated with more aggressive behavior.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Gastric cancer is among the most common cancers and a leading cause of cancer death worldwide, with marked geographic variation. Incidence is highest in East Asia (notably Korea, Japan, and China), parts of Eastern Europe, and the Andean region of South America, tracking the regional prevalence of H. pylori and dietary patterns. Overall incidence has declined in many countries over recent decades, though young-onset and proximal/junctional tumors are an area of growing concern.</p>
