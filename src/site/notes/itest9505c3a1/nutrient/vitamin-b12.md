<h1>Vitamin B12</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a water-soluble, cobalt-containing vitamin that is unique among vitamins in its complexity and its exclusive natural occurrence in animal-derived foods. It functions as a cofactor for two critical enzymes: methionine synthase (which remethylates homocysteine to methionine, requiring methylcobalamin) and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (which converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA in odd-chain fatty acid and amino acid metabolism, requiring adenosylcobalamin).</p>
<p>B12 absorption is an elaborate multi-step process requiring adequate stomach acid (to release B12 from food proteins), intrinsic factor (produced by gastric parietal cells), and functional ileal receptors (cubilin-megalin complex). This complexity makes B12 deficiency common in the elderly (atrophic gastritis reduces acid and intrinsic factor), in individuals taking proton pump inhibitors or metformin, after gastric bypass surgery, and in strict vegans who do not supplement. The liver stores 2-5 years' supply, so deficiency develops slowly.</p>
<p>Neurological damage from B12 deficiency -- subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive decline, and psychiatric symptoms -- can be irreversible if not treated promptly. Methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin are the bioactive coenzyme forms, while cyanocobalamin (the most common supplement form) requires hepatic conversion. Sublingual and intramuscular routes bypass the intrinsic factor requirement, making them effective for individuals with absorption disorders.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Cofactor for methionine synthase, converting homocysteine to methionine</li>
<li>Required for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase in fatty acid and amino acid metabolism</li>
<li>Essential for DNA synthesis and proper cell division (especially red blood cells)</li>
<li>Maintains myelin sheath integrity around nerve fibers</li>
<li>Participates in the methionine cycle supporting methylation reactions (SAMe production)</li>
<li>Supports mood regulation through neurotransmitter metabolism</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Liver and organ meats (richest natural source)</li>
<li>Clams, mussels, and oysters</li>
<li>Beef and lamb</li>
<li>Fish (salmon, trout, tuna, sardines)</li>
<li>Dairy products (milk, yogurt, cheese)</li>
<li>Eggs</li>
<li>Fortified nutritional yeast</li>
<li>Fortified plant milks and cereals</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia (indistinguishable from folate deficiency hematologically), peripheral neuropathy (numbness, tingling, balance problems), subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, cognitive decline (memory loss, confusion), glossitis, fatigue, and psychiatric symptoms (depression, psychosis, &quot;megaloblastic madness&quot;). Importantly, folate supplementation can correct the anemia while neurological damage progresses silently.</p>
<p>Vitamin B12 has no established tolerable upper limit because toxicity is extremely rare -- excess is efficiently excreted renally due to its water-soluble nature. Very high doses of cyanocobalamin have rarely been associated with acne-like skin eruptions. There is no evidence of adverse effects from food or supplements in healthy individuals, making B12 one of the safest vitamins to supplement, particularly for at-risk groups (vegans, elderly, those on PPIs or metformin).</p>
