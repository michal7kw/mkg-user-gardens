<h1>Hepatitis C</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Hepatitis C is a liver infection caused by the <strong>hepatitis C virus (HCV)</strong>, a single-stranded RNA virus of the Flaviviridae family. It is transmitted primarily through blood-to-blood contact, with injection drug use the dominant route in most settings today. A defining feature of HCV is its propensity to establish <strong>chronic infection</strong>: the majority of acutely infected people fail to clear the virus and progress to lifelong infection, in part because of the virus's high genetic variability, which has also frustrated vaccine development.</p>
<p>Most infections are clinically silent. Acute hepatitis C is usually asymptomatic, and chronic infection often produces no symptoms for years to decades, during which low-grade hepatic inflammation drives slowly progressive fibrosis. This indolence is why hepatitis C is frequently undiagnosed until advanced liver disease appears, and why one-time screening of adults and risk-based testing are recommended to identify infected individuals before complications develop.</p>
<p>The therapeutic landscape has been transformed by <strong>direct-acting antivirals (DAAs)</strong>. Modern pangenotypic, all-oral regimens cure (achieve sustained virologic response) in well over 90% of patients within 8-12 weeks, with excellent tolerability. Cure halts disease progression and reduces — though in cirrhotics does not eliminate — the long-term risk of hepatocellular carcinoma, making global elimination of hepatitis C a realistic public-health goal.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Acute hepatitis C is symptomatic in only a minority; when present, symptoms are nonspecific (fatigue, nausea, anorexia, right upper quadrant discomfort), and overt jaundice is less common than in hepatitis A or B. Chronic infection is typically asymptomatic, with <strong>fatigue</strong> the most commonly reported complaint. Patients may first present with manifestations of advanced liver disease (ascites, variceal bleeding, encephalopathy) or with extrahepatic immune phenomena such as mixed cryoglobulinemia (purpura, arthralgia, neuropathy) and membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis.</p>

<p>A positive <strong>anti-HCV</strong> indicates exposure but cannot distinguish active from resolved infection; a positive <strong>HCV RNA</strong> confirms ongoing infection. Persistence of HCV RNA beyond six months defines chronic infection.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The leading risk factor in most countries is <strong>injection drug use</strong> with shared equipment. Historically, transfusion of unscreened blood products (before routine donor screening in the early 1990s) was a major source. Other routes include unsafe medical injections and inadequately sterilized equipment, hemodialysis, and occupational needlestick exposure. Perinatal and sexual transmission occur but are comparatively inefficient.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>The goal of treatment is <strong>cure</strong>, defined as sustained virologic response (undetectable HCV RNA 12 weeks after completing therapy). Pangenotypic <strong>DAA regimens</strong> such as sofosbuvir/velpatasvir or glecaprevir/pibrentasvir achieve cure in the vast majority of patients with short, well-tolerated oral courses, and have largely replaced interferon-based therapy. Patients with established cirrhosis still require ongoing <strong>HCC surveillance</strong> after cure, and complications of decompensated cirrhosis are managed accordingly. Harm-reduction measures (needle exchange, opioid agonist therapy) are central to preventing transmission and reinfection.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Chronic hepatitis C drives progressive fibrosis to <strong>cirrhosis</strong> in a substantial fraction of untreated patients over decades, with attendant risks of hepatic decompensation and <strong>hepatocellular carcinoma</strong>. Extrahepatic complications include mixed cryoglobulinemia with vasculitis and glomerulonephritis, and an increased risk of B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Co-infection with HIV or hepatitis B accelerates liver damage.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>With DAA-based cure, the prognosis is excellent and disease progression is halted; many patients with early fibrosis see histologic improvement. Prognosis is poorer when cirrhosis is already established before cure, as residual HCC risk persists. Untreated chronic infection carries a meaningful lifetime risk of cirrhosis, liver failure, and liver cancer.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Tens of millions of people are chronically infected with HCV worldwide, with genotype distribution and prevalence varying by region. Modeling studies estimate the global burden and inform elimination targets. The advent of curative therapy has shifted the epidemiologic challenge from treatment efficacy to diagnosis, access, and prevention of reinfection.</p>
