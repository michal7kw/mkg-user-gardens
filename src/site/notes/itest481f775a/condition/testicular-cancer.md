<h1>Testicular Cancer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Testicular cancer is a malignancy arising in the testis, of which germ cell tumors account for the overwhelming majority. These are broadly divided into seminomas, which tend to be more indolent and radiosensitive, and non-seminomatous germ cell tumors (including embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumor, choriocarcinoma, and teratoma), which are often more aggressive and more likely to be metastatic at diagnosis. Many tumors are mixed. The precursor lesion, germ cell neoplasia in situ, reflects an origin in arrested fetal germ cells, linking testicular cancer to a broader testicular dysgenesis spectrum that also includes cryptorchidism and infertility.</p>
<p>Although uncommon overall, testicular cancer is the most frequently diagnosed solid malignancy in young men aged roughly 15-40, and its incidence has been rising in many populations. It is one of the great success stories of oncology: even when it presents with widespread metastatic disease, it is highly curable because germ cell tumors are exquisitely sensitive to platinum-based chemotherapy. Cure rates exceed 95% for localized disease and remain high even for advanced disease, making accurate staging, risk stratification, and avoidance of overtreatment central concerns of modern management.</p>
<p>Serum tumor markers play an unusually prominent role in testicular cancer. Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (beta-hCG), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) aid diagnosis, contribute directly to staging and prognostic grouping, and are used to monitor treatment response and detect relapse. Because the marker profile differs between subtypes (AFP elevation indicates a non-seminomatous component), these tests are integral to clinical decision-making throughout the disease course.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The classic presentation is a painless lump, swelling, or change in consistency of one testicle, often noticed by the patient himself. Some men report a dull ache or heaviness in the scrotum or lower abdomen. Hormone-producing tumors can cause gynecomastia, and metastatic disease may present with back pain, a neck mass, or respiratory symptoms.</p>

<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The strongest risk factor is cryptorchidism (undescended testis), followed by a personal or family history of testicular cancer, prior germ cell neoplasia in situ, infertility and testicular dysgenesis, younger age, and higher incidence in white populations.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Nearly all patients begin with radical inguinal orchiectomy, which is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Subsequent management depends on subtype, stage, and tumor markers: selected low-stage tumors are followed with active surveillance, while metastatic or higher-stage disease is treated with platinum-based combination chemotherapy (classically bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin). Retroperitoneal lymph node dissection is used in selected non-seminoma cases, and radiotherapy has a more limited, selective role in seminoma. Sperm banking before treatment is recommended given fertility risks.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Complications include metastatic spread to retroperitoneal lymph nodes, lungs, and other sites; infertility and hypogonadism from the tumor or its treatment; long-term toxicities of chemotherapy (including cardiovascular and neurological effects); secondary malignancies; and relapse requiring salvage therapy.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Testicular cancer has an excellent prognosis, among the best of any malignancy. Localized disease is cured in the great majority of cases, and even metastatic disease retains high cure rates with chemotherapy. Prognosis is stratified by the International Germ Cell Consensus Classification using marker levels and sites of metastasis; non-seminoma, very high marker levels, and non-pulmonary visceral metastases predict relatively poorer outcomes.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Testicular cancer is relatively rare but is the most common solid cancer in young adult men, with peak incidence between ages 15 and 40. Incidence has risen over recent decades, particularly in white populations, while mortality has fallen sharply due to effective chemotherapy.</p>
