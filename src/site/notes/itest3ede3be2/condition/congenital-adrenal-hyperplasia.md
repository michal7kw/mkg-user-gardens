<h1>Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a group of <strong>autosomal recessive disorders</strong> of adrenal steroidogenesis caused by deficiency of one of the enzymes required for cortisol synthesis. The result is a <strong>block in the cortisol production pathway</strong> that triggers a <strong>compensatory rise in ACTH</strong> (from loss of negative feedback to the pituitary), which drives adrenal hyperplasia and <strong>shunting of steroid precursors into the androgen pathway</strong> -- producing the cardinal features of <strong>cortisol/aldosterone deficiency</strong> and <strong>androgen excess</strong>. <strong>21-hydroxylase deficiency (CYP21A2 mutations)</strong> accounts for <strong>~95%</strong> of cases; the remainder are 11-beta-hydroxylase, 3-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, and 17-alpha-hydroxylase deficiencies.</p>
<p>CAH exists on a clinical spectrum that mirrors the degree of enzyme deficiency:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Classic salt-wasting CAH</strong> (complete enzyme deficiency, ~75% of classic cases) -- the most severe form. Presents in the <strong>neonatal period (1-2 weeks of life)</strong> with <strong>adrenal crisis</strong>: vomiting, dehydration, hypotension, <strong>hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia</strong>. <strong>Males</strong> appear normal at birth (no genital ambiguity) and may be missed by exam -- newborn screening (17-hydroxyprogesterone) has been lifesaving. <strong>Females</strong> are typically detected at birth due to <strong>ambiguous genitalia</strong> (clitoromegaly, labial fusion, common urogenital sinus from in utero androgen exposure)</li>
<li><strong>Classic simple-virilizing CAH</strong> (partial enzyme deficiency, ~25% of classic cases) -- aldosterone production is preserved, so no salt-wasting crisis, but cortisol deficiency and androgen excess persist. Presents with ambiguous genitalia in females and premature virilization in both sexes</li>
<li><strong>Non-classic (late-onset) CAH</strong> (mild deficiency, most common form overall; 1:1000 to 1:100 depending on ethnicity) -- presents in late childhood to adulthood with <strong>premature pubarche, hirsutism, acne, irregular menses, polycystic ovary-like features, and infertility</strong>. Often mistaken for PCOS</li>
</ul>
<p>The diagnosis rests on <strong>elevated 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP)</strong>, the substrate that accumulates proximal to the 21-hydroxylase block; values &gt;10,000 ng/dL confirm classic CAH, with <strong>ACTH stimulation test</strong> and <strong>CYP21A2 genetic testing</strong> for equivocal cases. <strong>Newborn screening</strong> (universal in most developed countries) measures 17-OHP on the heel-stick blood spot, dramatically reducing the neonatal mortality that historically affected undiagnosed male infants with salt-wasting crisis.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Glucocorticoid replacement (cornerstone for all forms):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Children (classic)</strong> -- <strong>hydrocortisone</strong> 10-15 mg/m2/day in 3 divided doses (to allow normal growth; avoid excessive doses that suppress growth)</li>
<li><strong>Adults (classic)</strong> -- <strong>hydrocortisone</strong> 15-25 mg/day (split AM/PM), OR <strong>prednisone</strong> 5-7.5 mg/day, OR <strong>dexamethasone</strong> 0.25-0.5 mg at bedtime (especially for women with androgen excess; dexamethasone is most potent at ACTH suppression but carries highest osteoporosis risk)</li>
<li><strong>Goal</strong> -- replace cortisol deficiency AND suppress ACTH (and thus androgen excess); a difficult balance because over-treatment causes <strong>iatrogenic Cushing syndrome</strong> (osteoporosis, weight gain, glucose intolerance) and under-treatment allows androgen excess</li>
<li><strong>Monitor</strong> -- clinical (growth, virilization, BMI, bone density) + biochemical (17-OHP, androstenedione, testosterone, renin, electrolytes)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Mineralocorticoid replacement (for salt-wasting and simple-virilizing classic forms):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fludrocortisone</strong> 0.05-0.2 mg/day (start at 0.1 mg in neonates)</li>
<li><strong>Salt supplementation</strong> (sodium chloride 1-3 g/day) in infants until they start solid foods</li>
<li><strong>Monitor</strong> -- blood pressure, electrolytes, plasma renin activity</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Stress-dose glucocorticoids (CRITICAL -- prevents adrenal crisis):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Minor illness (fever &gt;38°C, gastroenteritis)</strong> -- double or triple the usual oral hydrocortisone dose for 24-48 hours</li>
<li><strong>Major stress (surgery, severe trauma, sepsis)</strong> -- <strong>hydrocortisone 50-100 mg IV</strong> (children: 50 mg/m2), then 50 mg IV every 6 hours until stable, then taper</li>
<li><strong>Vomiting or inability to take oral meds</strong> -- <strong>parenteral hydrocortisone</strong> (IM or IV); ** Solu-Cortef Act-o-Vial** at home for emergencies</li>
<li><strong>Medical alert bracelet/necklace</strong> -- mandatory</li>
<li><strong>Patient and family education</strong> -- this is the most important element; adrenal crisis is the leading cause of death in CAH</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Management of androgen excess (non-classic CAH and adjunctive in classic):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low-dose dexamethasone</strong> at bedtime (most potent ACTH suppressor; caution re: bone)</li>
<li><strong>Anti-androgen + aromatase inhibitor</strong> combination (leuprolide, letrozole in prepubertal girls with central precocious puberty)</li>
<li><strong>Oral contraceptives</strong> with anti-androgenic progestins (drospirenone) for hirsutism and acne in non-classic CAH</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Genital reconstructive surgery (females with virilization):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Highly individualized decision</strong> -- traditionally done in infancy, but increasingly deferred to allow patient autonomy (controversial; consent concerns)</li>
<li><strong>Clitoroplasty</strong> (size reduction preserving sensation) and <strong>vaginoplasty</strong> (separation of urogenital sinus)</li>
<li>Multidisciplinary team: pediatric endocrinology, urology, gynecology, psychology, ethics</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Management of associated conditions:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Testicular adrenal rest tumors</strong> -- bilateral testicular masses composed of ectopic adrenal tissue driven by ACTH; monitor with annual ultrasound; optimize glucocorticoid suppression</li>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis</strong> -- DEXA; calcium + vitamin D; bisphosphonate if osteoporotic; minimize glucocorticoid dose</li>
<li><strong>Infertility</strong> -- women often subfertile (anovulation from androgen excess, surgical complications); men from testicular adrenal rest tumors and suppressed gonadotropins</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular risk</strong> -- monitor and treat traditional risk factors; chronic androgen excess and iatrogenic Cushing both increase risk</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Future and investigational therapies:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Modified-release hydrocortisone</strong> (Chronocort, Efmody) -- better mimics circadian cortisol rhythm; approved in Europe for CAH</li>
<li><strong>CRN00808 and other ACTH antagonists</strong> (corticotropin-releasing factor receptor antagonists) -- investigational; may reduce glucocorticoid burden</li>
<li><strong>Gene therapy</strong> -- AAV-mediated CYP21A2 transfer; preclinical and early-phase trials</li>
<li><strong>Bilateral adrenalectomy</strong> -- controversial; removes source of androgen excess but creates Addison's; reserved for refractory disease</li>
</ul>
