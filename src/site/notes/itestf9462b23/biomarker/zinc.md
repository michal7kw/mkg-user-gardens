<h1>Zinc</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Zinc is an essential trace mineral required by more than 300 metalloenzymes and roughly 2,000 zinc-finger transcription factors, giving it broad roles in DNA synthesis, cell division, protein metabolism, antioxidant defense, and the structure and signaling of the immune system. The body holds 2-3 grams of total zinc, concentrated in muscle and bone, with no dedicated storage pool. Because tissues draw continuously on a small, tightly regulated circulating fraction, dietary intake and intestinal absorption must be maintained day to day.</p>
<p>Serum or plasma zinc measures only about 0.1% of total body zinc, yet it remains the most practical and widely used biomarker of zinc status. Circulating zinc is bound mainly to albumin (~70%) and alpha-2-macroglobulin, so changes in those carrier proteins, hemodilution, and time of day all shift the result. Importantly, zinc is a negative acute-phase reactant: during infection, trauma, or any inflammatory state, zinc is redistributed from plasma into the liver and tissues, lowering serum levels independent of true body stores. For this reason results should be interpreted alongside an inflammatory marker such as CRP and, where possible, with a morning fasting sample.</p>
<p>Clinically, serum zinc is ordered to confirm suspected deficiency (impaired immunity, delayed wound healing, dermatitis, growth failure, hypogonadism, altered taste), to monitor patients at risk (malabsorption, chronic diarrhea, alcoholism, parenteral nutrition, restrictive diets), and to detect excess from over-supplementation. It performs well at the extremes of status but is relatively insensitive for marginal deficiency, where functional tests, response to a supplementation trial, or dietary assessment add value.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals vary by laboratory, assay (atomic absorption spectrometry vs. ICP-MS), specimen (serum vs. plasma), age, sex, and fasting state.</li>
<li>Serum zinc falls in the postprandial period and during acute illness; a morning, fasting sample minimizes diurnal and dietary variation.</li>
<li>Hemolysis falsely raises results because erythrocytes are zinc-rich; rubber stoppers and some collection tubes can introduce zinc contamination.</li>
<li>Because zinc is a negative acute-phase reactant, a low value during active inflammation may reflect redistribution rather than true deficiency.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase serum zinc:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Zinc supplementation or zinc-rich diet (oysters, red meat, shellfish, legumes)</li>
<li>Recent fasting state and morning sampling (higher than afternoon)</li>
<li>Hemolysis or zinc contamination of the sample (artifactual)</li>
<li>Catabolic/tissue-breakdown states releasing intracellular zinc</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease serum zinc:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Inadequate intake or high dietary phytate (whole grains, legumes) reducing absorption</li>
<li>Malabsorption (celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, bariatric surgery, chronic diarrhea)</li>
<li>Acute inflammation, infection, trauma, or surgery (redistribution; negative acute-phase response)</li>
<li>Chronic liver and kidney disease, including increased urinary losses</li>
<li>Hypoalbuminemia (less carrier protein)</li>
<li>Pregnancy and lactation (increased demand and hemodilution)</li>
<li>Competing minerals at high dose (excess iron or copper) and chronic alcohol use</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Zinc Deficiency</strong> — the direct cause; manifests as impaired cell-mediated immunity, delayed wound healing, dermatitis, alopecia, diarrhea, hypogeusia (loss of taste), growth retardation in children, and hypogonadism in men.</li>
<li><strong>Impaired immune function</strong> — zinc is essential for thymulin activity, T-lymphocyte function, and balanced Th1/Th2 responses; deficiency raises susceptibility to infection and is linked to more severe respiratory illness.</li>
<li><strong>Malabsorption and Malabsorption (Malnutrition and Malabsorption, Celiac Disease)</strong> — reduced intestinal uptake lowers status even with adequate intake.</li>
<li><strong>Copper Imbalance</strong> — zinc and copper compete for intestinal absorption; chronically high zinc induces copper deficiency, while the serum zinc:copper ratio is used as a functional status marker.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Excess supplementation or exposure</strong> — sustained high intake can cause nausea, impaired immune response at very high doses, and secondary copper deficiency with anemia and neutropenia.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Confirming and tracking treatment response in suspected deficiency</li>
<li>Surveillance in malabsorption, parenteral nutrition, chronic diarrhea, and restrictive diets</li>
<li>Watching for copper depletion during long-term high-dose zinc therapy</li>
<li>Interpreting alongside CRP/albumin to separate true deficiency from inflammatory redistribution</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Zinc supplementation</strong> — elemental zinc 15-30 mg/day corrects mild-to-moderate deficiency; higher therapeutic doses are used short-term under supervision. Doses above ~40 mg/day chronically risk copper depletion, so pair prolonged high-dose use with copper monitoring.</li>
<li><strong>Increase dietary zinc</strong> — emphasize oysters, red meat, poultry, shellfish, dairy, eggs, legumes, nuts, and seeds; animal-source zinc is more bioavailable than plant sources.</li>
<li><strong>Reduce phytate-driven malabsorption</strong> — soaking, sprouting, fermenting, or leavening grains and legumes lowers phytate and improves zinc absorption.</li>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying cause</strong> — manage malabsorptive conditions (celiac disease, IBD), chronic diarrhea, and alcohol use that deplete zinc.</li>
<li><strong>Balance with other minerals</strong> — avoid taking high-dose iron at the same time as zinc, and maintain copper intake when supplementing zinc long-term.</li>
<li><strong>Reassess in context of inflammation</strong> — when a low value coincides with elevated CRP, recheck after the acute phase resolves before escalating supplementation.</li>
</ol>
