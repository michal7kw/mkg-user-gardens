<h1>MYC</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>MYC is the gene encoding c-Myc (UniProt P01106), a basic helix-loop-helix leucine-zipper (bHLH-LZ) transcription factor and one of the most extensively studied proto-oncogenes. c-Myc functions as a master regulator of the transcriptional programs that drive cell growth and proliferation, acting on an estimated 10–15% of all genes. The MYC gene was originally identified as the cellular homolog of the viral oncogene v-myc.</p>
<p>As a transcription factor, c-Myc heterodimerizes with its obligate partner protein and binds E-box DNA sequences to activate or repress target genes governing ribosome biogenesis, protein synthesis, nucleotide and energy metabolism, and cell-cycle entry. Through these programs c-Myc couples external growth signals to the biosynthetic and bioenergetic capacity a cell needs to divide. Its activity is tightly controlled at the level of transcription, mRNA stability, and rapid protein turnover, reflecting its potent growth-promoting potential.</p>
<p>Deregulation of MYC — through gene amplification, chromosomal translocation, or constitutive upstream signaling — is among the most common molecular events in human cancer. Aberrant c-Myc activity sustains the proliferative and metabolic demands of tumor cells, making it a long-sought but historically &quot;undruggable&quot; therapeutic target now being pursued through indirect and emerging direct strategies.</p>
<h2>Function</h2>
<p>c-Myc drives a broad anabolic program. By transcriptionally up-regulating components of the ribosome and the machinery of protein synthesis, it expands the cell's capacity for biomass production (ribosome biogenesis). In parallel it reprograms metabolism, enhancing glycolysis and glutamine utilization to supply the carbon, nitrogen, and ATP required for rapid proliferation. These coordinated outputs allow c-Myc to act as a central hub that translates growth-factor signaling into the metabolic and biosynthetic state characteristic of dividing — and of cancer — cells.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>MYC deregulation is a hallmark of many malignancies and is generally associated with aggressive disease and poor prognosis. Because c-Myc lacks a conventional enzymatic active site, direct inhibition has been difficult; therapeutic efforts therefore focus on disrupting its dimerization, destabilizing the protein, blocking its transcription (e.g., via bromodomain inhibitors), or exploiting the metabolic vulnerabilities that MYC-driven tumors acquire. The dependence of these tumors on MYC-induced glycolysis and ribosome biogenesis offers a rational basis for metabolism- and translation-targeted approaches.</p>
