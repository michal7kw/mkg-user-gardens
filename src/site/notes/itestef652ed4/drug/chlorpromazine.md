<h1>Chlorpromazine</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Chlorpromazine, introduced into psychiatry in 1952 by Delay and Deniker, is the prototype first-generation (typical) antipsychotic and one of the most consequential drugs in modern medicine. As a phenothiazine, it is a broad-spectrum receptor antagonist: a potent dopamine D2 receptor blocker (the basis of its antipsychotic action) with additional clinically meaningful antagonism at alpha-1 adrenergic receptors (causing orthostatic hypotension), histamine H1 receptors (causing sedation and weight gain), and muscarinic cholinergic receptors (causing anticholinergic effects). This broad binding profile explains both its therapeutic versatility and its substantial side-effect burden.</p>
<p>Clinically, chlorpromazine retains a role in acute psychosis (particularly agitated schizophrenia and bipolar mania), severe nausea and vomiting (including chemotherapy- and opioid-induced), intractable hiccups, and preoperative sedation. For outpatient schizophrenia management it has been largely replaced by second-generation antipsychotics (olanzapine, risperidone, aripiprazole) which carry a lower risk of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) and tardive dyskinesia. Chlorpromazine remains on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines and is included in many emergency department formularies for acute agitation.</p>
<p>A Cochrane systematic review of 50+ trials (Adams et al., 2014) confirmed chlorpromazine's enduring efficacy for schizophrenia, comparable in symptom reduction to other typical antipsychotics but with a less favorable neurological side-effect profile than many comparators. <strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (one of the most extensively studied antipsychotics in the literature).</p>
<h2>Pharmacology</h2>
<p><strong>Mechanism of Action:</strong><br />
Chlorpromazine's clinical effects derive from antagonism at multiple receptor types:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dopamine D2 (Ki ~5 nM):</strong> Postsynaptic blockade in mesolimbic pathway is the primary antipsychotic mechanism. Concomitant blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway produces extrapyramidal symptoms (parkinsonism, akathisia, dystonia, tardive dyskinesia). Blockade in the tuberoinfundibular pathway elevates prolactin (galactorrhea, gynecomastia).</li>
<li><strong>Alpha-1 adrenergic:</strong> Orthostatic hypotension, reflex tachycardia, dizziness</li>
<li><strong>Histamine H1:</strong> Sedation, weight gain, anxiolysis</li>
<li><strong>Muscarinic M1:</strong> Anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, cognitive blunting)</li>
<li><strong>5-HT2A:</strong> Modest antagonism; contributes to the favorable EPS profile compared to butyrophenones like haloperidol</li>
<li><strong>D1, D4:</strong> Weaker antagonism with uncertain clinical contribution</li>
<li><strong>Thermoregulation:</strong> Depresses the hypothalamic temperature set point (poikilothermia -- patient drifts toward ambient temperature; useful in heat stroke, dangerous in hypothermia)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pharmacokinetics:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Absorption: Erratic oral bioavailability (10-70% due to first-pass); IM injection more reliable (peak 15-30 min)</li>
<li>Distribution: Highly lipophilic; Vd 10-20 L/kg; &gt;90% protein-bound</li>
<li>Metabolism: Extensive hepatic via CYP2D6 and CYP3A4; &gt;100 metabolites identified (7-hydroxychlorpromazine is active)</li>
<li>Elimination: Half-life ~30 h (range 16-36 h, longer with chronic use)</li>
<li>Time to steady-state: 7-10 days</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Key Drug Interactions:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Other CNS depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines): additive sedation and respiratory depression</li>
<li>Antihypertensives: additive hypotension</li>
<li>Anticholinergics: additive anticholinergic toxicity (delirium, ileus, urinary retention)</li>
<li>Lithium: rare neurotoxicity syndrome</li>
<li>Levodopa / dopamine agonists: mutual antagonism</li>
<li>QTc-prolonging drugs (amiodarone, ondansetron, macrolides): additive torsades risk</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Use</h2>
<p><strong>Primary Indications:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Schizophrenia and other psychoses (especially acute agitation; IM formulation)</li>
<li>Bipolar I disorder, manic episode (acute)</li>
<li>Severe nausea/vomiting refractory to first-line antiemetics (chemotherapy, palliative care)</li>
<li>Intractable hiccups (singultus) -- one of the few reliably effective agents</li>
<li>Preoperative sedation</li>
<li>Adjunctive treatment of severe tetanus (spasm control)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Dosing:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Psychosis start: 25-100 mg PO TID, titrate over 1-2 weeks to 400-800 mg/day</li>
<li>Acute agitation: 25-50 mg IM, may repeat in 1 h (max 100 mg/4h)</li>
<li>Antiemetic: 10-25 mg PO q4-6h or 25 mg IM</li>
<li>Hiccups: 25-50 mg PO TID; refractory cases may require 25-50 mg IM</li>
<li>Tetanus: 50-100 mg IM q6-8h</li>
<li>Hepatic impairment: reduce dose by 50%</li>
<li>Elderly: start at 25-33% of adult dose</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<p><strong>Contraindications:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Known hypersensitivity to phenothiazines</li>
<li>Comatose state or CNS depression from other causes</li>
<li>Bone marrow depression</li>
<li>Severe hepatic impairment</li>
<li>Concurrent MAOI (relative -- hypertensive crisis risk)</li>
<li>Parkinson's disease (relative; worsens motor symptoms)</li>
<li>Pediatric surgery (&lt;1 year)</li>
</ul>
