<h1>Neutrophils</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Neutrophils are the most abundant circulating white blood cell, normally constituting 40-70% of the total leukocyte population. As the principal effectors of the innate immune system, they are the body's first cellular responders to bacterial and fungal invasion, migrating rapidly from the bloodstream to sites of infection or tissue damage in response to chemotactic signals. Once at the target site, neutrophils neutralize pathogens through phagocytosis, the release of reactive oxygen species (respiratory burst), degranulation of antimicrobial enzymes, and the extrusion of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).</p>
<p>The measure is reported in two complementary ways. The percentage reflects the proportion of neutrophils within the white blood cell differential, while the absolute neutrophil count (ANC) — derived by multiplying the total WBC count by the neutrophil percentage — gives the actual number of neutrophils per unit volume of blood. The ANC is far more clinically meaningful than the percentage alone, because it directly quantifies the body's available defensive capacity against bacterial infection. A normal percentage can mask a dangerously low absolute count when the total WBC is depressed.</p>
<p>Neutrophils are produced in the bone marrow, where they mature over approximately 7-14 days before release into circulation. They have a very short circulating half-life of roughly 6-8 hours before marginating into tissues, so the count is a dynamic, rapidly shifting metric that responds within hours to acute stressors such as infection, corticosteroids, or physical stress. This rapid responsiveness makes the neutrophil count and ANC central to the evaluation of acute infection, the monitoring of chemotherapy toxicity, and the assessment of marrow function.</p>

<p><em>Notes:</em> The percentage alone is unreliable in isolation; always interpret alongside the total WBC count and the ANC. Reference intervals vary modestly by laboratory, age, and demographic factors (individuals of African descent may have constitutionally lower counts — &quot;benign ethnic neutropenia&quot;). The ANC = (total WBC) × (% neutrophils + % bands) / 100.</p>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<h3>Factors that Increase Neutrophils (Neutrophilia)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bacterial infection:</strong> The classic driver — acute pyogenic bacterial infections trigger rapid marrow release of neutrophils, often with a &quot;left shift&quot; (immature bands).</li>
<li><strong>Acute inflammation / tissue necrosis:</strong> Burns, infarction (e.g., myocardial), trauma, and surgery release inflammatory mediators that mobilize neutrophils.</li>
<li><strong>Stress / catecholamines:</strong> Physical or emotional stress, exercise, and epinephrine cause demargination of neutrophils into the circulating pool (demargination neutrophilia).</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroids:</strong> Reduce margination and egress into tissues, raising the circulating count.</li>
<li><strong>Smoking:</strong> Causes a chronic mild neutrophilia.</li>
<li><strong>Myeloproliferative disorders:</strong> Chronic myeloid leukemia and other myeloproliferative neoplasms cause marked, sustained neutrophilia.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Factors that Decrease Neutrophils (Neutropenia)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Chemotherapy / marrow suppression:</strong> Cytotoxic drugs and radiation suppress marrow production — the most common iatrogenic cause; the ANC nadir defines infection risk.</li>
<li><strong>Viral infection:</strong> Many viral illnesses (influenza, hepatitis, HIV, EBV) transiently suppress neutrophil production or accelerate consumption.</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional deficiency:</strong> Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency impair marrow DNA synthesis, lowering neutrophil output (often with macrocytosis).</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune destruction:</strong> Systemic lupus erythematosus, Felty syndrome, and autoimmune neutropenia destroy circulating neutrophils.</li>
<li><strong>Overwhelming sepsis:</strong> Severe sepsis can exhaust marrow reserves, producing neutropenia — a poor prognostic sign.</li>
<li><strong>Congenital / constitutional:</strong> Cyclic neutropenia, severe congenital neutropenia, and benign ethnic neutropenia.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Elevated levels (neutrophilia, &gt; 7.7 x10^3/µL) are associated with:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><a href="/garden/itestef652ed4/condition/bacterial-infections/" class="internal-link">Bacterial Infections</a></strong>: The prototypical cause; acute bacterial infection produces neutrophilia, frequently with a left shift toward immature band forms.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/garden/itestef652ed4/condition/inflammation/" class="internal-link">Inflammation</a></strong>: Acute inflammatory states, tissue necrosis, trauma, and post-surgical responses elevate the count via inflammatory mediator release.</li>
<li><strong>Stress and corticosteroids</strong>: Demargination from catecholamines or steroid therapy raises the circulating count without true marrow expansion.</li>
</ul>
<p>Reduced levels (neutropenia, ANC &lt; 1.8 x10^3/µL) are associated with:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Marrow suppression</strong>: Chemotherapy, radiation, and aplastic processes are the leading causes of clinically significant neutropenia.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/garden/itestef652ed4/condition/sepsis/" class="internal-link">Sepsis</a></strong>: Neutropenia in the setting of severe infection reflects marrow exhaustion and carries a poor prognosis.</li>
</ul>
<p>Monitoring use cases:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ANC stratifies infection risk:</strong> ANC 1.0-1.8 (mild), 0.5-1.0 (moderate), and &lt; 0.5 x10^3/µL (severe). Severe neutropenia is a clinical emergency warranting neutropenic precautions and prompt evaluation of any fever (febrile neutropenia).</li>
<li><strong>Chemotherapy monitoring:</strong> Serial ANC measurement guides dose timing, growth-factor support, and the safe administration of subsequent cycles.</li>
<li><strong>Left shift:</strong> The appearance of immature band neutrophils signals an intense demand on the marrow, typically from acute bacterial infection.</li>
</ul>
