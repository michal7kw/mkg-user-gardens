<h1>Western Diet</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The Western diet (also called the Standard American Diet) is a dietary pattern that emerged with industrialized food production and is defined by a high intake of ultra-processed and energy-dense foods: refined grains, added sugars and sugar-sweetened beverages, saturated and trans fats, red and processed meats, and high-sodium packaged foods. It is correspondingly low in dietary fiber, whole grains, legumes, fruits, vegetables, and unsaturated fats. Unlike most entries in this knowledge graph, the Western diet is documented as a <em>harmful</em> exposure-like intervention—a pattern to avoid rather than adopt.</p>
<p>Epidemiological and mechanistic research consistently links the Western diet to the global rise in non-communicable chronic disease. It is a major driver of obesity, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, certain cancers, and inflammatory bowel disease. Its effects are mediated not only through caloric excess but through specific food components that reshape metabolism, immune function, and the gut microbiome.</p>
<p>Evidence Level: 4/5. Large prospective cohorts and controlled mechanistic studies provide strong and consistent evidence that the Western dietary pattern increases cardiometabolic and inflammatory disease risk.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>The Western diet exerts harm through multiple interacting mechanisms. Excess refined carbohydrate and sugar intake drives postprandial hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and progressive insulin resistance. High saturated and trans fat intake worsens dyslipidemia and endothelial dysfunction. Landmark work by Christ and colleagues demonstrated that a Western diet triggers NLRP3-inflammasome-dependent innate immune reprogramming, leaving myeloid progenitors in a heightened inflammatory state even after the diet is withdrawn—a form of &quot;trained immunity&quot; that perpetuates systemic inflammation. Low fiber intake reduces short-chain fatty acid (butyrate) production and impairs gut barrier integrity, promoting dysbiosis, endotoxemia, and further metabolic inflammation. The net result is chronic low-grade inflammation underlying the shared pathophysiology of cardiometabolic and degenerative disease.</p>
<h2>Health Impact Summary</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Obesity and metabolic syndrome</strong>: Energy density and palatability promote overconsumption and adiposity</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes</strong>: Refined carbohydrates and insulin resistance accelerate dysglycemia</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular disease</strong>: Dyslipidemia, hypertension, and endothelial dysfunction</li>
<li><strong>Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease</strong>: Fructose and saturated fat drive hepatic steatosis</li>
<li><strong>Inflammatory bowel disease</strong>: Dysbiosis and impaired gut barrier</li>
<li><strong>Systemic inflammation</strong>: NLRP3 activation and trained immunity</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety Information</h2>
<p>The Western diet is itself the hazard; the recommended &quot;intervention&quot; is replacement with an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern (e.g., Mediterranean, DASH, or MIND diet). There are no contraindications to <em>reducing</em> Western-diet exposure; dietary transitions should preserve nutritional adequacy.</p>
