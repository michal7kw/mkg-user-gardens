<h1>Cytoplasm</h1>
<h2>Description</h2>
<p>The cytoplasm is the entire contents of a cell enclosed within the plasma membrane but outside the nucleus. It consists of the cytosol — a concentrated aqueous solution of ions, metabolites, proteins, and macromolecules — together with the cytoskeleton and all of the membrane-bound organelles suspended within it. The cytoplasm is a crowded, highly organized compartment in which diffusion, molecular crowding, and dynamic cytoskeletal scaffolds shape the rate and localization of biochemical reactions.</p>
<h2>Function</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Metabolism</strong>: The cytosol is the site of glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, fatty-acid synthesis, and numerous other metabolic reactions</li>
<li><strong>Protein synthesis</strong>: Free ribosomes in the cytosol translate mRNAs encoding cytosolic and certain organellar proteins</li>
<li><strong>Signal transduction</strong>: Many signaling cascades (second messengers, kinase relays) propagate through the cytosol between the membrane and the nucleus</li>
<li><strong>Transport and organization</strong>: The cytoskeleton provides tracks for organelle and vesicle movement and maintains cell shape and internal architecture</li>
<li><strong>Protein quality control</strong>: The cytosolic ubiquitin-proteasome system degrades damaged and regulatory proteins</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Relevance</h2>
<p>Disorders of cytoplasmic enzymes underlie many inherited metabolic diseases (e.g., glycolytic enzyme deficiencies causing hemolytic anemia). Cytoplasmic inclusion bodies and protein aggregates are hallmarks of neurodegenerative disease, and leakage of cytoplasmic enzymes such as aminotransferases and lactate dehydrogenase into the blood is widely used as a marker of cell injury.</p>
