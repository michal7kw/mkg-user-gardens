<h1>Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH) is a hematological red blood cell (RBC) index quantifying the average mass of hemoglobin protein per individual red blood cell, expressed in picograms (pg; 10^-12 grams). It is calculated from the complete blood count (CBC) as <strong>MCH = Hemoglobin (g/dL) × 10 / RBC count (×10^6/uL)</strong>. MCH is reported alongside MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume) and MCHC (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration) as part of the standard RBC indices, and is used to characterize anemias.</p>
<p>MCH almost universally scales in parallel with MCV: a physically larger cell (macrocytic, high MCV) physically holds a greater total mass of hemoglobin, yielding a high MCH. Conversely, a physically small cell (microcytic, low MCV) caused by iron restriction contains less absolute hemoglobin, lowering MCH. Because it tracks size so closely, MCH alone offers relatively little independent diagnostic value beyond what MCV already predicts, but it remains a critical confirmatory index within the CBC suite.</p>
<p>Clinically, <strong>low MCH (&lt;27 pg, &quot;hypochromic&quot;)</strong> indicates iron-restricted erythropoiesis -- the cell cannot synthesize sufficient hemoglobin. The two principal causes are <strong>iron deficiency anemia</strong> (most common worldwide) and <strong>thalassemia</strong> (alpha or beta trait). <strong>High MCH (&gt;33 pg, &quot;macrocytic&quot;)</strong> indicates macrocytosis, in which larger-than-normal cells hold more hemoglobin mass; the principal causes are <strong>vitamin B12 deficiency</strong> (pernicious anemia, malabsorption), <strong>folate deficiency</strong>, and <strong>liver disease / alcoholism</strong>. The combination of MCV and MCH (with MCHC, RDW) provides a powerful anemia classification framework: microcytic hypochromic (low MCV, low MCH), macrocytic (high MCV, high MCH), and normocytic normochromic (normal MCV, normal MCH).</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational CBC index; universal clinical use; WHO essential).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range is analyzer-specific (impedance vs flow cytometry methods differ slightly)</li>
<li>Always interpret with MCV, MCHC, RDW, hemoglobin, and the peripheral smear</li>
<li>MCH tracks closely with MCV; discordance suggests lab artifact or unusual hemoglobinopathies</li>
<li>False elevation with cold agglutinins, hyperglycemia, lipemia (interfere with cell counter)</li>
<li>Newborns have higher MCH (~34-40 pg); declines to adult range by 6 months</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase MCH (macrocytosis, hypochromia resolves):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 deficiency</strong> (pernicious anemia, malabsorption, gastric bypass, vegan diet, metformin)</li>
<li><strong>Folate deficiency</strong> (poor diet, alcoholism, malabsorption, pregnancy, methotrexate, phenytoin)</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease / cirrhosis / alcoholism</strong> (macrocytosis from altered lipid metabolism)</li>
<li><strong>Reticulocytosis</strong> (immature RBCs are larger; hemolytic anemia, acute blood loss recovery)</li>
<li><strong>Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)</strong></li>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong> (mild macrocytosis)</li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced macrocytosis:</strong> hydroxyurea, zidovudine (AZT), methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin, chemotherapy</li>
<li><strong>Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, aplastic anemia</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease MCH (hypochromia, iron-restricted erythropoiesis):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency anemia</strong> (most common cause; dietary, malabsorption, blood loss)</li>
<li><strong>Thalassemia</strong> (alpha or beta trait, minor, intermedia)</li>
<li><strong>Sideroblastic anemia</strong></li>
<li><strong>Anemia of chronic disease / chronic inflammation</strong> (hepcidin-mediated iron sequestration)</li>
<li><strong>Lead poisoning</strong> (impaired heme synthesis)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic kidney disease</strong> (functional iron deficiency)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Low MCH (&lt;27 pg, hypochromic) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency anemia:</strong> the most common cause of microcytic hypochromic anemia worldwide; low MCV, low MCH, low ferritin, high TIBC, low transferrin saturation</li>
<li><strong>Thalassemia trait:</strong> low MCV (often &lt;75 fL), low MCH (often &lt;25 pg), high RBC count, normal iron studies, normal or elevated ferritin; ethnic predisposition (Mediterranean, Asian, African)</li>
<li><strong>Anemia of chronic disease:</strong> low-normal MCV, low-normal MCH, high ferritin, low TIBC</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>High MCH (&gt;33 pg, macrocytic) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 deficiency:</strong> megaloblastic anemia; hypersegmented neutrophils, pancytopenia, neurologic symptoms (subacute combined degeneration, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive impairment)</li>
<li><strong>Folate deficiency:</strong> megaloblastic anemia; identical hematologic picture to B12 deficiency; no neurologic symptoms</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease / alcoholism:</strong> non-megaloblastic macrocytosis; target cells, acanthocytes</li>
<li><strong>Reticulocytosis:</strong> hemolytic anemia, acute blood loss recovery</li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced:</strong> hydroxyurea, zidovudine, methotrexate, chemotherapy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Not independently diagnostic</strong> -- always interpret with MCV, hemoglobin, ferritin, B12, folate, peripheral smear</li>
<li><strong>Tracks with MCV</strong> -- redundant with MCV in most clinical situations</li>
<li><strong>Lab artifacts</strong> (cold agglutinins, lipemia) can falsely elevate MCH</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Anemia classification (with MCV, MCHC, RDW)</li>
<li>Iron deficiency anemia treatment monitoring (MCH rises with iron repletion over 4-8 weeks)</li>
<li>B12 deficiency treatment monitoring (MCH falls with B12 repletion)</li>
<li>Thalassemia trait identification (with MCV, Hb electrophoresis)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>MCH reflects underlying erythropoiesis; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For low MCH (iron deficiency):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Oral iron supplementation:</strong> ferrous sulfate 325 mg TID (65 mg elemental TID) on empty stomach; alternate-day dosing improves absorption (Stoffel 2017)</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C</strong> 200-500 mg with iron enhances non-heme iron absorption</li>
<li><strong>Heme-iron dietary emphasis:</strong> red meat, organ meats, shellfish</li>
<li><strong>Cast-iron cookware</strong></li>
<li><strong>Avoid calcium, coffee, tea, bran</strong> at the same meal as iron</li>
<li><strong>Investigate and treat underlying cause:</strong> celiac disease, H. pylori, menorrhagia, GI bleeding</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For high MCH (B12/folate deficiency):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 (methylcobalamin or cyanocobalamin)</strong> 1000 mcg IM daily x 1 week, then weekly x 1 month, then monthly; or oral 1000-2000 mcg daily</li>
<li><strong>Folate (methylfolate preferred)</strong> 400-1000 mcg daily</li>
<li><strong>Always check B12 before treating with folate alone</strong> (folate can mask B12-deficiency anemia while allowing neurologic damage to progress)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For liver disease / alcohol-related macrocytosis:</strong> hepatology referral; alcohol cessation; nutritional support</li>
<li><strong>For thalassemia trait:</strong> no treatment needed (carrier state); genetic counseling</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> offending drugs (methotrexate, hydroxyurea) if possible</li>
</ol>
