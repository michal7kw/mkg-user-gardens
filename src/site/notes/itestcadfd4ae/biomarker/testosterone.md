<h1>Testosterone</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Testosterone is the principal androgen and an anabolic steroid hormone synthesized primarily by the Leydig cells of the testes in men and, in smaller amounts, by the ovaries and adrenal cortex in women. Production is governed by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis: gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus stimulates pituitary release of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and LH in turn drives testicular testosterone synthesis through a negative-feedback loop. As a biomarker, serum testosterone is the laboratory measurement of total circulating testosterone, the majority of which is bound to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) and albumin, with only 1-2% present as biologically active free hormone.</p>
<p>In circulation, testosterone exerts effects through the androgen receptor (AR) to regulate development of male reproductive tissues, spermatogenesis, muscle mass, bone density, erythropoiesis, libido, and mood. It is also a precursor for estradiol via aromatase and for dihydrotestosterone via 5-alpha-reductase, and it is metabolized hepatically in part through cytochrome P450 enzymes including CYP3A4. Because total testosterone is sensitive to changes in SHBG, free or bioavailable testosterone is often calculated or measured when SHBG is abnormal (e.g., in obesity, insulin resistance, or aging).</p>
<p>Serum testosterone is among the most frequently ordered endocrine tests. It is central to diagnosing male hypogonadism, evaluating hyperandrogenism in women (such as in polycystic ovary syndrome), investigating delayed or precocious puberty, and monitoring testosterone replacement or anti-androgen therapy. Because levels follow a strong circadian rhythm peaking in the early morning and decline acutely with illness and stress, accurate diagnosis requires at least two fasting morning specimens measured by a reliable assay.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Ranges vary by assay and laboratory; the 264-916 ng/dL adult male range derives from CDC-harmonized assays in non-obese young men and should be applied with laboratory-specific cutoffs.</li>
<li>Total testosterone falls gradually with age (~1-2% per year after age 30-40); SHBG rises with age, so free testosterone may decline faster than total.</li>
<li>Immunoassays are less accurate at the low concentrations seen in women and hypogonadal men; liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) is preferred for these populations.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Testosterone:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Early morning sampling (circadian peak ~6-10 a.m.)</li>
<li>Resistance/high-intensity exercise (acute transient rise)</li>
<li>Anabolic-androgenic steroid or exogenous testosterone use</li>
<li>Adrenal or gonadal androgen-secreting tumors</li>
<li>Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (in women/children)</li>
<li>Correction of zinc or vitamin D deficiency</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Testosterone:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Aging (progressive Leydig-cell decline)</li>
<li>Obesity and insulin resistance (increased aromatization, lower SHBG)</li>
<li>Chronic illness, acute systemic stress, and sleep deprivation</li>
<li>Pituitary or hypothalamic disease (secondary hypogonadism)</li>
<li>Opioids, glucocorticoids, and certain antifungals/anti-androgens</li>
<li>Excess alcohol intake and chronic overtraining</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hypogonadism</strong> -- the defining biochemical feature; symptomatic men with consistently low morning testosterone meet diagnostic criteria for androgen deficiency.</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 Diabetes and insulin resistance</strong> -- low total testosterone independently predicts incident type 2 diabetes in men (meta-analysis OR ~1.5), reflecting bidirectional links between androgens and metabolic health.</li>
<li><strong>Metabolic Syndrome</strong> -- low testosterone clusters with central obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension.</li>
<li><strong>Osteoporosis</strong> -- androgen deficiency accelerates bone loss and increases fracture risk in men.</li>
<li><strong>Sarcopenia</strong> -- declining testosterone contributes to age-related loss of muscle mass and strength.</li>
<li><strong>Depression</strong> -- low testosterone is associated with depressed mood, fatigue, and reduced libido.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Polycystic Ovary Syndrome</strong> -- hyperandrogenism is a core diagnostic feature in women, often with hirsutism and oligomenorrhea.</li>
<li>Androgen-secreting adrenal or gonadal tumors, anabolic steroid use, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Diagnosing and confirming male hypogonadism (paired morning samples).</li>
<li>Titrating and monitoring testosterone replacement therapy for efficacy and safety.</li>
<li>Evaluating hyperandrogenism and treatment response in women.</li>
<li>Workup of infertility, delayed/precocious puberty, and unexplained osteoporosis in men.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Optimize body composition</strong> -- weight loss in overweight/obese men reliably raises total and free testosterone by reducing aromatization and increasing SHBG.</li>
<li><strong>Resistance training and adequate sleep</strong> -- structured strength training and 7-9 hours of quality sleep support endogenous production; chronic sleep restriction acutely lowers levels.</li>
<li><strong>Correct micronutrient deficiencies</strong> -- repletion of zinc (typically 25-30 mg/day elemental) restores testosterone in deficient men; vitamin D repletion may modestly help when deficient.</li>
<li><strong>Consider evidence-based adaptogens</strong> -- ashwagandha root extract (~300-600 mg/day standardized) modestly increases testosterone in men in randomized trials and meta-analysis.</li>
<li><strong>Limit alcohol and avoid testosterone-suppressing medications</strong> where clinically possible (e.g., chronic opioids, high-dose glucocorticoids).</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying disease</strong> -- managing diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea, and hyperprolactinemia can normalize secondary hypogonadism; testosterone replacement therapy under specialist care is indicated for confirmed symptomatic deficiency.</li>
</ol>
