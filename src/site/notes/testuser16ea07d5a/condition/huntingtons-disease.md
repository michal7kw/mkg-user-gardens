<h1>Huntington's Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Huntington's disease (HD) is an <strong>autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder</strong> caused by a <strong>CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion (&gt;36 repeats) in the HTT gene</strong> (chromosome 4p16.3), encoding the huntingtin protein. The expansion produces a mutant huntingtin protein with an elongated polyglutamine tract that misfolds, aggregates, and is toxic to neurons -- particularly the <strong>medium spiny neurons of the striatum (caudate and putamen)</strong>. HD affects approximately <strong>1-10 per 100,000</strong> people of European descent (less common in Asian and African populations) and is the most common inherited cause of chorea.</p>
<p>The clinical triad is <strong>motor dysfunction (chorea), cognitive decline, and psychiatric symptoms</strong>, typically emerging in <strong>mid-life (35-50 years)</strong>, though juvenile (&lt;20 years) and late-onset (&gt;60 years) forms exist. <strong>Chorea</strong> -- the involuntary, brief, jerky, irregular movements flowing from one body part to another -- is the motor hallmark and gives the disease its historical name (&quot;Huntington's chorea&quot;). It is accompanied by <strong>dystonia, rigidity</strong>, and <strong>bradykinesia</strong> (especially in later stages and the Westphal variant), <strong>impaired saccadic eye movements</strong> (often the earliest objective sign), and progressive <strong>dysarthria and dysphagia</strong>. <strong>Cognitive decline</strong> is of the <strong>frontal-subcortical pattern</strong> -- executive dysfunction, slowed processing speed, impaired judgment and insight, rather than the cortical signs (aphasia, agnosia) of Alzheimer disease. <strong>Psychiatric symptoms</strong> are often the earliest and most disabling: <strong>depression</strong> (in ~50%, with elevated suicide risk), <strong>anxiety, irritability, apathy</strong>, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and <strong>psychosis</strong>.</p>
<p>The <strong>CAG repeat length</strong> is the major determinant of <strong>age of onset</strong> (inverse correlation; longer repeats = earlier onset) and follows the phenomenon of <strong>anticipation</strong> -- the expansion tends to lengthen in successive generations, especially through <strong>paternal transmission</strong>, producing earlier and more severe disease in offspring. A repeat length of <strong>&gt;=40</strong> confers full penetrance; <strong>36-39</strong> is reduced penetrance (may or may not develop disease). Diagnosis is by <strong>genetic testing</strong> (CAG repeat count) in a symptomatic patient or a presymptomatic at-risk adult. Neuroimaging shows <strong>caudate atrophy</strong> (the &quot;boxcar ventricle&quot; sign on MRI) but is supportive, not diagnostic. The disease is inexorably progressive, with <strong>median survival of 15-20 years</strong> from symptom onset; the leading causes of death are <strong>aspiration pneumonia</strong> and <strong>cardiovascular disease</strong>.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>There is <strong>no disease-modifying therapy</strong> for HD. All current treatment is <strong>symptomatic and supportive</strong>, aimed at improving quality of life and managing complications. A multidisciplinary team approach (neurology, psychiatry, physical/occupational/speech therapy, genetics, social work) is essential.</p>
<p><strong>Motor symptoms:</strong></p>
<p><strong>Chorea:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Deutetrabenazine (Austedo)</strong> -- vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitor; depletes monoamines; FDA-approved 2017 for chorea in HD. Start 6 mg daily, titrate to 24-48 mg/day. Fewer depression side effects than tetrabenazine</li>
<li><strong>Tetrabenazine (Xenazine)</strong> -- VMAT2 inhibitor; FDA-approved for HD chorea. Start 12.5 mg daily, titrate to 25-100 mg/day. Risk of depression and suicidality (black box warning)</li>
<li><strong>Valbenazine (Ingrezza)</strong> -- VMAT2 inhibitor; FDA-approved for tardive dyskinesia, used off-label for HD chorea</li>
<li><strong>Antipsychotics</strong> -- <strong>olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine</strong>; useful when chorea coexists with psychosis, irritability, or behavioral symptoms. Avoid typical antipsychotics (haloperidol) except in severe refractory chorea (high parkinsonism risk)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Rigidity, dystonia, and bradykinesia (later stages):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Levodopa</strong> for parkinsonian features (limited benefit; may worsen chorea)</li>
<li><strong>Botulinum toxin</strong> injections for focal dystonia</li>
<li><strong>Baclofen, tizanidine</strong> for spasticity</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Psychiatric symptoms:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Depression</strong> -- SSRIs (sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram); SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine); mirtazapine (also helps sleep and appetite). Avoid bupropion (lowers seizure threshold; HD patients prone to falls)</li>
<li><strong>Anxiety</strong> -- SSRIs, buspirone; benzodiazepines with caution (fall risk, cognitive impairment)</li>
<li><strong>Psychosis</strong> -- atypical antipsychotics (quetiapine, clozapine; lower extrapyramidal risk than typicals)</li>
<li><strong>Irritability and impulse dyscontrol</strong> -- SSRIs, mood stabilizers (valproate, carbamazepine)</li>
<li><strong>Apathy</strong> -- no proven therapy; stimulants (methylphenidate) occasionally tried</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cognitive symptoms:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cholinesterase inhibitors</strong> (donepezil, rivastigmine) -- modest, inconsistent benefit; not standard</li>
<li><strong>Memantine</strong> -- limited evidence</li>
<li>Cognitive rehabilitation and compensatory strategies</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Supportive and multidisciplinary care:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Physical therapy</strong> -- maintains mobility, balance, strength; reduces falls</li>
<li><strong>Occupational therapy</strong> -- adaptations for daily living; home safety</li>
<li><strong>Speech and language therapy</strong> -- communication aids; swallowing evaluation and strategies; PEG placement when aspiration risk high</li>
<li><strong>Nutrition</strong> -- <strong>high-calorie diet</strong> (HD patients lose weight due to hyperkinesia and metabolic changes); supplements; <strong>PEG tube</strong> in late-stage disease for nutrition and hydration</li>
<li><strong>Sleep</strong> -- treat sleep disturbances (melatonin, trazodone); address restless legs and periodic limb movements</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Genetic counseling:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>At-risk family members</strong> (offspring have 50% risk) -- predictive genetic testing protocols per IHN/HDSA guidelines; extensive pre- and post-test counseling; testing of minors is discouraged (no treatment to alter course)</li>
<li><strong>Prenatal</strong> and <strong>preimplantation genetic diagnosis</strong> (PGD) available for affected parents wishing to have unaffected children</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Investigational disease-modifying therapies (the frontier):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)</strong> -- <strong>tominersen</strong> (Roche; targets HTT mRNA for degradation; GENERATION HD1 trial negative in 2021 but SELECT-HD trial ongoing with refined dosing); <strong>WVE-003</strong> (Wave Life Sciences; allele-selective targeting mutant HTT); <strong>AMT-130</strong> (uniQure; AAV5-delivered miRNA targeting HTT, intrastriatal injection; phase 2 ongoing)</li>
<li><strong>Zinc finger protein gene silencing</strong> -- Sangamo (preclinical)</li>
<li><strong>Stem cell therapy</strong> -- preclinical</li>
<li><strong>Neuroprotective agents</strong> -- creatine, coenzyme Q10, PBT2 (all failed in trials)</li>
</ul>
