<h1>Complex I</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase, the largest complex of the <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/pathway/electron-transport-chain/" class="internal-link">Electron Transport Chain</a> with 45 subunits and a mass of nearly 1 MDa. It catalyzes the first step of <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/pathway/oxidative-phosphorylation/" class="internal-link">Oxidative Phosphorylation</a> by oxidizing <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/metabolite/nadh/" class="internal-link">NADH</a> and transferring electrons to ubiquinone (<a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/supplement/coenzyme-q10/" class="internal-link">Coenzyme Q10</a>), while pumping protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Located in the inner membrane of the <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/cellularcomponent/mitochondria/" class="internal-link">Mitochondria</a>, Complex I is the principal entry point for reducing equivalents generated by the <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/pathway/citric-acid-cycle/" class="internal-link">Citric Acid Cycle</a>, <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/pathway/glycolysis/" class="internal-link">Glycolysis</a> (via shuttle systems), and <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/pathway/beta-oxidation/" class="internal-link">Beta-Oxidation</a>.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Complex I has a characteristic L-shaped structure with a hydrophilic arm protruding into the matrix and a membrane-embedded hydrophobic arm. The reaction proceeds through three coupled steps:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>NADH oxidation.</strong> <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/metabolite/nadh/" class="internal-link">NADH</a> binds at the flavin mononucleotide (FMN) prosthetic group in the matrix arm and is oxidized to <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/metabolite/nad/" class="internal-link">NAD+</a>, delivering two electrons to FMN as it becomes FMNH2.</li>
<li><strong>Electron relay.</strong> Electrons pass one at a time through a chain of seven iron-sulfur (Fe-S) clusters spanning ~95 Å toward the membrane domain.</li>
<li><strong>Ubiquinone reduction.</strong> At the interface of the two arms, ubiquinone (<a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/supplement/coenzyme-q10/" class="internal-link">Coenzyme Q10</a>) is reduced to ubiquinol, accepting two electrons and two protons.</li>
</ol>

<p>The free energy released during electron transfer drives a conformational change that pumps approximately 4 protons (H⁺) from the matrix into the intermembrane space, contributing to the proton-motive force later used by <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/protein/atp-synthase/" class="internal-link">ATP Synthase</a>.</p>
<h2>Role in the Electron Transport Chain</h2>
<p>Complex I is the first of four membrane complexes that establish the electrochemical proton gradient. It feeds electrons derived from <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/metabolite/nadh/" class="internal-link">NADH</a> into the mobile ubiquinone pool, which carries them to <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/protein/complex-iii/" class="internal-link">Complex III</a>. Because Complex I actively pumps protons, electrons entering here yield more <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> than those entering downstream via <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/protein/complex-ii/" class="internal-link">Complex II</a>. The reduced <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/supplement/coenzyme-q10/" class="internal-link">Coenzyme Q10</a> produced here is the convergence point for electrons from both Complex I and Complex II, linking <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/metabolite/nadh/" class="internal-link">NADH</a>- and <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/metabolite/fadh2/" class="internal-link">FADH2</a>-derived reducing power into a single pool.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mitochondrial disease.</strong> Complex I deficiency is the most common defect of the respiratory chain and underlies disorders such as Leigh syndrome and MELAS, presenting with lactic acidosis, neurodegeneration, and exercise intolerance.</li>
<li><strong>Riboflavin dependence.</strong> The FMN cofactor is derived from <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/supplement/riboflavin/" class="internal-link">Riboflavin</a> (vitamin B2); supplementation can partially rescue certain riboflavin-responsive Complex I deficiencies.</li>
<li><strong>CoQ10 status.</strong> Adequate <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/supplement/coenzyme-q10/" class="internal-link">Coenzyme Q10</a> is required for electron handoff; CoQ10 depletion (e.g., statin therapy) can impair flux through Complex I and downstream complexes.</li>
<li><strong>ROS generation.</strong> Complex I is a major site of electron leak to oxygen, producing <a href="/garden/itest891fdda7/metabolite/reactive-oxygen-species/" class="internal-link">Reactive Oxygen Species</a> implicated in aging and neurodegenerative disease. Complex I is also the target of the parkinsonism-inducing toxin rotenone.</li>
</ul>
