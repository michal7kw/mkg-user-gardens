<h1>Rhabdomyolysis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Rhabdomyolysis is characterized by the <strong>rapid breakdown of skeletal muscle tissue</strong>, releasing toxic intracellular contents -- principally <strong>myoglobin, creatine kinase (CK), potassium, phosphate, and urate</strong> -- into the systemic circulation. The hallmark laboratory finding is a <strong>markedly elevated serum creatine kinase</strong> (&gt;5 times the upper limit of normal, typically &gt;5,000 U/L, sometimes &gt;100,000 U/L).</p>
<p>The clinical consequences stem from these released contents:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Myoglobin</strong> -- filtered by glomeruli but toxic to renal tubular cells; causes pigmented casts, tubular obstruction, and <strong>acute kidney injury</strong> (the most feared complication)</li>
<li><strong>Potassium</strong> -- hyperkalemia can cause life-threatening <strong>cardiac arrhythmias</strong></li>
<li><strong>Phosphate</strong> -- hyperphosphatemia causes secondary hypocalcemia</li>
<li><strong>CK</strong> -- not directly toxic but is the primary diagnostic and monitoring marker</li>
</ul>
<p>The classic triad of <strong>muscle pain, weakness, and dark (cola-colored) urine</strong> is present in only a minority of cases; many patients present with non-specific symptoms (fatigue, malaise, nausea) or are identified incidentally on laboratory testing.</p>
<p><strong>Common causes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Trauma:</strong> crush injuries, prolonged immobilization (falls, unconsciousness), earthquakes</li>
<li><strong>Exercise:</strong> especially unaccustomed strenuous exercise, endurance events, eccentric exercise</li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> statins (particularly with interacting drugs like macrolides, fibrates, cyclosporine), antipsychotics (NMS), anesthetics (malignant hyperthermia)</li>
<li><strong>Substances:</strong> alcohol, cocaine, MDMA, amphetamines</li>
<li><strong>Infections:</strong> influenza, coxsackievirus, legionella, severe bacterial sepsis</li>
<li><strong>Electrolyte abnormalities:</strong> hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia</li>
<li><strong>Genetic metabolic myopathies:</strong> McArdle disease, CPT-II deficiency</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Muscle pain, tenderness, stiffness</strong> (may be diffuse or localized to affected muscle groups)</li>
<li><strong>Weakness</strong> (variable severity)</li>
<li><strong>Dark, tea-colored or cola-colored urine</strong> (myoglobinuria; pathognomonic)</li>
<li><strong>Muscle swelling</strong> (especially in compartment syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Fatigue, malaise</strong></li>
<li><strong>Nausea, vomiting</strong></li>
<li><strong>Confusion</strong> (uremia, electrolyte disturbances)</li>
<li><strong>Symptoms of the underlying cause</strong> (trauma, infection, overdose)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Severe cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Oliguria/anuria</strong> (AKI)</li>
<li><strong>Cardiac arrhythmias</strong> (hyperkalemia)</li>
<li><strong>Hypotension</strong> (volume depletion, distributive shock)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p><strong>Creatine kinase (CK)</strong> -- the primary diagnostic marker:</p>
<ul>
<li>Diagnostic threshold: &gt;5x ULN (~1,000 U/L in most labs)</li>
<li>Typical range: 5,000-100,000+ U/L</li>
<li>Peak at 24-72 hours; monitor for trends</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Myoglobin</strong> -- appears in blood and urine early; clears faster than CK:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Urine dipstick</strong> positive for &quot;blood&quot; but microscopy shows <strong>no RBCs</strong> (false-positive heme from myoglobin)</li>
<li><strong>Serum myoglobin</strong> elevated</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Other labs:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Potassium</strong> -- elevated (from muscle cell release)</li>
<li><strong>Phosphate</strong> -- elevated</li>
<li><strong>Calcium</strong> -- low (early; may rebound later)</li>
<li><strong>Uric acid</strong> -- elevated</li>
<li><strong>Creatinine</strong> -- elevated (AKI; creatinine rises faster than BUN in rhabdomyolysis)</li>
<li><strong>AST/ALT</strong> -- elevated (released from muscle, not liver)</li>
<li><strong>Urinalysis:</strong> pigmented granular casts; dipstick positive for blood without RBCs</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>ECG</strong> -- assess for hyperkalemia (peaked T waves, wide QRS)</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (the most important intervention):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Normal saline</strong> 1-2 L bolus, then 200-500 mL/hr to maintain urine output 200-300 mL/hr</li>
<li>Goal: flush myoglobin through the renal tubules before AKI develops</li>
<li>Continue until CK is &lt;5,000-10,000 U/L and urine clears</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Electrolyte management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hyperkalemia:</strong> calcium gluconate (cardiac membrane stabilization), insulin/dextrose, beta-2 agonists, sodium polystyrene sulfonate; <strong>avoid</strong> potassium-containing IV fluids</li>
<li><strong>Hypocalcemia:</strong> do NOT treat unless symptomatic (tetany, seizures); calcium will rebound as calcium-phosphate complexes dissociate</li>
<li><strong>Hyperphosphatemia:</strong> phosphate binders; dialysis if severe</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Urine alkalinization (sodium bicarbonate):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Controversial; some evidence for reducing myoglobin-induced tubular injury</li>
<li>Target urine pH &gt;6.5; monitor serum bicarbonate and calcium</li>
<li>Use if serum pH &lt;7.1 or concurrent metabolic acidosis</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Treat underlying cause:</strong> discontinue offending drugs, treat infections, fasciotomy for compartment syndrome</p>
<p><strong>Dialysis:</strong> indicated for severe AKI with refractory hyperkalemia, acidosis, volume overload, or uremia</p>
