<h1>Estrogen Contraceptives</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Estrogen contraceptives — more precisely <strong>combined hormonal contraceptives (CHCs)</strong>, most commonly <strong>combined oral contraceptives (COCs)</strong> — contain a synthetic <strong>estrogen</strong> (usually ethinylestradiol) together with a <strong>progestin</strong>. They are among the most widely used reversible contraceptive methods worldwide and are also prescribed for non-contraceptive benefits including menstrual cycle regulation, dysmenorrhea, acne, and premenstrual symptom control.</p>
<p>Their defining clinical distinction from progestin-only methods is the <strong>estrogen component</strong>, which provides cycle control and contributes to efficacy but is responsible for the characteristic increase in <strong>thromboembolic risk</strong>. This trade-off shapes prescribing: combined methods are highly effective and well tolerated by most users but are contraindicated in people with elevated thrombotic or arterial risk.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>The combination suppresses gonadotropin secretion through negative feedback on the <strong>hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis</strong>. By lowering follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH), the mid-cycle LH surge is abolished and <strong>ovulation is inhibited</strong>. The progestin additionally thickens cervical mucus (impeding sperm penetration) and thins the endometrium (reducing implantation receptivity). The estrogen primarily stabilizes the endometrium for cycle control and augments ovulation suppression.</p>
<h2>Clinical Uses</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Contraception</strong>: highly effective with correct, consistent use</li>
<li><strong>Menstrual disorders</strong>: regulation of irregular cycles, reduction of menorrhagia and dysmenorrhea</li>
<li><strong>Dermatologic</strong>: acne improvement via reduced ovarian androgen production</li>
<li><strong>Premenstrual symptoms</strong>: continuous or extended-cycle regimens</li>
</ul>
<h2>Safety and Risk Profile</h2>
<p>The estrogen component increases the relative risk of <strong>venous thromboembolism (VTE)</strong> roughly two- to fourfold over baseline, with absolute risk remaining low in healthy young non-smokers but rising with thrombophilia, obesity, immobilization, and the postpartum period. The estrogen-driven prothrombotic shift (increased coagulation factors, reduced anticoagulant proteins) also underlies an increased risk of <strong>arterial events</strong> — ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction — particularly in smokers &gt;= 35 years, those with hypertension, or migraine with aura. These risks define the principal contraindications.</p>
