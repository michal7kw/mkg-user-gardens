<h1>Chronic Lung Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Chronic lung disease is an umbrella term for a heterogeneous group of progressive, long-term respiratory disorders that persistently impair breathing, gas exchange, and lung mechanics. It encompasses <strong>obstructive</strong> diseases — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic bronchitis, emphysema, and persistent asthma — characterized by reduced expiratory airflow, as well as <strong>restrictive</strong> diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis, in which the lungs become stiff and reduced in volume. Although the underlying pathology differs, these conditions share a common trajectory of irreversible or only partially reversible loss of pulmonary function over time.</p>
<p>Clinically, chronic lung disease presents with dyspnea (often initially exertional and progressing to breathlessness at rest), chronic cough, wheezing, chest tightness, sputum production, and fatigue. The disease course is typically slow and progressive but is punctuated by acute exacerbations — episodes of worsening symptoms, frequently triggered by viral or bacterial infections or pollution — that drive accelerated lung-function decline, hospitalization, and increased mortality. Because the lungs are central to oxygen delivery, advanced disease produces systemic consequences including hypoxemia, pulmonary hypertension, right-heart strain (cor pulmonale), muscle wasting, and reduced exercise capacity.</p>
<p>Chronic lung disease, dominated by COPD, is one of the leading global causes of death and disability, with the burden falling heavily on low- and middle-income countries. Its principal drivers are modifiable environmental exposures — above all tobacco smoke, but also ambient and household air pollution and occupational dusts and chemicals — which makes prevention through exposure reduction a cornerstone of public health strategy.</p>
<h2>Pathophysiology</h2>
<p>The defining biological processes are <strong>chronic airway inflammation</strong>, <strong>oxidative stress</strong>, and <strong>tissue remodeling</strong>. Inhaled irritants recruit neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes that release proteases and reactive oxygen species, overwhelming endogenous antioxidant and antiprotease defenses (notably alpha-1 antitrypsin). The resulting protease–antiprotease and oxidant–antioxidant imbalance destroys alveolar walls (emphysema), thickens and narrows small airways, and promotes mucus hypersecretion. Depletion of glutathione and other antioxidants amplifies epithelial injury, providing the rationale for antioxidant/mucolytic agents such as N-acetylcysteine. Persistent inflammation also &quot;spills over&quot; systemically, raising circulating inflammatory markers and contributing to comorbid cardiovascular and metabolic disease.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Severity is graded primarily by spirometry: a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.70 defines persistent airflow obstruction, with FEV1 percent-predicted staging disease severity. Reduced diffusing capacity (DLCO) and chest imaging further characterize the phenotype. Exacerbation frequency and symptom burden, rather than lung function alone, are key prognostic determinants and guide pharmacologic escalation. Systemic inflammatory biomarkers — including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and fibrinogen — are associated with worse prognosis and cardiovascular risk, while secondary polycythemia (elevated hemoglobin/hematocrit) can develop in response to chronic hypoxemia.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Care is multimodal. <strong>Smoking cessation</strong> is the single most effective intervention to slow progression. <strong>Inhaled bronchodilators</strong> (long-acting beta-agonists and antimuscarinics) and, in selected patients, <strong>inhaled corticosteroids</strong> reduce symptoms and exacerbations. <strong>Pulmonary rehabilitation</strong> — structured exercise and education — improves exercise tolerance and quality of life. <strong>Long-term oxygen therapy</strong> prolongs survival in chronic hypoxemia. Adjuncts include <strong>vaccination</strong> to prevent infective exacerbations and <strong>antioxidant/mucolytic therapy</strong> (e.g., N-acetylcysteine), which evidence suggests can reduce exacerbations, particularly at higher doses. Nutritional optimization, including correction of vitamin D deficiency, and management of comorbidities round out comprehensive care.</p>
