<h1>Crohns Disease</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Crohn's disease is a chronic, relapsing-remitting inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) that can involve any segment of the gastrointestinal tract from mouth to anus, although the terminal ileum and proximal colon are most frequently affected. Its defining histopathological feature is <strong>transmural inflammation</strong> — extending through the full thickness of the bowel wall — distributed in a discontinuous, &quot;skip lesion&quot; pattern that distinguishes it from the continuous, mucosa-limited inflammation of ulcerative colitis.</p>
<p>The pathogenesis reflects a dysregulated innate and adaptive immune response to the commensal gut microbiota in a genetically susceptible host. Variants in genes such as NOD2/CARD15 impair bacterial sensing and autophagy, while environmental triggers — especially cigarette smoking, a Westernized diet, and early antibiotic exposure — modulate disease onset and course. The resulting chronic transmural inflammation drives the characteristic structural complications: fibrostenotic strictures, penetrating fistulas, and abscesses.</p>
<p>Clinically, Crohn's disease matters because of its progressive, often disabling course and its substantial extraintestinal and nutritional burden. Many patients ultimately require surgery, and long-standing colonic involvement raises colorectal cancer risk. Modern &quot;treat-to-target&quot; management aims for endoscopic mucosal healing rather than symptom control alone, leveraging immunomodulators and biologics to alter the natural history of the disease.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Symptoms depend on disease location, extent, and phenotype (inflammatory, stricturing, or penetrating). Common features include chronic abdominal pain (classically right lower quadrant with ileal disease), chronic diarrhea that is often non-bloody, weight loss, fatigue, and low-grade fever. Perianal disease — fistulas, fissures, and abscesses — is a hallmark and may precede luminal symptoms. Extraintestinal manifestations affecting joints, eyes, and skin are common.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>There is no single diagnostic test; diagnosis integrates clinical, endoscopic, histological, radiological, and laboratory findings. Ileocolonoscopy with biopsy is central, showing focal, segmental, transmural inflammation, with non-caseating granulomas in a minority of patients. Cross-sectional enterography (MR or CT) assesses small-bowel involvement and complications. Fecal calprotectin and serum CRP/ESR support disease activity assessment and monitoring.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The strongest risk factors are a family history of IBD and susceptibility variants (notably NOD2/CARD15). Cigarette smoking is the most important modifiable risk factor and worsens disease behavior. A Westernized diet, urban living, early-life antibiotic exposure, and altered gut microbiota composition all contribute.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is individualized by disease severity and phenotype. Smoking cessation is essential. Corticosteroids (including ileal-release budesonide) treat flares but are not for maintenance. Immunomodulators (azathioprine, methotrexate) and biologics (anti-TNF agents such as infliximab and adalimumab, as well as anti-integrin and anti-IL-12/23 agents) are used for moderate-to-severe disease, increasingly early in a treat-to-target strategy. Surgery is reserved for strictures, fistulas, abscesses, or medically refractory disease and is not curative.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Transmural inflammation produces strictures with bowel obstruction, fistulas (entero-enteric, enterocutaneous, perianal), and abscesses. Chronic inflammation and reduced absorptive surface cause malabsorption, micronutrient deficiencies (iron, B12), and anemia. Long-standing colonic disease increases colorectal cancer risk, and severe colitis can rarely progress to toxic megacolon.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Crohn's disease follows a chronic, unpredictable relapsing course. While most patients achieve good quality of life with modern therapy, a significant proportion develop complications requiring surgery over their lifetime. Early effective therapy aimed at mucosal healing improves long-term outcomes and may reduce the need for surgery.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Crohn's disease is most prevalent in North America and Northern Europe, with incidence rising in newly industrialized countries undergoing Westernization. It typically presents in adolescence or early adulthood, with a smaller second peak in later life, and affects both sexes roughly equally.</p>
