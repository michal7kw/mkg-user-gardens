<h1>Bowel Obstruction and Perforation</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Bowel obstruction and perforation are <strong>surgical emergencies</strong> of the gastrointestinal tract that share the risk of <strong>peritonitis, sepsis, and death</strong> if not promptly recognized and treated. <strong>Obstruction</strong> is a mechanical or functional blockage of the intestinal lumen; <strong>perforation</strong> is a full-thickness tear in the bowel wall that spills contaminated contents into the peritoneal cavity. The two often coexist -- strangulated obstruction progresses to ischemia and perforation, and perforation itself can present as obstruction from paralytic ileus.</p>
<p><strong>Small bowel obstruction (SBO)</strong> accounts for ~75% of all bowel obstructions. The most common cause in developed countries is <strong>adhesions</strong> (~60-75% of SBO), typically from prior abdominal or pelvic surgery. Other causes include <strong>hernias</strong> (~15-20%), <strong>malignancy</strong> (~10%, usually metastatic), <strong>Crohn disease</strong> strictures, <strong>intussusception</strong>, <strong>volvulus</strong>, and <strong>gallstone ileus</strong> (a gallstone erodes into the duodenum, obstructing the terminal ileum). <strong>Large bowel obstruction (LBO)</strong> is most commonly caused by <strong>colorectal cancer</strong> (~60%), followed by <strong>volvulus</strong> (especially sigmoid in elderly), <strong>diverticular stricture</strong>, and <strong>fecal impaction</strong>.</p>
<p>The pathophysiology of obstruction involves <strong>proximal dilation</strong> from accumulated gas and fluid, <strong>bowel wall edema</strong>, and -- if strangulation develops -- <strong>ischemia, necrosis, and perforation</strong>. Strangulation typically occurs with <strong>closed-loop obstruction</strong> (e.g., incarcerated hernia, volvulus) where the vascular supply is compromised; mortality rises from <strong>3-5% for simple obstruction to 20-30% with strangulation</strong>. <strong>Perforation</strong> causes <strong>chemical peritonitis</strong> initially (gastric acid, bile, pancreatic enzymes) followed by <strong>bacterial peritonitis</strong> within hours, progressing to <strong>septic shock</strong>. Free air on imaging (pneumoperitoneum) is the radiographic hallmark of perforation.</p>
<p>The clinical presentation varies by site and cause. <strong>SBO</strong> presents with <strong>crampy abdominal pain</strong> (colicky, every 4-5 minutes for proximal, longer intervals for distal), <strong>vomiting</strong> (early and bilious in proximal; late and feculent in distal), <strong>abdominal distension</strong> (more pronounced in distal), and <strong>constipation/obstipation</strong> (late sign). <strong>LBO</strong> presents with <strong>more gradual onset of distension, less vomiting</strong>, and <strong>absolute constipation</strong>. <strong>Perforation</strong> produces sudden <strong>severe generalized abdominal pain</strong>, <strong>rigidity and guarding</strong>, <strong>rebound tenderness</strong>, <strong>hypotension</strong>, and <strong>fever</strong>. Diagnosis relies on <strong>upright CXR</strong> (free air under diaphragm for perforation), <strong>CT abdomen with contrast</strong> (site, cause, ischemia, perforation), and clinical exam.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Immediate management (all patients):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>NPO</strong> (nothing by mouth)</li>
<li><strong>Nasogastric tube</strong> decompression (relieves vomiting, reduces distension, monitors output)</li>
<li><strong>IV fluid resuscitation</strong> (lactated Ringer's; correct hypovolemia, electrolytes -- potassium, magnesium)</li>
<li><strong>Foley catheter</strong> to monitor urine output</li>
<li><strong>Broad-spectrum antibiotics</strong> if perforation, ischemia, or peritonitis suspected (piperacillin-tazobactam or ceftriaxone + metronidazole)</li>
<li><strong>Pain management</strong> (opioids)</li>
<li><strong>Surgical consultation</strong> immediately</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Differentiate simple vs strangulated/perforated (this drives management):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Conservative management (uncomplicated adhesive SBO):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Indicated for <strong>partial SBO</strong> without signs of strangulation</li>
<li>~<strong>70-80% resolve within 48-72 hours</strong> with NG decompression and fluids</li>
<li><strong>Water-soluble contrast</strong> (gastrografin) given via NG tube -- both diagnostic (predicts need for surgery) and therapeutic (osmotic effect promotes resolution)</li>
<li><strong>Surgery if no resolution in 48-72 hours</strong> or if signs of strangulation develop</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Surgical management (definitive for most):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Indications for urgent surgery:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Complete obstruction</strong> (no gas in colon on imaging)</li>
<li><strong>Strangulation</strong> -- fever, leukocytosis, tachycardia, severe pain, peritonitis, elevated lactate, free fluid</li>
<li><strong>Perforation</strong> -- free air, peritonitis, sepsis</li>
<li><strong>Closed-loop obstruction</strong> (incarcerated hernia, volvulus)</li>
<li><strong>Failure of conservative management</strong> (no improvement in 48-72 hours)</li>
<li><strong>Malignancy, hernia, volvulus, or intussusception as cause</strong> (these do not resolve with conservative care)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Surgical procedures:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adhesiolysis</strong> for adhesive SBO</li>
<li><strong>Bowel resection with primary anastomosis</strong> for gangrene, tumor, or ischemia (if patient stable and bowel prepared)</li>
<li><strong>Bowel resection with stoma</strong> (colostomy or ileostomy) if primary anastomosis unsafe (peritonitis, hemodynamic instability, unprepared bowel)</li>
<li><strong>Hernia repair</strong> (with or without mesh)</li>
<li><strong>Endoscopic decompression</strong> for sigmoid volvulus (rectal tube, colonoscopic detorsion) followed by elective sigmoid colectomy</li>
<li><strong>Hartmann's procedure</strong> (sigmoid resection with end colostomy) for perforated sigmoid diverticulitis or obstructing rectosigmoid cancer</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Specific causes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Colorectal cancer</strong> -- resection with primary anastomosis or stoma; bridge to surgery with self-expanding metal stent in selected LBO cases</li>
<li><strong>Diverticular perforation</strong> -- Hartmann's procedure or primary anastomosis with diverting ileostomy</li>
<li><strong>Volvulus</strong> -- endoscopic detorsion first (sigmoid); surgery (cecal) for cecal volvulus</li>
<li><strong>Intussusception</strong> -- pneumatic reduction in children; surgery in adults (always -- look for lead point tumor)</li>
<li><strong>Incarcerated hernia</strong> -- manual reduction if early and no strangulation; otherwise surgical repair</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Post-operative care and complications:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ICU monitoring</strong> for severe cases</li>
<li><strong>Intra-abdominal abscess</strong> -- percutaneous drainage</li>
<li><strong>Wound infection and dehiscence</strong> -- more common with peritonitis</li>
<li><strong>Anastomotic leak</strong> -- usually presents days 5-7 with fever, pain, sepsis</li>
<li><strong>Adhesions</strong> -- leading cause of recurrent SBO; barrier products (Seprafilm) at initial surgery reduce recurrence</li>
<li><strong>Short bowel syndrome</strong> after extensive resection -- requires long-term parenteral nutrition, teduglutide</li>
</ul>
