<h1>Anaphylaxis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction that develops rapidly - usually within minutes to a couple of hours of exposure to a trigger - and can progress to airway obstruction, circulatory shock, and death. It is most often immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated, in which prior sensitization to an allergen (such as a food, drug, or insect venom) leads to cross-linking of IgE bound to mast cells and basophils on re-exposure. This triggers explosive degranulation and release of preformed and newly synthesized mediators - histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and platelet-activating factor - that cause vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchoconstriction, and increased mucus secretion. Non-IgE (direct mast-cell-activating) and idiopathic mechanisms also occur.</p>
<p>The cardinal feature is the simultaneous involvement of multiple organ systems. Skin and mucosal signs (urticaria, flushing, angioedema) are present in the majority of episodes, but their absence does not exclude the diagnosis - some of the most dangerous reactions present primarily as cardiovascular collapse or as isolated bronchospasm, particularly in patients with asthma. The combination of rapid onset, multi-system involvement, and a plausible exposure is what defines the syndrome clinically.</p>
<p>Anaphylaxis matters because it is both common enough to be encountered in any clinical setting and reversible if treated promptly - yet remains a cause of preventable death when epinephrine is delayed. International guidelines (WAO, EAACI, and national bodies) are unanimous that intramuscular epinephrine is the first-line treatment and that antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjuncts only. Because the diagnosis is clinical, treatment must never be postponed to obtain confirmatory tests.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Onset is typically abrupt, and the spectrum ranges from mild cutaneous reactions to fulminant shock:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cutaneous/mucosal</strong> (~80-90% of episodes): generalized urticaria, pruritus, flushing, and angioedema of the lips, tongue, or face</li>
<li><strong>Respiratory</strong>: throat tightness, hoarseness, stridor (laryngeal edema), wheeze, cough, dyspnea, and hypoxia</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular</strong>: hypotension, tachycardia (or paradoxical bradycardia), dizziness, syncope, and frank distributive shock</li>
<li><strong>Gastrointestinal</strong>: cramping abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea</li>
<li><strong>Neurologic/other</strong>: anxiety, a sense of impending doom, confusion, and loss of consciousness</li>
</ul>
<p>A <strong>biphasic reaction</strong> - recurrence of symptoms hours after apparent resolution without re-exposure - occurs in a minority of patients and justifies a period of post-treatment observation.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Anaphylaxis is diagnosed clinically using widely adopted criteria: acute onset with skin/mucosal involvement plus either respiratory compromise or reduced blood pressure; or two or more rapidly developing organ-system features after likely allergen exposure; or hypotension after exposure to a known allergen. A serum <strong>tryptase</strong> measurement drawn 1-3 hours after symptom onset, compared with a later baseline, can support the diagnosis (especially in venom- and drug-induced reactions) and, if persistently elevated, prompts evaluation for an underlying clonal mast cell disorder. Allergen-specific IgE testing identifies culprit triggers during follow-up but has no role in the acute event.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>A history of prior anaphylaxis or any established food, drug, or insect-venom allergy is the strongest risk factor. Coexisting asthma - particularly poorly controlled disease - predisposes to severe respiratory reactions and fatalities. Underlying mast cell disorders and elevated baseline tryptase (including hereditary alpha-tryptasemia) increase both the likelihood and severity of reactions. Cofactors such as exercise, alcohol, NSAIDs, acute infection, and menstruation can lower the reaction threshold, while concurrent beta-blocker or ACE-inhibitor therapy can make reactions more refractory to treatment.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Intramuscular epinephrine</strong> delivered into the anterolateral thigh is the immediate, first-line, life-saving treatment and may be repeated every 5-15 minutes as needed. Patients should be placed supine with legs elevated (unless dyspneic or vomiting), given high-flow oxygen, and resuscitated with intravenous fluids for hypotension. Antihistamines relieve cutaneous symptoms and corticosteroids are sometimes used to attenuate biphasic reactions, but neither replaces epinephrine. After stabilization, patients are observed for biphasic reactions and discharged with an epinephrine auto-injector, an action plan, and referral to an allergist for trigger identification and long-term management.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The principal acute complications are respiratory failure from laryngeal edema or severe bronchospasm and cardiovascular collapse leading to arrhythmia or cardiac arrest. Prolonged shock can cause hypoxic-ischemic brain injury and multi-organ damage. Biphasic and protracted/refractory reactions complicate management, and death - though uncommon relative to incidence - occurs most often when epinephrine is delayed, underdosed, or withheld.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>With prompt recognition and timely intramuscular epinephrine, the great majority of episodes resolve fully without sequelae. Prognosis worsens with delayed treatment, severe or refractory reactions, and the presence of comorbid asthma or cardiovascular disease. Recurrence risk persists for life in patients with a fixed allergen, making sustained avoidance, auto-injector availability, and specialist follow-up essential.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Anaphylaxis is increasingly recognized, with an estimated lifetime prevalence of roughly 0.3-5% depending on the population and case definition, and incidence appears to be rising, particularly food-triggered reactions in children. Common triggers vary by age - foods predominate in children, while drugs and insect venom are more frequent in adults. Despite its severity, case-fatality rates are low when treatment is timely.</p>
