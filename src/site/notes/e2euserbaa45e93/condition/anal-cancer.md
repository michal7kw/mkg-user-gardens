<h1>Anal Cancer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Anal cancer is a malignancy arising in the anal canal or perianal skin, of which squamous cell carcinoma is by far the most common histological type. Like cervical cancer, the great majority of cases are driven by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus, especially HPV-16, which produces oncoproteins that disable tumor-suppressor pathways and transform anal squamous epithelium. The disease evolves through precursor lesions — anal squamous intraepithelial lesions, with high-grade lesions (HSIL/AIN 2-3) representing the immediate precancerous stage — providing a target for screening and preventive treatment in high-risk populations.</p>
<p>Although uncommon relative to colorectal cancer, the incidence of anal cancer has been rising, particularly among people living with HIV, men who have sex with men, and others with chronic immunosuppression, in whom HPV persistence is more likely. The anatomical and biological parallels with cervical neoplasia have shaped both prevention (HPV vaccination) and secondary prevention strategies, including high-resolution anoscopy to detect and treat high-grade precursor lesions before invasion occurs.</p>
<p>A defining clinical feature of anal cancer is that, unlike most gastrointestinal cancers, primary treatment is organ-preserving chemoradiotherapy rather than surgery. The landmark recognition that combined chemoradiation could cure most patients while avoiding a permanent colostomy transformed management, with surgery reserved for salvage of residual or recurrent disease. This makes accurate staging and multidisciplinary care central to outcomes.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Patients typically present with anal bleeding, pain, and a palpable mass or lump, often initially attributed to hemorrhoids, which can delay diagnosis. Other features include pruritus, discharge, a non-healing ulcer, change in bowel habits, and tenesmus. Spread to inguinal lymph nodes may produce palpable groin lymphadenopathy.</p>

<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant risk factor is persistent high-risk HPV infection, amplified by HIV infection and other immunosuppression, receptive anal intercourse, multiple sexual partners, smoking, and a prior history of anal high-grade intraepithelial lesions or other HPV-related anogenital neoplasia.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Standard primary treatment is concurrent chemoradiotherapy, classically combining mitomycin or cisplatin with 5-fluorouracil and external-beam radiation, which achieves cure in the majority of patients while preserving anorectal function. Surgical salvage by abdominoperineal resection is reserved for residual or recurrent disease, and local excision may suffice for very small, early lesions. Treating high-grade precursor lesions and HPV vaccination are the cornerstones of prevention.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Complications include local persistence or recurrence after chemoradiation, acute and late radiation toxicity (proctitis, dermatitis, pelvic fibrosis, and sexual dysfunction), distant metastasis, and — when salvage surgery is required — the need for a permanent colostomy.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>With chemoradiotherapy, early-stage anal cancer has a favorable prognosis and high rates of locoregional control and survival. Outcomes decline with larger tumors, nodal involvement, and distant spread, and immunosuppressed patients may have a more aggressive course and higher recurrence risk.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Anal cancer is relatively rare in the general population but its incidence has increased over recent decades, with markedly higher rates among people with HIV and other immunocompromised groups. The strong HPV association means that vaccination and targeted screening of high-risk populations are expected to reduce its future burden.</p>
