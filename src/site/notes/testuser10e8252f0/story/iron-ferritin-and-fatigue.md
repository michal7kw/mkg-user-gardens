<h1>Iron, Ferritin and Fatigue</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Iron is the atom at the center of <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/labtest/hemoglobin/" class="internal-link">Hemoglobin</a>, the molecule that carries oxygen in your red blood cells, and it powers energy production inside every mitochondrion. Two markers tell most of the story: <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/labtest/ferritin/" class="internal-link">Ferritin</a>, which reflects your <strong>stored</strong> iron, and <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/biomarker/serum-iron/" class="internal-link">Serum Iron</a> (read with transferrin/TIBC), which reflects iron <strong>in transit</strong>. Because ferritin falls first, iron deficiency can drain your energy long before hemoglobin drops and true <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/condition/anemia/" class="internal-link">Anemia</a> appears — which is why fatigue with a &quot;normal&quot; blood count often traces back to a low ferritin.</p>
<h2>The Story</h2>
<p>Your body has no active route to excrete excess iron, so it guards intake tightly and banks the surplus as ferritin, mostly in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. When intake or absorption falls short — or when losses rise (menstruation, GI bleeding, blood donation, endurance training) — you draw down the ferritin reserve first. Serum iron and transferrin saturation drop next, and only when stores are exhausted does hemoglobin fall into frank <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/condition/iron-deficiency-anemia/" class="internal-link">Iron Deficiency Anemia</a> (PMID: 25946282). This staging is the key to interpretation: a ferritin in the low-normal range with symptoms of fatigue, poor exercise tolerance, hair thinning, or restless legs can represent <strong>iron-deficiency without anemia</strong>, a genuine and treatable state (PMID: 33285139).</p>
<p>The complication is that ferritin is also an acute-phase protein — it rises with inflammation and infection. So a &quot;normal&quot; ferritin during active inflammation can mask real depletion; reading it alongside <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/biomarker/hs-crp/" class="internal-link">hs-CRP</a> and transferrin saturation avoids that trap. On the other end, high ferritin without inflammation can signal iron overload (including hereditary hemochromatosis), which is its own problem — so iron is a marker where both ends of the range matter (PMID: 26314490).</p>
<p>Repletion is straightforward but deserves care. Dietary iron comes in two forms: heme (meat, better absorbed) and non-heme (plants, absorption enhanced by vitamin C and blunted by tea, coffee, and calcium taken at the same time). Supplemental iron works but commonly causes GI upset; alternate-day dosing improves absorption and tolerability. Because the body cannot dump excess, iron should be supplemented to correct a <em>documented</em> deficiency, not taken blindly.</p>
<h2>What This Means For You</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Read ferritin and serum iron together.</strong> Low ferritin = depleted stores (the earliest, most sensitive sign). Low transferrin saturation confirms it. Hemoglobin tells you whether it has progressed to anemia.</li>
<li><strong>Fatigue with a normal blood count?</strong> Check ferritin — iron-deficiency without anemia is real and treatable.</li>
<li><strong>Mind the inflammation caveat:</strong> a normal ferritin during illness or high <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/biomarker/hs-crp/" class="internal-link">hs-CRP</a> can hide true depletion.</li>
<li><strong>What lowers iron stores:</strong> menstruation, GI blood loss, frequent blood donation, endurance training, low-meat or restrictive diets, and impaired absorption (celiac disease, acid suppression).</li>
<li><strong>Repletion:</strong> dietary <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/supplement/iron/" class="internal-link">Iron</a> plus <a href="/garden/testuser10e8252f0/supplement/vitamin-c/" class="internal-link">Vitamin C</a> to boost non-heme absorption; supplement only a documented deficiency (the body cannot excrete excess iron), and separate iron from tea/coffee/calcium.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>Ferritin reflects stored iron and falls first; serum iron/transferrin saturation fall next; hemoglobin drops last.</li>
<li>Iron-deficiency <em>without</em> anemia is a real cause of fatigue with a normal blood count — check ferritin.</li>
<li>Ferritin rises with inflammation, so read it alongside hs-CRP and transferrin saturation to avoid a false &quot;normal.&quot;</li>
<li>Vitamin C boosts non-heme iron absorption; tea, coffee, and calcium taken together blunt it.</li>
<li>The body cannot excrete excess iron — supplement only a documented deficiency; high ferritin can mean overload.</li>
</ul>
