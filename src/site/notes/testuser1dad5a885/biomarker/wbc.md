<h1>WBC</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>White blood cell (WBC) count, also called leukocyte count, measures the total number of white blood cells (leukocytes) in a volume of blood, reported as cells per microliter (cells/uL) or thousand per microliter (x10^3/uL). WBCs are the cellular effectors of the immune system, responsible for defending the body against infections, foreign substances, and abnormal cells (including cancer). The total WBC count encompasses five major cell types, each serving distinct immunological functions:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Neutrophils (50-70%):</strong> first responders to bacterial infection and acute inflammation; phagocytosis</li>
<li><strong>Lymphocytes (20-40%):</strong> adaptive immunity; T-cells (cell-mediated), B-cells (antibody production), NK cells (viral and tumor surveillance)</li>
<li><strong>Monocytes (2-8%):</strong> tissue macrophage precursors; phagocytosis; antigen presentation</li>
<li><strong>Eosinophils (1-4%):</strong> parasitic infections; allergic reactions; hypersensitivity</li>
<li><strong>Basophils (0.5-1%):</strong> IgE-mediated allergic responses; histamine release</li>
</ul>
<p>The WBC count is one of the most commonly ordered laboratory tests worldwide and is a core component of the complete blood count (CBC). It provides a rapid assessment of immune system activity and is essential in the evaluation of infections, inflammatory conditions, allergic reactions, immunodeficiency states, and hematologic malignancies. While the total WBC count gives an overview, the <strong>differential count</strong> (the proportion of each cell type) is often more diagnostically informative, as specific patterns point to different underlying conditions (e.g., neutrophilia suggests bacterial infection; lymphocytosis suggests viral infection; eosinophilia suggests allergy or parasitic infection).</p>
<p>Normal WBC values vary by age (neonates and children have higher counts), race (individuals of African descent often have lower baseline counts, termed &quot;benign ethnic neutropenia&quot;), time of day (physiological afternoon peak), stress level, and exercise status. Medications -- particularly corticosteroids, lithium, and beta-agonists -- can significantly alter the WBC count.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational hematology test; universal clinical use; WHO essential laboratory test).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range varies by lab and analyzer</li>
<li><strong>Steroids demarginate neutrophils</strong> -- falsely elevated WBC after corticosteroid administration</li>
<li><strong>Time of day variation</strong> -- WBC peaks in late afternoon</li>
<li><strong>Stress, exercise, smoking</strong> elevate WBC</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> -- WBC rises physiologically to ~10,000-12,000; up to 25,000 during labor</li>
<li><strong>Hemolysis in tube, clotted sample</strong> falsely lowers WBC</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase WBC (leukocytosis, &gt;11,000 cells/uL):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bacterial infection</strong> (neutrophilia; the most common cause)</li>
<li><strong>Viral infection</strong> (lymphocytosis; EBV, CMV, HIV, hepatitis)</li>
<li><strong>Parasitic infection</strong> (eosinophilia)</li>
<li><strong>Inflammation:</strong> rheumatoid arthritis, IBD, vasculitis, tissue necrosis</li>
<li><strong>Stress response:</strong> physical or emotional stress, surgery, trauma, burns, seizure</li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> corticosteroids (demargination), lithium, beta-agonists, epinephrine, G-CSF, lithium</li>
<li><strong>Hematologic malignancies:</strong> acute and chronic leukemia, lymphoma, myeloproliferative disorders</li>
<li><strong>Smoking</strong> (chronic mild leukocytosis)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (physiologic; up to 25,000 during labor)</li>
<li><strong>Asplenia</strong> (post-splenectomy)</li>
<li><strong>Rebound</strong> after chemotherapy or marrow suppression</li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroid therapy</strong> (demarginates neutrophils)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease WBC (leukopenia, &lt;4,500 cells/uL):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Viral infections:</strong> HIV, hepatitis, EBV, influenza, COVID-19 (transient suppression)</li>
<li><strong>Bone marrow suppression:</strong> chemotherapy, radiation therapy, aplastic anemia</li>
<li><strong>Medications:</strong> clozapine, carbamazepine, methimazole, methotrexate, sulfasalazine, immunosuppressants</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune:</strong> systemic lupus erythematosus (leukopenia is a criterion), Felty syndrome</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional deficiencies:</strong> vitamin B12, folate, copper deficiency</li>
<li><strong>Sepsis</strong> (late-stage or overwhelming; poor prognosis)</li>
<li><strong>Hypersplenism</strong> (sequestration and destruction in enlarged spleen)</li>
<li><strong>Benign ethnic neutropenia</strong> (constitutional in individuals of African descent; not pathological)</li>
<li><strong>Typhoid fever, brucellosis, rickettsial disease</strong></li>
<li><strong>Radiation exposure</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated WBC (leukocytosis) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bacterial infection:</strong> neutrophilia with left shift (band cells); toxic granulation</li>
<li><strong>Viral infection:</strong> lymphocytosis (atypical lymphocytes in EBV/CMV)</li>
<li><strong>Inflammatory disease:</strong> rheumatoid arthritis, IBD, vasculitis, gout</li>
<li><strong>Acute appendicitis, cholecystitis, pneumonia, meningitis</strong></li>
<li><strong>Hematologic malignancy:</strong> leukemia (acute: blasts; chronic: maturation defect); lymphoma</li>
<li><strong>Tissue necrosis:</strong> MI, trauma, surgery, burns</li>
<li><strong>Stress response:</strong> critical illness, seizure, exercise, anxiety</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (physiologic)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Low WBC (leukopenia) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Viral infection:</strong> HIV, hepatitis, influenza, COVID-19</li>
<li><strong>Bone marrow failure:</strong> aplastic anemia, MDS, chemotherapy, radiation</li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced:</strong> clozapine, carbamazepine, methimazole, methotrexate, sulfasalazine</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune:</strong> SLE, Felty syndrome</li>
<li><strong>Severe sepsis</strong> (poor prognosis)</li>
<li><strong>Hypersplenism</strong> (cirrhosis, portal hypertension)</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional deficiency:</strong> B12, folate, copper</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Non-specific</strong> -- the total WBC count requires the differential for clinical utility</li>
<li><strong>Steroids, stress, smoking</strong> can falsely elevate</li>
<li><strong>Acute phase response</strong> to any physiologic stress</li>
<li><strong>Always interpret with differential, hemoglobin, platelets, peripheral smear</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Infection workup (with differential, CRP, procalcitonin)</li>
<li>Inflammatory disease activity monitoring (with CRP, ESR)</li>
<li>Chemotherapy / radiation monitoring (CBC q1-3 days during therapy)</li>
<li>Drug-induced leukopenia monitoring (clozapine weekly; carbamazepine monthly)</li>
<li>Hematologic malignancy workup (with peripheral smear, flow cytometry, bone marrow biopsy)</li>
<li>Sepsis screening (with lactate, procalcitonin)</li>
<li>Pre-operative assessment</li>
<li>Routine health screening (with CBC)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>WBC reflects underlying immune activity; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For elevated WBC (leukocytosis):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Treat underlying infection:</strong> targeted antibiotics (bacterial); supportive care (viral)</li>
<li><strong>Treat inflammatory disease:</strong> DMARDs (RA), immunosuppression (vasculitis, IBD)</li>
<li><strong>Discontinue offending drugs</strong> (lithium, corticosteroids) when possible</li>
<li><strong>Hematology referral</strong> for suspected leukemia or myeloproliferative disease</li>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong> -- reduces chronic mild leukocytosis</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For low WBC (leukopenia):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Discontinue offending drugs</strong> (clozapine, carbamazepine, methimazole) if possible</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying viral infection</strong> (antivirals for HIV, hepatitis)</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional supplementation:</strong> B12, folate, copper</li>
<li><strong>G-CSF (filgrastim) or GM-CSF</strong> for chemotherapy-induced neutropenia</li>
<li><strong>Treat autoimmune disease</strong> (SLE, Felty syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Splenectomy</strong> for hypersplenism (selective)</li>
<li><strong>Strict infection precautions</strong> if severely neutropenic (ANC &lt;500)</li>
<li><strong>Antibiotic prophylaxis</strong> (fluoroquinolone, antifungal, antiviral) for prolonged neutropenia</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Lifestyle interventions:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adequate sleep, stress management</strong> -- chronic stress mildly elevates WBC</li>
<li><strong>Smoking cessation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Healthy diet</strong> rich in fruits, vegetables, omega-3 fatty acids</li>
<li><strong>Regular exercise</strong> (modest immune-modulating effect)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Vaccinations</strong> before immunosuppressive therapy (pneumococcal, influenza, hepatitis B, zoster)</li>
</ol>
