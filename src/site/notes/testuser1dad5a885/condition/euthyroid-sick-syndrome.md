<h1>Euthyroid Sick Syndrome</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Euthyroid sick syndrome (ESS), also called <strong>non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS)</strong> or <strong>low T3 syndrome</strong>, refers to the alterations in thyroid hormone metabolism that accompany severe non-thyroidal illness. The hallmark is <strong>low serum triiodothyronine (T3)</strong> -- the metabolically active hormone -- with initially normal or low thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and normal or low thyroxine (T4) in more severe disease. The patient is clinically <strong>euthyroid</strong> despite abnormal tests; the thyroid gland itself is normal, and the changes reflect <strong>adaptive downregulation of metabolic rate</strong> to conserve energy during catabolic stress.</p>
<p>The biochemical patterns evolve with illness severity:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Low T3 syndrome</strong> (most common; occurs within 24 hours of acute illness) -- <strong>low total and free T3</strong>, normal TSH and T4. Reflects impaired peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 by <strong>type 1 deiodinase (D1)</strong>, with shunting to inactive <strong>reverse T3 (rT3)</strong>. Mediated by <strong>cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-alpha)</strong> and <strong>elevated cortisol</strong> that inhibit D1</li>
<li><strong>Low T3 and low T4</strong> -- in more prolonged or severe illness, <strong>free T4</strong> also falls (impaired T4 binding, increased clearance); TSH remains low or normal. Associated with worse prognosis</li>
<li><strong>Transient TSH elevation during recovery</strong> -- TSH rises (sometimes to 20 mIU/L) as the axis recovers, mimicking primary hypothyroidism; resolves within weeks</li>
</ol>
<p>The mechanism is multifactorial: <strong>suppression of TSH secretion</strong> (via elevated cortisol, dopamine, and cytokines acting on the hypothalamus/pituitary), <strong>reduced D1 and D2 deiodinase activity</strong> (less T4 -&gt; T3 conversion), <strong>increased D3 activity</strong> (T4 -&gt; rT3 in critical illness), <strong>altered thyroid hormone binding</strong> (low TBG and albumin), and <strong>impaired tissue uptake</strong> of thyroid hormones. The teleological interpretation is that lowering T3 <strong>reduces metabolic rate and oxygen consumption</strong> during catabolic stress -- an adaptive response that is largely beneficial, though extreme suppression may contribute to organ dysfunction (e.g., low-T3 cardiomyopathy in advanced heart failure).</p>
<p>The clinical importance of ESS is <strong>not treatment</strong> -- multiple RCTs of thyroid hormone replacement in critical illness have shown <strong>no benefit and potential harm</strong> -- but <strong>differentiation from true thyroid disease</strong>. This distinction matters because mislabeling a critically ill patient as hypothyroid can lead to inappropriate levothyroxine, while missing <strong>true primary hypothyroidism</strong> (which does require treatment) delays necessary therapy. The key tool is <strong>reverse T3</strong> (elevated in ESS; low in primary hypothyroidism) and <strong>serial testing</strong> (ESS normalizes with recovery; true thyroid disease persists).</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Confirm the diagnosis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Clinical context</strong> -- acute or chronic non-thyroidal illness; no history of thyroid disease</li>
<li><strong>Thyroid panel</strong> -- low T3, variable TSH and T4; <strong>elevated reverse T3</strong> distinguishes ESS from primary hypothyroidism (though rT3 assays are not universally available and not always reliable)</li>
<li><strong>Serial testing</strong> -- recheck after clinical recovery; ESS resolves while primary thyroid disease persists</li>
<li><strong>Consider true thyroid disease</strong> if TSH &gt;20 mIU/L persists, anti-TPO antibodies are positive, or symptoms/signs of hypothyroidism persist after recovery</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Treat the underlying illness (the cornerstone):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>ESS is a biomarker, not a primary disorder; it resolves as the underlying illness improves</li>
<li>Optimize hemodynamics, oxygenation, infection control, and organ support</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional support</strong> -- enteral nutrition preferred; starvation itself causes low T3 within 24-72 hours</li>
<li>Correct metabolic derangements (acidosis, electrolytes, glucose)</li>
<li>Discontinue or reduce thyroid-affecting drugs when possible (high-dose glucocorticoids, dopamine, amiodarone, iodinated contrast)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Do NOT routinely give thyroid hormone:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses in critical illness (including cardiac surgery, burns, sepsis, organ failure) have shown <strong>no mortality benefit</strong> from levothyroxine or liothyronine (T3), and some signal for harm (arrhythmia, hypermetabolism)</li>
<li>Exceptions under investigation: <strong>T3 supplementation in pediatric cardiac surgery</strong> (mixed evidence) and <strong>high-dose T3 in donor heart optimization</strong> before transplantation</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Practical approach in the ICU patient with abnormal thyroid tests:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Defer definitive thyroid diagnosis until clinical recovery if possible</li>
<li>If suspicion of true hypothyroidism is high (history of autoimmune disease, prior thyroidectomy/I-131, goiter, very high TSH &gt;20, positive antibodies): start levothyroxine</li>
<li>If suspicion of adrenal insufficiency (central hypothyroid + cortisol deficiency): give hydrocortisone BEFORE levothyroxine (prevents adrenal crisis)</li>
<li>Recheck thyroid panel 4-6 weeks after recovery</li>
</ol>
