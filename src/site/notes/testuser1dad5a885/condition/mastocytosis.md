<h1>Mastocytosis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Mastocytosis is a heterogeneous group of clonal disorders defined by the pathological accumulation of neoplastic mast cells in one or more organs. The mast cell - a tissue-resident effector cell of the innate and allergic immune system - normally stores granules rich in histamine, tryptase, heparin, and other mediators. In mastocytosis, an expanded clone of abnormal mast cells both releases these mediators inappropriately and infiltrates tissues, giving rise to a disease that spans a remarkably broad clinical range: from a self-limited childhood skin condition to an aggressive, leukemia-like malignancy.</p>
<p>The molecular driver in the great majority of cases is an activating somatic point mutation in the KIT receptor tyrosine kinase, most commonly <strong>KIT D816V</strong>. This mutation renders the receptor constitutively active independent of its ligand (stem cell factor), promoting mast cell survival, proliferation, and accumulation. Detection of the KIT mutation, together with aberrant mast cell immunophenotype (notably CD25 expression), abnormal morphology, dense tissue infiltrates, and an elevated baseline serum tryptase, forms the basis of the WHO diagnostic criteria.</p>
<p>Clinically, mastocytosis is broadly divided into <strong>cutaneous mastocytosis</strong> (confined to the skin, common in children and often regressing by adolescence) and <strong>systemic mastocytosis</strong> (involving the bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs, predominant in adults). Systemic disease is further subclassified - from indolent systemic mastocytosis through smouldering, aggressive, mast-cell-leukemia, and forms associated with another hematologic neoplasm - a stratification that drives prognosis and treatment intensity. Symptoms arise from two mechanisms acting in parallel: episodic mediator release (flushing, pruritus, anaphylaxis, GI symptoms) and direct organ infiltration (cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, bone disease).</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The presentation reflects both mediator release and mast cell burden:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cutaneous</strong>: maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis (urticaria pigmentosa) with reddish-brown macules that urticate on stroking (positive Darier sign); more common as the dominant feature in children</li>
<li><strong>Mediator-related</strong>: flushing, pruritus, recurrent urticaria, and episodic anaphylaxis (classically provoked by insect stings), plus abdominal pain, diarrhea, and peptic ulcer disease from histamine-stimulated gastric acid</li>
<li><strong>Skeletal</strong>: diffuse bone pain, osteopenia/osteoporosis, and fragility fractures</li>
<li><strong>Constitutional/organ infiltration</strong> (advanced disease): fatigue, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and cytopenias from marrow involvement</li>
</ul>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Systemic mastocytosis is diagnosed using the WHO criteria, requiring one major plus one minor, or three minor, criteria. The <strong>major</strong> criterion is multifocal dense mast cell infiltrates (aggregates of &gt;=15 mast cells) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous tissue. <strong>Minor</strong> criteria are: &gt;25% atypical/spindle-shaped mast cells; an activating KIT mutation (usually D816V); aberrant mast cell expression of CD25 (with or without CD2/CD30); and a persistently elevated baseline serum <strong>tryptase</strong> above 20 ng/mL. Because hereditary alpha-tryptasemia also raises baseline tryptase (and frequently coexists), it must be considered when interpreting the tryptase criterion. Bone marrow biopsy with immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry, plus highly sensitive KIT D816V testing, is central to the workup.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Mastocytosis is fundamentally a clonal neoplastic disorder rather than an environmentally driven disease. The key driver is a somatic activating KIT mutation in the mast cell lineage. Hereditary alpha-tryptasemia - an inherited increase in TPSAB1 copy number raising baseline tryptase - is enriched among mastocytosis patients and is associated with more severe mediator symptoms and anaphylaxis. Rare familial cases and germline KIT variants exist. In advanced systemic disease, the mast cell clone may be accompanied by an associated hematologic neoplasm.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is tailored to subtype and symptom burden. For indolent and cutaneous disease, the priorities are <strong>trigger avoidance</strong> and aggressive control of mediator symptoms: H1 and H2 antihistamines, mast cell stabilizers (e.g., cromolyn), and leukotriene receptor antagonists, with omalizumab reserved for refractory mediator symptoms and recurrent anaphylaxis. All patients at risk of anaphylaxis should carry an epinephrine auto-injector. Mastocytosis-associated osteoporosis is treated with bisphosphonates. For <strong>advanced systemic mastocytosis</strong> (aggressive SM, mast cell leukemia, SM-AHN), KIT-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors - midostaurin and the highly selective avapritinib - have transformed outcomes, with cytoreductive chemotherapy or allogeneic stem cell transplant in selected high-grade cases.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The most acute danger is life-threatening anaphylaxis, which can be the presenting feature. Chronic mediator exposure and marrow involvement lead to osteoporosis with fragility fractures. Indolent disease can, uncommonly, progress to aggressive systemic mastocytosis or mast cell leukemia, and systemic disease may be complicated by an associated hematologic neoplasm. Extensive organ infiltration causes hepatic, splenic, and bone marrow dysfunction with cytopenias.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis is strongly subtype-dependent. Childhood cutaneous mastocytosis frequently regresses spontaneously, and indolent systemic mastocytosis in adults typically has a near-normal life expectancy with good symptom control. By contrast, aggressive systemic mastocytosis, mast cell leukemia, and SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm carry a guarded-to-poor prognosis, though KIT-directed therapies have improved survival in advanced disease.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Mastocytosis is rare, with systemic forms estimated at roughly 1 per 10,000 people. Cutaneous mastocytosis predominates in children and often resolves with age, whereas systemic mastocytosis - the great majority of which is the indolent subtype - is the dominant adult presentation. Aggressive variants account for only a small minority of cases.</p>
