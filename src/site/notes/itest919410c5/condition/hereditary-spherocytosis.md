<h1>Hereditary Spherocytosis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Hereditary spherocytosis (HS) is the <strong>most common inherited hemolytic anemia</strong> in individuals of Northern European descent, with a prevalence of approximately <strong>1 in 2000</strong>. It is caused by defects in <strong>red blood cell membrane or cytoskeletal proteins</strong> (spectrin, ankyrin, band 3, protein 4.2), leading to:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Membrane loss and surface area reduction</strong> -&gt; RBCs adopt a spherical shape (spherocytes) rather than the normal biconcave disc</li>
<li><strong>Reduced deformability</strong> -&gt; spherocytes cannot pass through the narrow splenic cords -&gt; <strong>premature destruction</strong> (extravascular hemolysis) in the spleen</li>
<li><strong>Osmotic fragility</strong> -&gt; spherocytes lyse more readily in hypotonic solutions (the basis of the osmotic fragility test)</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Inheritance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Autosomal dominant</strong> (~75% of cases): ANK1 (ankyrin), SPTB (beta-spectrin), SLC4A1 (band 3)</li>
<li><strong>Autosomal recessive</strong> (~25%): SPTA1 (alpha-spectrin; usually severe), EPB42 (protein 4.2)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Clinical severity spectrum:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mild (subclinical):</strong> compensated hemolysis; no anemia; incidental finding on labs</li>
<li><strong>Moderate:</strong> chronic anemia, jaundice, splenomegaly; the most common presentation</li>
<li><strong>Severe:</strong> transfusion-dependent; usually recessive inheritance</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Diagnostic triad:</strong> anemia + jaundice (elevated indirect bilirubin) + splenomegaly. The characteristic laboratory finding is an <strong>elevated MCHC</strong> (&gt;36 g/dL; due to cellular dehydration), which is highly specific for HS.</p>
<p><strong>Aplastic crisis:</strong> a sudden, life-threatening worsening of anemia caused by <strong>parvovirus B19</strong> infection, which temporarily halts erythropoiesis in the bone marrow. The already-short RBC lifespan in HS (reduced from 120 to ~30-90 days) means even a brief cessation of RBC production causes precipitous anemia. Requires urgent transfusion.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Folic acid supplementation</strong> (1-5 mg/day): essential in all HS patients. Chronic hemolysis increases erythropoietic demand, and folate deficiency can precipitate megaloblastic crisis.</p>
<p><strong>Splenectomy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Curative for hemolysis</strong> (removes the site of RBC destruction); spherocytes persist in blood but lifespan normalizes</li>
<li><strong>Indications:</strong> moderate-to-severe HS with symptomatic anemia; recurrent gallstones; growth failure; recurrent transfusion requirement; severe splenomegaly</li>
<li><strong>Approach:</strong> laparoscopic splenectomy (preferred)</li>
<li><strong>Pre-operative vaccinations</strong> (at least 2 weeks before): pneumococcal (PCV13 + PPSV23), Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), meningococcal (MenACWY + MenB)</li>
<li><strong>Post-splenectomy prophylaxis:</strong> penicillin V 250 mg BID (lifelong for children; at least 1-2 years for adults)</li>
<li><strong>Partial splenectomy:</strong> increasingly used in children to preserve some splenic immune function while reducing hemolysis</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Cholecystectomy:</strong> if symptomatic pigment gallstones develop (can occur in adolescence or young adulthood)</p>
<p><strong>Transfusion:</strong> for severe anemia, aplastic crisis (parvovirus B19), or splenic sequestration</p>
<p><strong>Pregnancy:</strong> HS patients require closer monitoring; folate supplementation; risk of crisis from parvovirus infection; splenectomy is deferred until after delivery unless severe</p>
