<h1>Pancreatic Cancer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Pancreatic cancer is a malignant tumor arising from the pancreas, with the overwhelming majority being <strong>pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC)</strong> that originates in the duct-lining epithelium of the exocrine pancreas. A smaller fraction are neuroendocrine tumors (PNETs), which behave very differently. PDAC is notorious for its aggressive biology: it tends to invade locally, spread to regional lymph nodes, and metastasize to the liver and peritoneum early, often before any symptoms appear.</p>
<p>The clinical challenge is that the pancreas is a deep retroperitoneal organ, so early tumors are typically silent. Symptoms such as painless jaundice, epigastric/back pain, and weight loss usually emerge only once the tumor obstructs the bile duct or invades surrounding structures, by which point fewer than one in five patients has resectable disease. New-onset diabetes in an older adult without classic risk factors is increasingly recognized as a potential early warning sign, since PDAC can be diabetogenic.</p>
<p>Pancreatic cancer matters out of proportion to its incidence: although relatively uncommon, it is one of the deadliest cancers, with five-year survival historically in the single digits and only modest recent improvement. Modeling work projected that pancreatic cancer would become one of the leading causes of cancer death in coming decades. Improving outcomes hinges on earlier detection, better systemic therapy, and surgical resection in the minority who present with localized disease.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Presentation depends heavily on tumor location. Tumors in the <strong>head</strong> of the pancreas commonly cause painless obstructive jaundice (dark urine, pale stools, pruritus) by compressing the common bile duct, sometimes with a palpable, non-tender gallbladder (Courvoisier sign). Tumors in the <strong>body and tail</strong> are more likely to present late with epigastric or back pain and weight loss. Across locations, anorexia, early satiety, fatigue, steatorrhea from exocrine insufficiency, and new or worsening diabetes are frequent. Migratory superficial thrombophlebitis (Trousseau sign) reflects the cancer-associated hypercoagulable state.</p>

<p><strong>CA 19-9</strong> is the principal serum tumor marker; it is useful for monitoring treatment response and recurrence but is <strong>not</strong> recommended for screening because it is elevated in many benign biliary/pancreatic conditions and is undetectable in Lewis-antigen-negative individuals. Definitive diagnosis requires histologic confirmation of adenocarcinoma.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The strongest modifiable risk factor is <strong>cigarette smoking</strong>. Other contributors include chronic pancreatitis, long-standing type 2 diabetes, obesity, and heavy alcohol use. Inherited susceptibility accounts for roughly 5-10% of cases, including germline BRCA2, Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, hereditary pancreatitis, Lynch syndrome, and familial atypical multiple mole melanoma (FAMMM). Incidence rises sharply with age, with most diagnoses after 60.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>For the minority with resectable disease, <strong>surgical resection</strong> (pancreaticoduodenectomy/Whipple for head tumors; distal pancreatectomy for body/tail) combined with chemotherapy offers the only chance of long-term survival. Multi-agent regimens such as FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel are used in the neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and metastatic settings. For unresectable or metastatic disease, treatment is palliative: systemic chemotherapy, biliary stenting for obstruction, pain control, and pancreatic enzyme replacement with nutritional support. Germline and tumor molecular testing can identify targetable alterations (e.g., BRCA-mutated tumors responsive to platinum and PARP inhibitors).</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Common complications include biliary obstruction with cholangitis, duodenal/gastric outlet obstruction, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency with malabsorption and weight loss, and venous thromboembolism from the hypercoagulable state. Metastatic spread to the liver, peritoneum (with ascites), and lungs ultimately drives mortality.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis remains poor. Overall five-year survival is low, though it is substantially better for patients diagnosed with localized, resectable disease who complete surgery and chemotherapy. Because most patients present with locally advanced or metastatic disease, median survival is measured in months for unresectable cases. Earlier detection and improved systemic therapy are the principal levers for better outcomes.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Pancreatic cancer is comparatively uncommon but disproportionately lethal, ranking among the top causes of cancer death in high-income countries. Incidence increases with age and is slightly higher in men. Global cancer statistics show rising case counts tracking population aging and the prevalence of risk factors such as smoking, obesity, and diabetes.</p>
