<h1>Infectious Mononucleosis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Infectious mononucleosis (IM), often called &quot;mono&quot; or &quot;glandular fever,&quot; is an acute clinical syndrome that arises most commonly from primary infection with the <strong>Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)</strong>, a ubiquitous human gamma-herpesvirus. EBV is transmitted chiefly through saliva, which gives IM its colloquial name, the &quot;kissing disease.&quot; After an incubation period of roughly four to six weeks, the virus infects oropharyngeal epithelium and B lymphocytes, triggering a vigorous cytotoxic T-cell response that produces the characteristic atypical lymphocytosis and much of the symptomatology.</p>
<p>The classic presentation is a triad of <strong>fever, exudative pharyngitis, and posterior cervical lymphadenopathy</strong>, accompanied by pronounced fatigue. The illness predominates in adolescents and young adults; primary EBV infection in early childhood is frequently asymptomatic or mild, whereas delayed first exposure produces the full-blown syndrome in a substantial proportion of cases. Splenomegaly is common and clinically important because of the small but serious risk of splenic rupture. Although usually self-limiting over two to four weeks, fatigue can linger for months in a minority of patients.</p>
<p>Clinically, IM matters both for the acute morbidity it causes and for the broader role of EBV in human disease. Following primary infection EBV establishes lifelong latency in memory B cells, and the virus is epidemiologically linked to several malignancies (Burkitt and Hodgkin lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma) and to autoimmune conditions such as multiple sclerosis. Distinguishing IM from bacterial pharyngitis is essential, both to avoid unnecessary antibiotics and to prevent the ampicillin/amoxicillin-associated rash that follows their inappropriate use.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Onset is typically gradual, with a prodrome of malaise, headache, and low-grade fever. The established illness features:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pharyngitis</strong>: often severe, with tonsillar enlargement and a grey-white exudate that can mimic streptococcal infection</li>
<li><strong>Lymphadenopathy</strong>: symmetric, tender, most prominent in the posterior cervical chain</li>
<li><strong>Constitutional symptoms</strong>: high fatigue, fever, and myalgia</li>
<li><strong>Splenomegaly and hepatomegaly</strong>: splenic enlargement is frequent; mild hepatitis with elevated transaminases is common but usually asymptomatic</li>
<li><strong>Dermatologic signs</strong>: palatal petechiae, periorbital edema, and a maculopapular rash—especially after exposure to aminopenicillins</li>
</ul>

<p>The heterophile test is rapid and inexpensive but lacks sensitivity early in the course and in children under four, where EBV-specific serology or PCR is preferred. A negative heterophile test with a compatible illness should prompt EBV serology and consideration of other causes of a mononucleosis-like syndrome (CMV, acute HIV, toxoplasmosis).</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant determinant is <strong>age at primary exposure</strong>: symptomatic IM clusters in adolescents and young adults who escaped childhood EBV infection. Intimate salivary contact is the principal mode of transmission, so crowded settings such as university dormitories and military barracks raise incidence. Hygiene and socioeconomic factors that delay first EBV exposure paradoxically increase the likelihood of symptomatic disease later.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is overwhelmingly <strong>supportive</strong>. Rest, adequate hydration, and antipyretic/analgesic agents address the predominant symptoms. Because splenomegaly predisposes to rupture, patients are advised to avoid contact and collision sports for at least three to four weeks (longer if the spleen remains enlarged). Empiric aminopenicillins should be avoided, as they commonly provoke a florid rash. Corticosteroids are not routine and are reserved for specific complications such as impending airway obstruction from massive tonsillar hypertrophy or severe hematologic complications. Antivirals reduce viral shedding but do not improve clinical outcomes and are not recommended in uncomplicated disease.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Although most cases resolve uneventfully, recognized complications include <strong>splenic rupture</strong> (the most feared, often after minor trauma), upper airway obstruction, autoimmune hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia, and clinically apparent hepatitis. Rare neurological complications (encephalitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome) and, in immunocompromised hosts, chronic active EBV infection or lymphoproliferative disease may occur.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>The prognosis for immunocompetent patients is excellent, with full recovery in the great majority within two to four weeks, though fatigue may persist longer. Serious complications are uncommon. Following recovery, EBV persists lifelong as a latent infection, generally without symptoms but with relevance to later EBV-associated malignancies and autoimmunity.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>EBV is among the most widespread human viruses, infecting an estimated 90% or more of adults worldwide. Most primary infections in childhood are subclinical, particularly in lower-income settings where exposure occurs early. In higher-income countries, where first exposure is often delayed to adolescence, symptomatic IM is correspondingly more common, with peak incidence in those aged 15-24 years.</p>
