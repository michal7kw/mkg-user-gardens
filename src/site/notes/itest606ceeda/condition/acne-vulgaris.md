<h1>Acne Vulgaris</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Acne vulgaris is a <strong>chronic inflammatory disorder of the pilosebaceous unit</strong> (hair follicle and its associated sebaceous gland) that affects approximately <strong>85% of adolescents</strong> and persists into adulthood in <strong>~12-15%</strong> (predominantly women). The pathogenesis involves <strong>four key factors</strong>: <strong>(1) androgen-driven sebum overproduction</strong>, <strong>(2) abnormal follicular keratinization</strong> (comedo formation), <strong>(3) proliferation of Cutibacterium acnes</strong> (formerly <em>Propionibacterium acnes</em>), and <strong>(4) inflammation</strong> (innate and adaptive immune response to bacterial antigens and free fatty acids). The interaction of these factors produces the characteristic <strong>comedones, papules, pustules, and nodules</strong> of acne.</p>
<p>The clinical spectrum ranges from mild <strong>comedonal acne</strong> (non-inflammatory; open and closed comedones) through <strong>mild-to-moderate inflammatory acne</strong> (papules and pustules) to <strong>severe nodulocystic acne</strong> (deep, painful nodules and cysts; high risk of scarring). Lesions favor <strong>sebaceous gland-rich areas</strong> -- the <strong>face (especially T-zone), chest, upper back, and shoulders</strong>. <strong>Hormonal acne</strong> in women classically affects the <strong>lower face, jawline, and neck</strong>, is often premenstrual, and is driven by <strong>androgen excess</strong> (PCOS, late-onset congenital adrenal hyperplasia) or <strong>normal-range androgens acting on hormone-sensitive sebaceous glands</strong>. <strong>Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH)</strong> is especially prominent in darker skin types (Fitzpatrick IV-VI) and is often more distressing than the acne itself.</p>
<p>The impact of acne extends well beyond the skin. <strong>Scarring</strong> (atrophic &quot;ice pick,&quot; &quot;boxcar,&quot; &quot;rolling&quot;; or hypertrophic/keloid) is <strong>permanent</strong> and a major source of long-term morbidity. <strong>Psychological comorbidity</strong> is substantial -- <strong>depression, anxiety, low self-esteem, social withdrawal, and even suicidal ideation</strong> are significantly more common in acne patients. Early and effective treatment (including <strong>isotretinoin for severe disease</strong>) is critical to <strong>prevent irreversible scarring</strong> and mitigate psychosocial impact. Contrary to popular belief, acne is <strong>not caused by poor hygiene, chocolate, or greasy foods</strong> (though diet plays a role -- see below); the goal of therapy is to address the underlying pathophysiology, not to &quot;clean&quot; the skin.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Assess severity and tailor therapy (American Academy of Dermatology):</strong></p>
<p><strong>Mild comedonal acne:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Topical retinoid</strong> -- <strong>adapalene 0.1% gel nightly</strong> (OTC; well-tolerated), <strong>tretinoin 0.025-0.1%</strong> cream/gel nightly, or <strong>tazarotene 0.1%</strong>. The cornerstone; normalizes keratinization, prevents comedo formation, and reduces inflammation. Apply at night (retinoids are inactivated by UV); start every other night to reduce irritation</li>
<li><strong>Benzoyl peroxide 2.5-10%</strong> wash or leave-on; antibacterial and keratolytic; apply morning. Start 2.5% (as effective as 10% with less irritation); bleaches fabrics</li>
<li><strong>Salicylic acid</strong> for comedonal acne in patients intolerant of retinoids</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Mild-to-moderate inflammatory acne:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Topical retinoid + topical antimicrobial</strong> (combine for synergy)</li>
<li><strong>Topical antibiotic</strong> -- <strong>clindamycin 1%</strong> or <strong>erythromycin 2%</strong> (BID); <strong>ALWAYS combine with benzoyl peroxide</strong> to reduce antibiotic resistance. Limit courses to 3-4 months</li>
<li><strong>Benzoyl peroxide + clindamycin</strong> combination products (Benzaclind, Duac)</li>
<li><strong>Dapsone 5% gel</strong> (especially for inflammatory lesions in adult women)</li>
<li><strong>Topical azelaic acid 15-20%</strong> -- anti-inflammatory and depigmenting; good for PIH in darker skin</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Moderate-to-severe inflammatory acne:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Oral antibiotic</strong> -- <strong>doxycycline 50-100 mg BID</strong> (first-line), <strong>minocycline</strong> (extended-release formulations; risk of drug-induced lupus and pigmentation), <strong>sarecycline</strong> (narrow-spectrum, less GI/resistance; FDA-approved for acne). Limit to <strong>3-4 months</strong>; combine with <strong>topical retinoid + benzoyl peroxide</strong></li>
<li>Consider <strong>combined oral contraceptives</strong> (FDA-approved: Yaz, Estrostep, Ortho Tri-Cyclen; all have anti-androgenic progestins) or <strong>spironolactone</strong> for women</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Severe nodulocystic or refractory acne:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Isotretinoin (Accutane)</strong> -- <strong>0.5-1.0 mg/kg/day</strong> for <strong>15-20 weeks</strong> (cumulative dose 120-150 mg/kg). The most effective acne therapy; <strong>cures ~70-80% of patients</strong> with one course. Targets all four pathogenic factors. <strong>Teratogenic</strong> -- iPledge REMS mandatory (pregnancy tests, contraception); monitor lipids, LFTs, mood. Side effects: cheilitis (universal), dry skin/mucosa, photosensitivity, musculoskeletal aches, mood changes (depression/suicide -- controversial). Consider for scarring acne, acne unresponsive to oral antibiotics + topical therapy, or severe psychological distress</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Hormonal therapy (women):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Combined oral contraceptives</strong> with anti-androgenic progestins (drospirenone, norgestimate, norethindrone) -- first-line for hormonal acne (jawline, premenstrual flares, PCOS)</li>
<li><strong>Spironolactone 50-200 mg/day</strong> -- androgen receptor antagonist; reduces sebum production. Effective for hormonal acne; monitor potassium. Combine with OCP for contraception (teratogenic)</li>
<li><strong>Clomiphene or metformin</strong> if PCOS underlying</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive and complementary:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low glycemic index diet</strong> -- observational evidence supports reduced acne severity; trial for motivated patients</li>
<li><strong>Dairy reduction</strong> (especially skim milk) -- mixed evidence; may help subset</li>
<li><strong>Zinc 30 mg/day</strong> -- modest anti-inflammatory benefit</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> (EPA-rich) -- anti-inflammatory</li>
<li><strong>Probiotics</strong> -- gut-skin axis; limited but emerging evidence</li>
<li><strong>Niacinamide</strong> (topical 4-5%) -- anti-inflammatory; reduces PIH</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Procedure-based therapy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Chemical peels</strong> -- salicylic acid (BHA), glycolic acid (AHA), Jessner's solution; for comedonal acne and PIH</li>
<li><strong>Comedo extraction</strong> (manual; esthetician or dermatologist)</li>
<li><strong>Intralesional triamcinolone</strong> (dilute; 2.5 mg/mL) for painful nodules/cysts; rapid resolution</li>
<li><strong>Laser and light therapy</strong> -- blue light (kills <em>C. acnes</em> through porphyrin activation), red light (anti-inflammatory), photodynamic therapy (PDT with aminolevulinic acid), intense pulsed light (IPL); for refractory disease</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Treatment of scarring (after active acne controlled):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Atrophic scars</strong> -- <strong>microneedling</strong>, <strong>fractional CO2 laser</strong>, <strong>TCA cross</strong> (for ice pick), subcision (for rolling), fillers (hyaluronic acid, poly-L-lactic acid), punch excision</li>
<li><strong>Hypertrophic scars/keloids</strong> -- intralesional corticosteroid, silicone sheeting, pulsed dye laser, cryotherapy, 5-fluorouracil</li>
<li><strong>PIH</strong> -- azelaic acid, hydroquinone, kojic acid, chemical peels, sunscreen (prevent darkening)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Patient education:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Gentle skincare</strong> (avoid harsh scrubbing; non-comedogenic products)</li>
<li><strong>Sun protection</strong> (prevents PIH and medication photosensitivity)</li>
<li><strong>Realistic expectations</strong> -- improvement in 6-8 weeks; therapy is maintenance, not cure</li>
<li><strong>Adherence</strong> is critical; all topical therapies work best when used consistently</li>
</ul>
