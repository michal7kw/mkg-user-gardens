<h1>Total Protein</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Total protein is a broad first-line biochemical measurement capturing the cumulative concentration of all circulating proteins in the blood. Biologically, this total mass is almost entirely partitioned into two macro-categories:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Albumin</strong> (~60% of total) -- synthesized exclusively by the liver; maintains oncotic pressure and transports ligands (drugs, hormones, fatty acids, bilirubin)</li>
<li><strong>Globulins</strong> (~40% of total) -- include immunoglobulins/antibodies (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, IgE), complement components, transport proteins (transferrin, ceruloplasmin, haptoglobin), and enzymes</li>
</ul>
<p>While highly <strong>non-specific on its own</strong>, examining Total Protein alongside the Albumin-to-Globulin (A/G) ratio and serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) is a critical initial diagnostic sweep. A raw abnormal Total Protein prompts fractionation (SPEP) to identify which fraction (albumin vs alpha-1, alpha-2, beta, gamma globulins) is responsible, allowing accurate diagnosis of: hepatic failure (low albumin synthesis), nephrotic syndrome (urinary protein loss), malnutrition (substrate deficiency), or monoclonal gammopathies (multiple myeloma, MGUS).</p>
<p>Total protein is part of every comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) and is among the most commonly ordered blood tests worldwide. It is used in routine health screening, hospital admissions, and monitoring of patients with kidney disease, liver disease, multiple myeloma, malnutrition, and inflammatory conditions.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational clinical chemistry test; WHO essential laboratory test).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Children have slightly lower total protein (~5.5-7.5 g/dL)</li>
<li>Pregnancy: total protein declines by 0.5-1.0 g/dL (hemodilution)</li>
<li>Posture: standing raises total protein by ~0.5 g/dL</li>
<li>Tourniquet time: prolonged venous stasis raises total protein falsely</li>
<li>Always interpret with albumin, globulins, and SPEP</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase total protein (hyperproteinemia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Multiple myeloma</strong> -- the classic cause of markedly elevated total protein (monoclonal immunoglobulin overproduction)</li>
<li><strong>MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance)</strong> -- premalignant; smaller M-protein peak</li>
<li><strong>Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia</strong> (IgM overproduction)</li>
<li><strong>Severe dehydration</strong> -- hemoconcentration (apparent, not true, elevation)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic inflammatory / autoimmune disease</strong> -- polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia (RA, SLE, Sjögren's, chronic infection, cirrhosis)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic infection</strong> -- TB, HIV, viral hepatitis, endocarditis (polyclonal)</li>
<li><strong>Sarcoidosis</strong></li>
<li><strong>Corticosteroid therapy</strong> (acute; albumin elevation)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease total protein (hypoproteinemia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Malnutrition / starvation</strong> -- insufficient substrate for hepatic albumin synthesis</li>
<li><strong>Cirrhosis / end-stage liver disease</strong> -- impaired hepatocyte albumin synthesis</li>
<li><strong>Nephrotic syndrome / glomerulonephritis</strong> -- urinary protein loss (especially albumin)</li>
<li><strong>Protein-losing enteropathy</strong> -- GI protein loss (Crohn's, celiac disease, Menetrier disease)</li>
<li><strong>Severe burns</strong> -- skin protein loss</li>
<li><strong>Severe infection, sepsis</strong> (capillary leak)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (physiologic hemodilution)</li>
<li><strong>Hemodilution</strong> (excess IV fluids)</li>
<li><strong>Agammaglobulinemia, hypogammaglobulinemia</strong> (low globulin)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated total protein is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Multiple myeloma</strong> -- markedly elevated total protein with M-protein spike on SPEP; anemia, hypercalcemia, renal failure, lytic bone lesions (CRAB criteria)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic infection / autoimmune disease</strong> -- polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia</li>
<li><strong>Dehydration</strong> -- hemoconcentration (apparent)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced total protein is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Liver disease / cirrhosis</strong> -- impaired albumin synthesis (with low albumin, low total protein)</li>
<li><strong>Nephrotic syndrome</strong> -- urinary protein loss (with low albumin, low total protein, high urine protein)</li>
<li><strong>Malnutrition</strong> -- global protein deficiency</li>
<li><strong>Protein-losing enteropathy</strong> -- GI protein loss</li>
<li><strong>Agammaglobulinemia</strong> -- low globulin (genetic)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Highly non-specific alone</strong> -- always interpret with albumin, globulins, A/G ratio</li>
<li><strong>SPEP required</strong> for fractionation when abnormal</li>
<li><strong>Hydration status affects measurement</strong> (dehydration falsely elevates; hemodilution falsely lowers)</li>
<li><strong>Acute phase response</strong> alters globulin fractions</li>
<li><strong>Albumin is a late marker of malnutrition</strong> (half-life ~20 days)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Routine health screening (with CMP)</li>
<li>Liver disease monitoring (Child-Pugh, MELD components)</li>
<li>Nephrotic syndrome monitoring (with urine protein, albumin)</li>
<li>Multiple myeloma workup and monitoring (with SPEP, free light chains, beta-2 microglobulin)</li>
<li>Nutritional assessment (with albumin, prealbumin, transferrin)</li>
<li>Malabsorption evaluation</li>
<li>Hospital admission baseline</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>Total protein reflects underlying nutritional, hepatic, renal, or immune status; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For low total protein (hypoalbuminemia usually the driver):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Treat liver disease</strong> -- hepatology referral; manage cirrhosis complications (ascites, encephalopathy, varices); abstinence from alcohol</li>
<li><strong>Treat nephrotic syndrome</strong> -- ACE inhibitors/ARBs to reduce proteinuria; SGLT2 inhibitors in CKD; immunosuppression for glomerular disease</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional rehabilitation</strong> -- high-protein diet (1.2-1.5 g/kg/day); parenteral nutrition for severe cases; address malabsorption</li>
<li><strong>Treat protein-losing enteropathy</strong> -- gluten-free diet (celiac); immunosuppression (Crohn's)</li>
<li><strong>Avoid excessive diuresis</strong> (further reduces oncotic pressure)</li>
<li><strong>IV albumin</strong> for severe hypoalbuminemia with edema (select cases)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For elevated total protein:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hydration</strong> if dehydration (oral or IV fluids)</li>
<li><strong>Hematology referral</strong> for multiple myeloma workup (SPEP, free light chains, beta-2 microglobulin, bone marrow biopsy)</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying chronic infection or autoimmune disease</strong></li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Lifestyle / nutritional interventions:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>High-biological-value protein intake</strong> (lean meat, fish, eggs, dairy, soy) -- supports albumin synthesis</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong> -- supports hepatic and metabolic health</li>
<li><strong>Avoid alcohol</strong> (in liver disease)</li>
<li><strong>Address comorbidities</strong> that worsen protein loss (CKD, IBD, hyperthyroidism)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ol>
