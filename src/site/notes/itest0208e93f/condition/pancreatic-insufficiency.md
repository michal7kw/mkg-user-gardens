<h1>Pancreatic Insufficiency</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI, or simply pancreatic insufficiency) is the inability of the exocrine pancreas to produce and secrete sufficient <strong>digestive enzymes</strong> -- principally <strong>lipase, amylase, and proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin)</strong> -- to digest food, resulting in <strong>maldigestion</strong> and <strong>malabsorption</strong>. Because the pancreas secretes up to <strong>1-2 liters of enzyme-rich juice per day</strong>, and because <strong>90% of pancreatic function must be lost</strong> before symptoms appear, EPI is a relatively late manifestation of pancreatic disease. The dominant clinical consequence is <strong>fat malabsorption</strong> (lipase is the rate-limiting enzyme for fat digestion and pancreatic lipase is responsible for ~90% of fat digestion), producing <strong>steatorrhea</strong> -- the hallmark of EPI.</p>
<p>The leading causes of EPI vary by age. In <strong>children</strong>, <strong>cystic fibrosis (CF)</strong> accounts for the vast majority of cases (~85% of CF patients have pancreatic insufficiency, often from birth); other childhood causes include <strong>Shwachman-Diamond syndrome</strong>, <strong>Johanson-Blizzard syndrome</strong>, and <strong>Pearson syndrome</strong>. In <strong>adults</strong>, the most common causes are <strong>chronic pancreatitis</strong> (from alcohol, smoking, or recurrent acute pancreatitis; ~50-80% develop EPI over time), <strong>pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma</strong> (especially with tumor in the pancreatic head), and <strong>pancreatic surgery</strong> (Whipple / pancreaticoduodenectomy removes a large portion of the gland). Other causes include <strong>severe acute pancreatitis</strong> (necrosis), <strong>autoimmune pancreatitis</strong>, <strong>type 1 and type 3c diabetes mellitus</strong> (loss of endocrine and exocrine tissue), <strong>celiac disease</strong>, and <strong>Crohn's disease</strong> with secondary pancreatic involvement.</p>
<p>The clinical presentation reflects malabsorption of <strong>fats</strong> (predominantly), <strong>proteins</strong>, and <strong>fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)</strong>. Patients develop <strong>steatorrhea</strong> (oily, foul-smelling stools that float and are difficult to flush), <strong>weight loss despite adequate intake</strong>, <strong>abdominal bloating and cramping</strong>, <strong>fatigue</strong>, and signs of <strong>specific nutrient deficiencies</strong> -- night blindness (vitamin A), osteoporosis (vitamin D), neuropathy and hemolytic anemia (vitamin E), bleeding (vitamin K), and <strong>vitamin B12 deficiency</strong> (loss of the pancreatic enzymes that liberate R-protein-bound B12 in the duodenum). Diagnosis is suggested by symptoms and supported by <strong>fecal elastase-1</strong> (&lt;200 mcg/g is abnormal; &lt;100 is severe) -- the preferred non-invasive test. The <strong>13C-mixed triglyceride breath test</strong> is more sensitive but less available. Direct pancreatic function tests (secretin stimulation) are the gold standard but invasive and rarely used.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) -- the cornerstone:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pancrelipase</strong> (Creon, Pancreaze, Pertzye, Zenpep) -- porcine pancreatic enzymes</li>
<li><strong>Dosing</strong> -- start at <strong>40,000-50,000 USP units of lipase per main meal</strong>, <strong>25,000 USP with snacks</strong>, titrated to fat content and symptom control. Typical adult dose 75,000-160,000 USP per meal</li>
<li><strong>Administration</strong> -- with the <strong>first bite of food and throughout the meal</strong>; do not crush or chew capsules; may open and sprinkle on acidic food (apple sauce)</li>
<li><strong>Goal</strong> -- eliminate steatorrhea, stabilize weight, normalize fat-soluble vitamins</li>
<li><strong>Missed enzymes = missed calories</strong>; adherence is critical</li>
<li><strong>Side effects</strong> -- usually well tolerated; high doses can cause <strong>hyperuricemia</strong>, <strong>fibrosing colonopathy</strong> (especially in children with CF on very high doses)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive therapy to optimize PERT:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acid suppression</strong> -- <strong>proton pump inhibitor</strong> (omeprazole 20 mg) or <strong>H2 blocker</strong> (ranitidine) -- prevents acid inactivation of enzymes in the duodenum; especially useful in CF, after gastric surgery, or with persistent steatorrhea despite adequate PERT</li>
<li><strong>Dietary modification</strong> -- <strong>regular meals with adequate fat</strong> (restricting fat too aggressively worsens malnutrition); <strong>MCT oil</strong> (medium-chain triglycerides) is absorbed directly into the portal vein without micellar solubilization and is a useful calorie supplement</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Nutritional support:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fat-soluble vitamins</strong> -- water-miscible forms of <strong>A, D, E, K</strong>; monitor levels and supplement aggressively (especially vitamin D -- target 25-OH vitamin D &gt;30 ng/mL)</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12</strong> supplementation (oral 1000 mcg/day or IM monthly)</li>
<li><strong>High-calorie, high-protein diet</strong>; small frequent meals</li>
<li><strong>Oral nutritional supplements</strong> (e.g., Scandishake for CF patients)</li>
<li><strong>Enteral nutrition</strong> (nasogastric or PEG feeding) in severe malnutrition or failure to thrive</li>
<li><strong>Calcium</strong> 1000-1200 mg/day and <strong>bisphosphonate</strong> for osteoporosis</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Treat the underlying cause:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cystic fibrosis</strong> -- CFTR modulators (e.g., Trikafta / elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor) improve pancreatic function in some patients with gating mutations</li>
<li><strong>Chronic pancreatitis</strong> -- pain management, alcohol and smoking cessation, endoscopic or surgical therapy for ductal obstruction (stones, strictures)</li>
<li><strong>Diabetes mellitus (type 3c)</strong> -- insulin therapy; pancreatic enzyme replacement may improve glycemic control variability</li>
<li><strong>Celiac disease</strong> -- gluten-free diet</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune pancreatitis</strong> -- corticosteroids</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fecal elastase</strong> annually or with symptom changes</li>
<li><strong>Fat-soluble vitamin levels</strong> (A, D, E, INR for K) annually</li>
<li><strong>HbA1c</strong> and fasting glucose annually (risk of type 3c diabetes)</li>
<li><strong>Bone density</strong> (DEXA) every 2-3 years</li>
<li><strong>Weight, BMI, and growth</strong> (children) at every visit</li>
</ul>
