<h1>Vitamin K2</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) is a fat-soluble vitamin distinct from vitamin K1 (phylloquinone, found in green vegetables and primarily involved in blood coagulation). K2 exists in several subtypes designated by their side chain length: MK-4 (short-chain, found in animal products) through MK-13, with MK-7 (long-chain, from bacterial fermentation, especially natto) being the most clinically studied supplemental form. MK-7 has a significantly longer half-life (~72 hours) compared to MK-4 (~1 hour), providing sustained tissue distribution.</p>
<p>Vitamin K2 activates two critical calcium-regulating proteins through gamma-carboxylation: osteocalcin (which binds calcium into the bone matrix, promoting mineralization) and matrix Gla protein (MGP, which inhibits arterial calcification by binding calcium in vessel walls). This dual action -- directing calcium to bones and away from arteries -- makes K2 essential for cardiovascular and skeletal health simultaneously. Without adequate K2, calcium deposited by vitamin D supplementation may accumulate in arteries rather than bones.</p>
<p>The synergy between vitamins D and K2 is clinically significant. Vitamin D increases the production of both osteocalcin and MGP, but these proteins remain inactive (undercarboxylated) without sufficient K2 to complete their gamma-carboxylation. This is why vitamin K2 supplementation is increasingly recommended alongside vitamin D, particularly at doses above 2,000 IU/day. Undercarboxylated osteocalcin (ucOC) serves as a functional biomarker of K2 status, though it is not yet widely available in clinical practice.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Activates osteocalcin to bind calcium into the bone matrix</li>
<li>Activates matrix Gla protein (MGP) to prevent arterial calcification</li>
<li>Supports dental health by activating dentin-depositing proteins</li>
<li>Facilitates proper calcium distribution between bones and soft tissues</li>
<li>May regulate gene expression through SXR/PXR nuclear receptor activation</li>
<li>Supports mitochondrial electron transport (MK-4 specifically)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Natto (fermented soybeans -- richest MK-7 source)</li>
<li>Gouda and Brie cheese (MK-7, MK-9)</li>
<li>Egg yolks (MK-4, from pastured hens)</li>
<li>Grass-fed butter and ghee (MK-4)</li>
<li>Chicken liver and dark meat (MK-4)</li>
<li>Fermented foods (sauerkraut, some fermented cheeses)</li>
<li>Eel and other fatty fish (small amounts)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Vitamin K2 deficiency contributes to arterial calcification (increasing cardiovascular risk), poor bone mineralization (increasing fracture risk), and dental caries. Subclinical K2 insufficiency is believed to be widespread, as indicated by high levels of undercarboxylated osteocalcin and inactive MGP in population studies. The Rotterdam Study demonstrated that high dietary K2 (but not K1) intake was associated with significantly reduced coronary heart disease, aortic calcification, and all-cause mortality.</p>
<p>Vitamin K2 has no established tolerable upper limit and is generally considered non-toxic even at high supplemental doses. The primary safety concern is interaction with anticoagulant medications, particularly warfarin (vitamin K antagonist), where K2 supplementation can reduce warfarin's anticoagulant effect and increase clotting risk. Patients on warfarin should consult their physician before supplementing K2. Newer direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) do not interact with vitamin K and are compatible with K2 supplementation.</p>
