<h1>Vitamin D</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin D is a fat-soluble secosteroid that functions more like a hormone than a traditional vitamin. It exists in two forms: D2 (ergocalciferol, from plant/fungal sources) and D3 (cholecalciferol, synthesized in skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol upon UVB exposure and found in animal sources). D3 is approximately 87% more effective than D2 at raising and maintaining serum 25(OH)D levels, making it the preferred supplemental form.</p>
<p>Vitamin D undergoes two hydroxylation steps to become biologically active: first in the liver (to 25-hydroxyvitamin D / calcidiol, the primary circulating and storage form used for status assessment) and then in the kidneys (to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D / calcitriol, the active hormonal form). Calcitriol binds the vitamin D receptor (VDR), a nuclear transcription factor expressed in nearly every cell type, regulating the expression of over 1,000 genes involved in calcium homeostasis, immune modulation, cell proliferation, and differentiation.</p>
<p>Vitamin D deficiency is pandemic, affecting an estimated 1 billion people worldwide due to indoor lifestyles, sun avoidance, sunscreen use, higher latitudes, darker skin pigmentation, and aging (which reduces skin synthesis capacity). Optimal serum 25(OH)D levels are debated, with most experts recommending 40-60 ng/mL for comprehensive health benefits. Magnesium is required as a cofactor for the hydroxylation enzymes that activate vitamin D, and vitamin K2 is needed to direct the resulting calcium absorption to bones rather than arteries.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Regulates intestinal calcium and phosphorus absorption for bone mineralization</li>
<li>Modulates innate and adaptive immune responses (promotes antimicrobial peptide cathelicidin)</li>
<li>Influences over 1,000 genes through VDR-mediated transcription</li>
<li>Supports neuromuscular function and reduces fall risk in elderly</li>
<li>Regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis</li>
<li>Modulates insulin secretion and sensitivity via pancreatic beta-cell VDR</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dietary Sources</h2>
<ul>
<li>Fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring)</li>
<li>Cod liver oil (richest food source)</li>
<li>Egg yolks (especially from pastured hens)</li>
<li>Fortified milk and dairy products</li>
<li>Fortified orange juice and cereals</li>
<li>UV-exposed mushrooms (shiitake, maitake)</li>
<li>Beef liver</li>
<li>Cheese (small amounts)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Vitamin D deficiency in children causes rickets (soft, deformed bones), while in adults it manifests as osteomalacia (bone pain, muscle weakness) and accelerates osteoporosis. Deficiency is also associated with increased susceptibility to infections, autoimmune diseases (multiple sclerosis, type 1 diabetes), depression, fatigue, impaired wound healing, and increased cancer risk (colorectal, breast, prostate). Serum 25(OH)D below 20 ng/mL is considered deficient; 20-30 ng/mL insufficient.</p>
<p>Vitamin D toxicity is rare but serious, occurring with chronic supplementation exceeding 10,000 IU/day or serum 25(OH)D above 150 ng/mL. Toxicity causes hypercalcemia (nausea, vomiting, weakness, confusion), hypercalciuria, kidney stones, nephrocalcinosis, and vascular calcification. Importantly, sun exposure cannot cause vitamin D toxicity because excess previtamin D3 is photodegraded in the skin. Co-supplementation with vitamin K2 may help prevent vascular calcification associated with high vitamin D intake.</p>
