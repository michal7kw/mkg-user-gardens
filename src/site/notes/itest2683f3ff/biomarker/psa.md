<h1>PSA</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Prostate-specific antigen (PSA, also called kallikrein-3 or KLK3) is a 33 kDa serine protease produced by the epithelial cells of the prostate gland (both normal and malignant). Its physiological function is to liquefy the seminal coagulum after ejaculation, enabling sperm motility. Small amounts of PSA leak into the bloodstream normally; conditions that increase prostate gland size, vascularity, or epithelial turnover -- including benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), prostatitis, recent ejaculation, vigorous exercise, digital rectal exam, and prostate cancer -- elevate serum PSA. PSA is FDA-approved (1986 for monitoring, 1994 for screening) as the primary blood biomarker for prostate cancer screening, staging, treatment monitoring, and recurrence detection.</p>
<p>PSA screening remains one of the most debated topics in clinical medicine. The PLCO Cancer Screening Trial (Andriole et al., 2009) showed no mortality benefit from PSA screening, while the European ERSPC trial (Schröder et al., 2009) showed a 20% reduction in prostate cancer mortality but at the cost of substantial overdiagnosis and overtreatment of indolent cancers. The 2018 USPSTF guideline (Grade C, ages 55-69) recommends individualized shared decision-making: patients should be informed of the small potential benefit (~1 in 1000 mortality reduction) and substantial risks (false positives, biopsies, overdiagnosis of indolent cancers, treatment-related incontinence and erectile dysfunction). The 2023 AUA guideline similarly emphasizes shared decision-making, with optional screening starting at age 40-45 for higher-risk groups (African ancestry, family history, BRCA2 carriers).</p>
<p>Beyond cancer screening, PSA is monitored after definitive treatment (prostatectomy, radiation) for biochemical recurrence. A detectable/rising PSA after prostatectomy (ultrasensitive PSA rising to 0.2 ng/mL) signals recurrence, prompting consideration of salvage radiation or androgen deprivation therapy.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (extensively studied; FDA-approved; multiple landmark trials; widely used despite controversy).</p>

<p><strong>Age-specific normal PSA (higher cutoffs with age):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Age 40-49: &lt;2.5 ng/mL</li>
<li>Age 50-59: &lt;3.5 ng/mL</li>
<li>Age 60-69: &lt;4.5 ng/mL</li>
<li>Age 70-79: &lt;6.5 ng/mL</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride) lower PSA ~50%; double the measured PSA after 6 months of therapy for accurate interpretation</li>
<li>Significant individual variation; serial changes (PSA velocity) more informative than single values</li>
<li>%free-PSA (&lt;25% suggests cancer) and PHI (Prostate Health Index) improve specificity in gray zone</li>
<li>PSA density (&gt;0.15 ng/mL/mL) helps distinguish BPH from cancer</li>
<li>No PSA threshold excludes cancer with certainty</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase PSA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Prostate cancer (any stage; significant elevation suggests advanced disease)</li>
<li>Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) -- most common cause of mild-moderate elevation</li>
<li>Prostatitis (acute or chronic; antibiotic response lowers PSA)</li>
<li>Recent ejaculation (within 48 h)</li>
<li>Vigorous exercise (cycling in particular, due to perineal pressure)</li>
<li>Digital rectal exam (within 48 h)</li>
<li>Urinary retention or Foley catheter placement</li>
<li>Transurethral procedures (TURP, biopsy)</li>
<li>Aging (PSA rises ~4% per year as prostate grows)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease PSA:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride) -- ~50% reduction by 6 months</li>
<li>Antibiotic therapy (in prostatitis)</li>
<li>Anti-androgen therapy / androgen deprivation therapy (in prostate cancer treatment)</li>
<li>Statin use (modest observational reduction ~5-10%)</li>
<li>Obesity (PSA diluted by larger plasma volume; falsely low PSA per kg body weight)</li>
<li>Weight loss</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated PSA is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prostate cancer:</strong> sensitivity ~70-90%, specificity ~30-50% at cutoff 4 ng/mL; gray zone (4-10) ~25% cancer; &gt;10 ng/mL ~50%+</li>
<li><strong>BPH:</strong> most common cause of mild-moderate elevation; PSA density &lt;0.15 suggests BPH</li>
<li><strong>Prostatitis:</strong> antibiotic-responsive elevation</li>
<li><strong>Biochemical recurrence after definitive therapy:</strong> rising PSA signals recurrence; salvage therapy decisions follow</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations (and why screening is controversial):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low specificity for cancer</strong> -- most elevation is due to BPH or prostatitis, not cancer</li>
<li><strong>Overdiagnosis of indolent cancers</strong> -- autopsy studies show ~30% of men over 50 harbor prostate cancer at death from other causes; PSA screening detects many of these non-life-threatening cancers</li>
<li><strong>Lead-time and length-time biases</strong> inflate apparent survival benefits</li>
<li><strong>Treatment-related harm</strong> -- incontinence (~5-15%) and erectile dysfunction (~20-50%) after prostatectomy/radiation</li>
<li><strong>PLCO trial showed no mortality benefit; ERSPC trial showed ~20% mortality reduction at cost of overdiagnosis</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Screening (after shared decision-making) ages 55-69</li>
<li>Higher-risk patients: African ancestry, family history, BRCA2 carriers (start age 40-45)</li>
<li>Post-treatment recurrence monitoring (every 3 months year 1, 6 months years 2-5, annually thereafter)</li>
<li>Treatment response in metastatic disease</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>PSA reflects underlying prostate biology and is best &quot;improved&quot; through appropriate management of the underlying cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Confirm persistent elevation:</strong> repeat PSA after 4-6 weeks; avoid confounders (ejaculation, exercise, DRE)</li>
<li><strong>5-alpha-reductase inhibitors</strong> (finasteride 5 mg, dutasteride 0.5 mg daily) -- shrink prostate by ~25%, lower PSA ~50%; used for BPH; also reduce prostate cancer risk by ~25% (PCPT trial) but may increase high-grade cancer detection (SELECT/PCPT controversy)</li>
<li><strong>Lycopene</strong> 15-30 mg daily -- modest PSA reduction (~10-20%) in some studies; observational evidence for reduced prostate cancer risk</li>
<li><strong>Sulforaphane / cruciferous vegetables</strong> -- mechanistic and observational evidence for reduced prostate cancer risk; small trials show PSA velocity reduction</li>
<li><strong>Saw Palmetto</strong> 320 mg daily -- BPH symptom relief; modest PSA effect (small reduction)</li>
<li><strong>Anti-inflammatory diet</strong> (Mediterranean, omega-3) -- observational evidence for lower aggressive prostate cancer risk</li>
<li><strong>Prostatitis treatment:</strong> 4-6 weeks of levofloxacin or doxycycline if evidence of bacterial prostatitis; recheck PSA after</li>
<li><strong>PSA velocity / doubling time:</strong> if rising &gt;0.75 ng/mL/year or doubling time &lt;3 years, consider MRI-guided biopsy even if total PSA &lt;10</li>
<li><strong>Prostate MRI (PI-RADS scoring)</strong> before biopsy -- improves detection of clinically significant cancers, reduces overdiagnosis</li>
<li><strong>SELECT trial findings:</strong> selenium and vitamin E (synthetic dl-alpha-tocopherol) supplementation did NOT prevent prostate cancer in healthy men; vitamin E showed trend toward harm -- avoid high-dose synthetic vitamin E for prostate cancer prevention</li>
<li><strong>Lifestyle observational signals:</strong> exercise (150 min/week), plant-based dietary pattern, maintaining healthy weight, limiting red meat and dairy</li>
</ol>
