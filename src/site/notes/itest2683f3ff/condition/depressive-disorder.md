<h1>Depressive Disorder</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Major depressive disorder (MDD), commonly called depression, is a <strong>mood disorder characterized by persistent sadness, anhedonia (loss of interest or pleasure), cognitive and somatic symptoms, and impaired function</strong>, lasting at least two weeks and representing a change from baseline. Diagnosis requires <strong>&gt;=5 of 9 DSM-5 symptoms</strong> (SIG-E-CAPS: <strong>S</strong>leep changes, <strong>I</strong>nterest loss, <strong>G</strong>uilt/worthlessness, <strong>E</strong>nergy loss, <strong>C</strong>oncentration difficulty, <strong>A</strong>ppetite/weight change, <strong>P</strong>sychomotor changes, <strong>S</strong>uicidality; plus depressed mood), including at least one of <strong>depressed mood</strong> or <strong>anhedonia</strong>. Affecting approximately <strong>280 million people worldwide</strong> and with a <strong>lifetime prevalence of ~10-15%</strong>, MDD is the <strong>leading cause of disability globally</strong> (measured by disability-adjusted life years) and a major contributor to suicide.</p>
<p>The neurobiology is multifactorial and incompletely understood. The classic <strong>monoamine hypothesis</strong> posits deficiency of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine in synaptic clefts -- a simplification, but consistent with the efficacy of monoaminergic drugs and the rapid mood-lowering effect of tryptophan depletion. Modern theories integrate <strong>neuroplasticity</strong> (reduced BDNF, hippocampal atrophy in chronic depression), <strong>neuroinflammation</strong> (elevated CRP, IL-6, TNF-alpha in ~30-40% of patients; the &quot;inflammatory phenotype&quot; of depression), <strong>HPA axis dysregulation</strong> (cortisol hypersecretion, dexamethasone non-suppression), and <strong>glutamatergic dysfunction</strong> (the rapid antidepressant effect of ketamine suggests glutamate is central). Stress, particularly <strong>adverse childhood experiences</strong>, produces lasting epigenetic changes that sensitize the stress response and predispose to depression in adulthood.</p>
<p>Depression is <strong>heterogeneous</strong>: presentations range from <strong>melancholic</strong> (severe anhedonia, early morning awakening, weight loss, guilt) to <strong>atypical</strong> (mood reactivity, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, rejection sensitivity) to <strong>psychotic</strong> (delusions/hallucinations) to <strong>seasonal</strong> (seasonal affective disorder). <strong>Comorbidity is the rule</strong> -- anxiety disorders (~60%), substance use disorders (~20%), chronic pain, cardiovascular disease, and other psychiatric conditions frequently co-occur. <strong>Suicide risk</strong> is the most feared complication; depression is implicated in <strong>50-60% of suicides</strong>. <strong>Treatment-resistant depression (TRD)</strong> -- failure of &gt;=2 adequate antidepressant trials -- affects ~30% of patients and drives most of the morbidity and healthcare costs of depression.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Initial assessment:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PHQ-9</strong> (9-item Patient Health Questionnaire) for diagnosis and severity tracking (score 5-9 mild, 10-14 moderate, 15-19 moderately severe, 20-27 severe)</li>
<li><strong>Suicide risk assessment</strong> -- always; ask directly about ideation, plan, intent, access to means, prior attempts, protective factors</li>
<li>Screen for <strong>bipolar disorder</strong> (MDQ) -- antidepressants can precipitate mania</li>
<li>Identify <strong>comorbidities</strong> (anxiety, substance use, chronic pain, thyroid disease, B12 deficiency)</li>
<li>Address <strong>reversible contributors</strong> (hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, medications, sleep apnea)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>First-line treatment (mild-to-moderate):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Psychotherapy</strong> -- <strong>cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)</strong> and <strong>interpersonal therapy (IPT)</strong> are first-line and as effective as medication for mild-moderate depression; particularly preferred in adolescents, pregnancy, and preference-driven care</li>
<li><strong>SSRIs</strong> -- <strong>sertraline</strong> (50-200 mg), <strong>escitalopram</strong> (10-20 mg), <strong>fluoxetine</strong> (20-60 mg); first-line pharmacotherapy. Start low, titrate every 2-4 weeks. Side effects: GI, sexual dysfunction, insomnia, hyponatremia (elderly), serotonin syndrome (rare)</li>
<li><strong>Combination of medication + psychotherapy</strong> is more effective than either alone</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>First-line pharmacotherapy (moderate-to-severe):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>SSRIs</strong> (above) or <strong>SNRIs</strong> -- <strong>venlafaxine</strong> (75-375 mg), <strong>duloxetine</strong> (30-120 mg)</li>
<li><strong>Atypical antidepressants</strong> -- <strong>bupropion</strong> (150-450 mg; activating; no sexual side effects; avoid in seizure/eating disorders), <strong>mirtazapine</strong> (15-45 mg; sedating; weight gain), <strong>vortioxetine</strong> (5-20 mg; pro-cognitive), <strong>vilazodone</strong> (10-40 mg)</li>
<li><strong>Tricyclic antidepressants</strong> (amitriptyline, nortriptyline) and <strong>MAOIs</strong> -- effective but more side effects; third-line; specialist use</li>
<li><strong>Switch</strong> antidepressant class if no response after 6-8 weeks at adequate dose</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Treatment-resistant depression (TRD, failure of &gt;=2 antidepressants):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Augmentation</strong> -- <strong>aripiprazole</strong> (2-10 mg), <strong>quetiapine</strong> (150-300 mg; most evidence), <strong>brexpiprazole</strong>, <strong>lithium</strong>, <strong>triiodothyronine (T3)</strong></li>
<li><strong>Switch</strong> to a different class or mechanism</li>
<li><strong>Esketamine (Spravato)</strong> intranasal -- FDA-approved 2019 for TRD; rapid onset; used with oral antidepressant; REMS program (supervised administration)</li>
<li><strong>Ketamine</strong> IV (off-label) -- rapid antidepressant and anti-suicidal effect within hours; glutamatergic mechanism</li>
<li><strong>Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS)</strong> -- non-invasive; ~50% response in TRD; FDA-approved for TRD and OCD</li>
<li><strong>Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)</strong> -- most effective treatment for severe, psychotic, suicidal, or catatonic depression; 70-90% response; cognitive side effects (transient in most)</li>
<li><strong>Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)</strong> -- for chronic refractory depression (&gt;=4 failed trials)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Acute suicidality:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ketamine/esketamine</strong> -- rapid anti-suicidal effect (within 24 hours)</li>
<li><strong>Hospitalization</strong> if high risk</li>
<li><strong>Lithium</strong> -- reduces suicide risk ~50% in mood disorders (long-term)</li>
<li><strong>Clozapine</strong> if comorbid with psychosis</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Adjunctive and lifestyle interventions:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Exercise</strong> -- 150 min/week aerobic; as effective as SSRIs for mild-moderate depression; supports neuroplasticity and BDNF</li>
<li><strong>Sleep optimization</strong> (CBT-I, treat sleep apnea)</li>
<li><strong>Light therapy</strong> (for seasonal affective disorder)</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> (EPA-rich; modest benefit as adjunct)</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D</strong> supplementation if deficient</li>
<li><strong>Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT)</strong> -- reduces relapse risk</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Maintenance:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Continue antidepressant for at least 6-9 months</strong> after first episode; lifelong for recurrent depression (&gt;=3 episodes)</li>
<li><strong>Psychotherapy</strong> reduces relapse</li>
<li><strong>Regular monitoring</strong> of mood, suicidality, function, and side effects</li>
</ul>
