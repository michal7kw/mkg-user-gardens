<h1>Anogenital Warts</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Anogenital warts (condylomata acuminata) are benign epithelial growths affecting the genital, perianal, and anal skin and mucous membranes, caused by infection with human papillomavirus (HPV). Approximately 90% of cases are attributable to the <strong>low-risk</strong> HPV types <strong>6 and 11</strong>, which have low oncogenic potential but very high transmissibility. They are among the most common clinical manifestations of sexually transmitted HPV infection, with a substantial lifetime risk in sexually active adults.</p>
<p>The virus infects basal keratinocytes through micro-abrasions in the epithelium during skin-to-skin sexual contact. After a variable incubation period (weeks to months), viral replication drives epithelial hyperproliferation producing the characteristic soft, exophytic, often cauliflower-like papules. Although benign, the lesions are frequently multiple, recur after treatment, and can cause significant psychosocial distress.</p>
<p>Because the responsible HPV types are vaccine-preventable, the quadrivalent and nonavalent HPV vaccines have produced dramatic population-level declines in anogenital wart incidence in countries with strong vaccination programs.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Lesions appear as soft, flesh-colored to grayish papules ranging from small flat plaques to large verrucous, cauliflower-like masses. Common sites include the penis, scrotum, vulva, vaginal introitus, cervix, perineum, and the perianal and anal canal region. Most warts are asymptomatic, but patients may report itching, irritation, bleeding, or discomfort, and large or perianal lesions can cause dyspareunia or painful defecation.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is usually <strong>clinical</strong>, based on visual recognition of characteristic lesions. Application of 3-5% acetic acid (acetowhitening) can reveal subclinical lesions. Biopsy is reserved for atypical, pigmented, indurated, ulcerated, or treatment-resistant lesions to exclude dysplasia or malignancy. HPV genotyping is not required for the diagnosis of typical warts.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Key risk factors include multiple or new sexual partners, early sexual debut, absence of HPV vaccination, immunosuppression (notably HIV infection and transplant recipients), and tobacco smoking. Immunocompromised individuals tend to have more extensive, persistent, and recurrent disease.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Treatment is directed at lesion clearance and symptom relief; no therapy eradicates the underlying virus. Patient-applied options include imiquimod (immune modulator), podophyllotoxin, and sinecatechins. Provider-applied treatments include cryotherapy, trichloroacetic acid, electrocautery, laser ablation, and surgical excision. Treatment choice depends on lesion size, number, location, and patient preference; recurrence is common regardless of modality. HPV vaccination is preventive rather than therapeutic.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>The prognosis is benign. A proportion of warts regress spontaneously through cell-mediated immunity, but recurrence after treatment is frequent. Lesions caused by HPV-6/11 carry minimal malignant potential, though coinfection with high-risk HPV types can coexist and warrants attention in screening contexts.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Anogenital warts are the most common viral STI presentation in many settings. Following the introduction of HPV vaccination, multiple national surveillance programs (e.g., Denmark, Israel) have documented marked reductions in incidence, especially in younger vaccinated cohorts, demonstrating strong herd and direct protection effects.</p>
