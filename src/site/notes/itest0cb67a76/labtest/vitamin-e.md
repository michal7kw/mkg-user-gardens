<h1>Vitamin E (Alpha-Tocopherol)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin E is a family of fat-soluble compounds (tocopherols and tocotrienols) that act as lipid-phase antioxidants, protecting polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes from oxidative damage. Alpha-tocopherol is the predominant and most biologically retained form in human plasma and is the analyte reported by most laboratories. The test measures serum or plasma alpha-tocopherol concentration, typically by HPLC.</p>
<p>Because vitamin E circulates bound to lipoproteins, serum levels track total lipids; results are therefore often expressed as a ratio to total cholesterol plus triglycerides to correct for hyperlipidemia. The sample is obtained by venipuncture. Clinicians order the test to evaluate suspected deficiency (fat malabsorption, cholestasis, abetalipoproteinemia, premature infants), to investigate neurologic or hemolytic findings, and to assess overall antioxidant/micronutrient status.</p>

<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Low alpha-tocopherol indicates vitamin E deficiency, most often secondary to fat malabsorption (cystic fibrosis, cholestatic liver disease, short bowel) or rare genetic disorders (abetalipoproteinemia, ataxia with vitamin E deficiency). Deficiency can cause peripheral neuropathy, spinocerebellar ataxia, retinopathy, and hemolytic anemia, particularly in preterm infants. Elevated levels usually reflect supplementation; very high intake may interfere with vitamin K-dependent clotting and increase bleeding risk. Because levels parallel serum lipids, low values in the setting of low cholesterol may not represent true deficiency, underscoring the value of lipid-adjusted interpretation.</p>
