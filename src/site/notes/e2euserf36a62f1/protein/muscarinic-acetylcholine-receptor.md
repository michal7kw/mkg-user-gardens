<h1>Muscarinic Acetylcholine Receptor</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The muscarinic acetylcholine receptors are a family of five G-protein coupled receptor subtypes (M1-M5, encoded by CHRM1-CHRM5) that mediate the slower, metabotropic responses to the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, in contrast to the fast ionotropic nicotinic receptors. The odd-numbered subtypes (M1, M3, M5) couple to Gq/11 and stimulate phospholipase C, whereas the even-numbered subtypes (M2, M4) couple to Gi/o and inhibit adenylyl cyclase.</p>
<p>Muscarinic receptors are the principal receptors at the effector organs of the parasympathetic nervous system. M2 receptors in the heart slow heart rate and conduction; M3 receptors drive smooth-muscle contraction (bronchi, gut, bladder), glandular secretion (salivary, lacrimal), and pupillary constriction. In the brain, M1 and M4 receptors are heavily involved in learning, memory, and movement control.</p>
<h2>Pharmacology</h2>
<p>Muscarinic antagonists (&quot;anticholinergics&quot;) such as atropine, ipratropium, oxybutynin, and scopolamine block these receptors and are used to increase heart rate, dilate bronchi, relax the bladder, and reduce secretions; their adverse effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, confusion) follow directly from the physiology. The progressive loss of cholinergic neurons in Alzheimer's disease and the role of muscarinic signaling in cognition underpin the cholinergic hypothesis of memory and the use of cholinesterase inhibitors.</p>
