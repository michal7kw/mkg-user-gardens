<h1>Gallbladder</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>The gallbladder is a small, pear-shaped muscular sac located on the inferior surface of the liver. Its function is to store and concentrate bile, the digestive fluid continuously produced by hepatocytes. Between meals, bile is diverted into the gallbladder, where the epithelium reabsorbs water and electrolytes to concentrate the bile up to tenfold. When fat- and protein-containing chyme enters the duodenum, the hormone cholecystokinin triggers gallbladder contraction and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi, expelling concentrated bile into the small intestine to emulsify dietary fat and enable absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.</p>
<p>Bile is composed of bile acids, cholesterol, phospholipids, bilirubin (the breakdown product of heme), and electrolytes. The relative proportions of cholesterol, bile salts, and phospholipids determine cholesterol solubility; supersaturation predisposes to gallstone formation.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Gallstone disease (cholelithiasis) is the predominant disorder of the gallbladder. Most stones in developed countries are cholesterol-rich and form when bile becomes supersaturated with cholesterol; pigment stones are enriched in bilirubin. Stones may be asymptomatic but can cause biliary colic, and obstruction of the cystic or common bile duct can precipitate acute cholecystitis, obstructive jaundice (with rising bilirubin and biliary enzymes such as GGT and alkaline phosphatase), ascending cholangitis, or gallstone pancreatitis. Symptomatic disease is commonly treated by cholecystectomy, after which the liver supplies bile directly to the intestine.</p>
