<h1>Esr</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR, or &quot;sed rate&quot;) is a non-specific blood test measuring the rate at which red blood cells (RBCs) settle to the bottom of a vertical anticoagulated tube over one hour, reported in millimeters per hour (mm/hr). The principle is simple: in healthy blood, RBCs carry a negative surface charge (zeta potential) that causes them to repel each other and settle slowly. When inflammation is present, hepatic synthesis of acute-phase proteins (especially fibrinogen and immunoglobulins) increases; these proteins neutralize the RBC surface charge, allowing RBCs to clump together (rouleaux formation) and settle faster. The result is a measurable increase in sedimentation rate that reflects the integrated inflammatory state over the prior 1-2 weeks.</p>
<p>ESR has been used clinically since the early 20th century (Westergren method standardized in 1921) and remains one of the most commonly ordered inflammatory markers. Its principal clinical uses are: (1) <strong>temporal arteritis (giant cell arteritis) and polymyalgia rheumatica</strong> -- ESR &gt;50 mm/hr is typical and supports the diagnosis (with clinical features; normal ESR does not exclude); (2) <strong>rheumatoid arthritis disease activity monitoring</strong> -- trends with disease activity; (3) <strong>multiple myeloma and other paraproteinemias</strong> -- very high ESR (&gt;100 mm/hr) is a classic finding; (4) <strong>osteomyelitis</strong> -- supports diagnosis when clinical suspicion is moderate; (5) <strong>chronic infection monitoring</strong> (e.g., endocarditis, prosthetic joint infection).</p>
<p>ESR is often paired with <strong>C-reactive protein (hsCRP)</strong> for inflammatory assessment. The two are complementary: <strong>CRP</strong> rises faster (within 6-12 h) and falls faster (days) than ESR, making CRP better for acute inflammation monitoring, while <strong>ESR</strong> changes more slowly (days-weeks) and reflects the integrated inflammatory state, making it better for chronic disease monitoring. When ESR and CRP are discordant, multiple myeloma (high ESR, normal CRP) or systemic lupus (high ESR, normal CRP in some patients) should be considered.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 4/5</strong> (well-validated; clinical guideline-endorsed for temporal arteritis/PMR; less specific than CRP).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>ESR naturally increases with age</li>
<li>Females have higher ESR than males (estrogen effect on fibrinogen)</li>
<li>Anemia (especially macrocytic) falsely elevates ESR (fewer RBCs = less resistance to settling)</li>
<li>Polycythemia, sickle cell disease, severe leukocytosis, hypofibrinogenemia falsely lower ESR</li>
<li>Pregnancy: physiologic elevation (fibrinogen rises to 400-600 mg/dL)</li>
<li>Obesity: modest elevation</li>
<li>Renal failure: elevated (uremic plasma proteins)</li>
<li>Significant inter-individual variation; within-subject serial changes more informative</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase ESR:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acute or chronic infection:</strong> bacterial, viral, fungal, mycobacterial</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune disease:</strong> rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, vasculitis, polymyalgia rheumatica, temporal arteritis</li>
<li><strong>Malignancy:</strong> multiple myeloma (often &gt;100 mm/hr), lymphoma, other cancers</li>
<li><strong>Chronic kidney disease</strong> (uremic proteins)</li>
<li><strong>Anemia</strong> (especially macrocytic)</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> (physiologic elevation)</li>
<li><strong>Obesity</strong></li>
<li><strong>Diabetes mellitus</strong> (chronic inflammation)</li>
<li><strong>Inflammatory bowel disease</strong> (Crohn's, UC)</li>
<li><strong>Endocarditis</strong> (subacute)</li>
<li><strong>Osteomyelitis</strong></li>
<li><strong>Tissue necrosis</strong> (MI, trauma, surgery)</li>
<li><strong>Drugs:</strong> oral contraceptives, estrogen, vitamin A, procainamide, methyldopa (raise ESR)</li>
<li><strong>Older age</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease ESR:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Polycythemia vera</strong> (too many RBCs)</li>
<li><strong>Sickle cell disease, sickle cell trait</strong> (altered RBC shape)</li>
<li><strong>Severe leukocytosis</strong></li>
<li><strong>Congestive heart failure</strong></li>
<li><strong>Hypofibrinogenemia</strong> (congenital or liver disease)</li>
<li><strong>Cryoglobulinemia</strong> (in vitro artifact at room temp)</li>
<li><strong>Drugs:</strong> high-dose steroids, NSAIDs, methotrexate, biologics (in therapeutic setting)</li>
<li><strong>Very high elevation of WBC</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated ESR is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Temporal arteritis (giant cell arteritis):</strong> ESR &gt;50 mm/hr in ~85%; treat empirically with high-dose corticosteroids if clinical suspicion high (don't wait for biopsy)</li>
<li><strong>Polymyalgia rheumatica:</strong> ESR &gt;40 mm/hr typical; response to low-dose prednisone is rapid</li>
<li><strong>Rheumatoid arthritis disease activity:</strong> trends with DAS28; useful for serial monitoring</li>
<li><strong>Systemic lupus erythematosus:</strong> ESR often elevated even when CRP is normal (unlike bacterial infection, where CRP rises more than ESR)</li>
<li><strong>Multiple myeloma:</strong> very high ESR (&gt;100 mm/hr) is classic; check serum protein electrophoresis</li>
<li><strong>Chronic infection:</strong> endocarditis, osteomyelitis, prosthetic joint infection, abscess</li>
<li><strong>Inflammatory bowel disease:</strong> modest elevation with active disease</li>
<li><strong>Vasculitis:</strong> polyarteritis nodosa, ANCA-associated vasculitis, Henoch-Schönlein purpura</li>
<li><strong>Malignancy:</strong> especially multiple myeloma and lymphoma</li>
<li><strong>Acute MI:</strong> transient elevation (peak at 3-7 days)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Non-specific</strong> -- does not identify the cause of inflammation</li>
<li><strong>Slower to change than CRP</strong> -- less useful for acute inflammation monitoring</li>
<li><strong>Affected by anemia, polycythemia, sickle cell, RBC shape</strong> -- false positives/negatives</li>
<li><strong>Reference range varies by age and sex</strong> -- use age-adjusted cutoffs</li>
<li><strong>CRP is generally preferred</strong> for acute inflammation; ESR for chronic disease monitoring</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Diagnosis and monitoring of temporal arteritis and polymyalgia rheumatica</li>
<li>Rheumatoid arthritis disease activity (with CRP, DAS28)</li>
<li>Multiple myeloma screening (with serum protein electrophoresis, free light chains)</li>
<li>Chronic infection monitoring (endocarditis, osteomyelitis)</li>
<li>Autoimmune disease activity (SLE, vasculitis)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>ESR reflects underlying inflammation; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Temporal arteritis / polymyalgia rheumatica:</strong> high-dose prednisone 40-60 mg daily (TAK); rapid taper over 1-2 years; low-dose aspirin 81 mg daily; bone protection (calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonate)</li>
<li><strong>Rheumatoid arthritis:</strong> methotrexate (anchor DMARD); biologics (TNFi, IL-6, anti-CD20); treat-to-target remission</li>
<li><strong>Multiple myeloma:</strong> hematology referral; bortezomib-based regimens; stem cell transplant eligible</li>
<li><strong>Infection:</strong> targeted antibiotics based on organism; surgical source control if needed</li>
<li><strong>SLE:</strong> hydroxychloroquine, corticosteroids, immunosuppressives (mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, azathioprine); biologics (belimumab, anifrolumab, rituximab)</li>
<li><strong>Curcumin</strong> 500-1000 mg daily -- modest anti-inflammatory effect; small ESR reduction in some studies</li>
<li><strong>Omega-3 fatty acids</strong> 1-4 g/day EPA+DHA -- anti-inflammatory; modest ESR reduction in chronic inflammatory states</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D optimization</strong> (2000-5000 IU D3 daily) -- observational evidence for lower ESR in vitamin D deficient patients</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong> rich in anti-inflammatory foods (olive oil, fish, vegetables)</li>
<li><strong>Weight loss</strong> in obese patients -- reduces chronic low-grade inflammation</li>
<li><strong>Treat anemia if present</strong> -- macrocytic anemia falsely elevates ESR</li>
<li><strong>Discontinue offending drugs</strong> if ESR-elevating (oral contraceptives, vitamin A) when clinically appropriate</li>
</ol>
