<h1>Hexokinase</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Hexokinase is the first enzyme of <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/pathway/glycolysis/" class="internal-link">Glycolysis</a>, catalysing the phosphorylation of <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/glucose/" class="internal-link">Glucose</a> to <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/glucose-6-phosphate/" class="internal-link">Glucose-6-Phosphate</a> at the expense of one <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a>. Four isoforms (HK I–IV) show tissue-specific expression; the high-affinity isoforms HK I and HK II associate with the outer membrane of the <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/cellularcomponent/mitochondria/" class="internal-link">Mitochondria</a> via the VDAC porin, physically coupling cytosolic glycolysis to mitochondrial <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> production. The liver isoform, hexokinase IV (glucokinase), has a high Km and is not product-inhibited, allowing it to act as a glucose sensor. By committing glucose to metabolism, hexokinase sets the entry point for <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/pathway/glycolysis/" class="internal-link">Glycolysis</a>.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Hexokinase transfers the γ-phosphate group from <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> to the C6 hydroxyl of <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/glucose/" class="internal-link">Glucose</a> via an <strong>induced-fit</strong> mechanism. Binding of glucose triggers a large conformational change in which the two lobes of the enzyme close over the substrate like a clam, excluding bulk water from the active site. This water exclusion is essential: it prevents the enzyme from wastefully hydrolysing <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> (a competing reaction with water as the phosphate acceptor) and instead ensures the phosphate is transferred to glucose. The product, <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/glucose-6-phosphate/" class="internal-link">Glucose-6-Phosphate</a>, carries a negative charge that traps it inside the cell, since the glucose transporters cannot export the phosphorylated sugar. This irreversible phosphorylation is the first of three regulated, energy-investing steps of glycolysis.</p>
<h2>Biological Role</h2>
<p>By producing <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/glucose-6-phosphate/" class="internal-link">Glucose-6-Phosphate</a>, hexokinase feeds substrate not only into <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/pathway/glycolysis/" class="internal-link">Glycolysis</a> but also into the pentose phosphate pathway and glycogen synthesis, making it a key branch point of carbohydrate metabolism. The reaction CONSUMES one <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> per glucose, part of the &quot;investment phase&quot; of glycolysis. Hexokinase activity is regulated by feedback inhibition: its own product, <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/glucose-6-phosphate/" class="internal-link">Glucose-6-Phosphate</a>, is a potent allosteric/product inhibitor of HK I–III, ensuring that glucose is not over-committed when downstream flux is already saturated. The energy-sensing kinase <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/protein/ampk/" class="internal-link">AMPK</a> further integrates hexokinase activity and mitochondrial association with the cell's overall energy charge.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Hexokinase deficiency (typically of the red-cell HK I isoform) causes a hereditary non-spherocytic hemolytic anemia, because erythrocytes depend almost entirely on glycolysis for <a href="/garden/testuser1f29c274f/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a>. Conversely, overexpression of hexokinase II and its mitochondrial binding is a hallmark of the <strong>Warburg effect</strong> in cancer, where tumour cells up-regulate glycolytic flux even in the presence of oxygen; mitochondrially bound HK II also suppresses apoptosis, making it a target of interest in oncology. Hepatic glucokinase (HK IV) is central to glucose sensing in the liver and pancreatic β-cells, and activating mutations cause hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia while inactivating mutations cause MODY2 diabetes.</p>
