<h1>Potassium</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Potassium (K+) is the body's principal intracellular cation, with roughly 98% of total body potassium residing inside cells and only about 2% in the extracellular fluid. The steep transmembrane gradient — maintained chiefly by the sodium-potassium ATPase pump — establishes the resting membrane potential of excitable tissues, making potassium indispensable for nerve impulse transmission, skeletal muscle contraction, and especially the generation and conduction of the cardiac action potential. Because the serum measurement captures only the small extracellular pool, even modest changes in serum potassium can signal substantial disturbances in whole-body balance or transcellular distribution.</p>
<p>Serum potassium concentration is governed by three interacting processes: dietary intake (typically 40-120 mEq/day), renal excretion (the kidney handles roughly 90% of potassium elimination, fine-tuned by aldosterone, distal sodium delivery, and tubular flow), and transcellular shifts driven by insulin, beta-adrenergic tone, and acid-base status. Acidosis tends to drive potassium out of cells (raising serum levels), while alkalosis, insulin, and catecholamines drive it into cells (lowering serum levels). This buffering allows the body to tolerate large intake swings, but it also means serum values must always be interpreted alongside renal function, acid-base state, and medications.</p>
<p>Potassium is one of the most frequently ordered and clinically consequential laboratory tests, appearing on virtually every basic and comprehensive metabolic panel. Its narrow therapeutic window is the reason for its importance: both hyperkalemia and hypokalemia destabilize myocardial membrane excitability and can cause potentially fatal arrhythmias. Serum potassium is therefore central to the evaluation of kidney disease, heart failure, adrenal disorders, the safe use of diuretics and renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, and the management of acutely ill patients.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals vary slightly by laboratory and assay; serum values typically run 0.3-0.5 mEq/L higher than plasma because potassium is released from platelets during clotting.</li>
<li><strong>Pseudohyperkalemia</strong> is a common pre-analytical artifact caused by hemolysis, fist clenching/tourniquet use during venipuncture, prolonged sample standing, or marked thrombocytosis/leukocytosis. A hemolyzed sample should be redrawn before treating an unexpected high value.</li>
<li>Neonates and infants have higher normal ranges than adults.</li>
</ul>

<p>Potassium is reported on the basic metabolic panel (BMP) and comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP). It is not routinely fasting-dependent, but proper collection technique is essential to avoid spurious elevation. When an abnormal result is unexpected or inconsistent with the clinical picture, prompt redraw without tourniquet stasis or hemolysis is recommended.</p>
<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Potassium (Hyperkalemia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Impaired renal excretion (acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease)</li>
<li>Medications: ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, amiloride), NSAIDs, trimethoprim</li>
<li>Hypoaldosteronism / adrenal insufficiency (reduced renal potassium secretion)</li>
<li>Metabolic acidosis (transcellular shift of K+ out of cells)</li>
<li>Tissue breakdown: rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis, hemolysis, severe burns</li>
<li>Insulin deficiency or hyperglycemia in diabetes</li>
<li>Excessive intake, especially with concurrent renal impairment</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Potassium (Hypokalemia):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Renal losses: loop and thiazide diuretics, primary aldosteronism, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes</li>
<li>Gastrointestinal losses: vomiting, diarrhea, nasogastric suction, laxative abuse</li>
<li>Transcellular shift: insulin therapy, beta-agonists, metabolic/respiratory alkalosis, refeeding</li>
<li>Magnesium deficiency (impairs renal potassium conservation, causing refractory hypokalemia)</li>
<li>Inadequate dietary intake (rare in isolation)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels (hyperkalemia) are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Chronic Kidney Disease and Acute Kidney Injury</strong> — reduced glomerular filtration and impaired distal secretion are the leading causes of sustained hyperkalemia.</li>
<li><strong>Cardiac arrhythmias</strong> — progressive ECG changes (peaked T waves, widened QRS, sine-wave pattern) can culminate in ventricular fibrillation or asystole; the clinical danger correlates with rate of rise as well as absolute level.</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease)</strong> — aldosterone deficiency reduces renal potassium excretion.</li>
<li><strong>Medication effects</strong> — RAAS inhibitors and potassium-sparing diuretics are frequent contributors, particularly in combination or with renal impairment.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels (hypokalemia) are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Atrial Fibrillation and ventricular ectopy</strong> — hypokalemia increases myocardial excitability and arrhythmia risk, an effect amplified in patients on digoxin.</li>
<li><strong>Muscle weakness, cramps, and (when severe) paralysis or rhabdomyolysis.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Hypertension and aldosterone excess</strong> — primary aldosteronism classically presents with hypokalemia and hypertension; conversely, higher dietary potassium intake is associated with lower blood pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Refractory hypokalemia from magnesium depletion</strong> — potassium cannot be corrected until the coexisting magnesium deficiency is treated.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Routine safety monitoring when starting or titrating diuretics and RAAS inhibitors</li>
<li>Serial monitoring in dialysis patients and during treatment of diabetic ketoacidosis</li>
<li>Workup of arrhythmias, muscle weakness, and unexplained hypertension</li>
<li>Critical-illness electrolyte surveillance</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Correct the underlying cause first</strong> — distinguish renal loss, GI loss, transcellular shift, or impaired excretion, because management differs fundamentally between hypokalemia and hyperkalemia.</li>
<li><strong>Replete magnesium when hypokalemia is refractory</strong> — coexisting magnesium deficiency must be corrected (oral or IV magnesium) before potassium will normalize.</li>
<li><strong>Dietary potassium for mild hypokalemia or blood-pressure benefit</strong> — potassium-rich foods (leafy greens, beans, bananas, potatoes, citrus) support normal levels in patients with intact renal function; increased intake is associated with lower blood pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Restrict dietary potassium and review medications for hyperkalemia</strong> — limit high-potassium foods and reassess ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, and NSAIDs in patients with reduced kidney function.</li>
<li><strong>Acute hyperkalemia management</strong> — stabilize the myocardium with IV calcium, shift potassium intracellularly with insulin/glucose and beta-agonists, and remove potassium with diuretics, potassium binders, or dialysis as indicated.</li>
<li><strong>Avoid pre-analytical artifact</strong> — ensure clean, non-hemolyzed draws to prevent unnecessary treatment of pseudohyperkalemia.</li>
</ol>
