<h1>Oropharyngeal Cancer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Oropharyngeal cancer is a malignancy of the oropharynx — the middle portion of the throat that includes the palatine and lingual tonsils, the base of the tongue, the soft palate, and the posterior and lateral pharyngeal walls. The overwhelming majority are squamous cell carcinomas. Over the past two decades the epidemiology has shifted dramatically: a growing share of cases, especially of the tonsil and tongue base, are caused by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (chiefly HPV-16), even as the classic tobacco- and alcohol-driven form has declined in many regions. These two etiologies define biologically and clinically distinct diseases.</p>
<p>HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer typically affects younger patients with fewer traditional risk factors and is identified by overexpression of the p16 protein on immunohistochemistry, a reliable surrogate for transforming HPV infection. Crucially, HPV-positive tumors respond far better to treatment and have substantially higher survival than HPV-negative tumors, which has prompted separate AJCC staging systems for the two and active investigation of treatment &quot;de-intensification&quot; to reduce toxicity in favorable HPV-positive disease without compromising cure. HPV-negative disease, by contrast, behaves more aggressively, is associated with field cancerization from tobacco and alcohol, and carries a higher risk of second primary tumors.</p>
<p>The clinical importance of oropharyngeal cancer lies in this divergence of biology and prognosis, the often subtle presentation (frequently a painless neck node), and the strong potential for primary prevention. Because HPV-related disease shares its cause with cervical and anal cancers, prophylactic HPV vaccination is expected to reduce future incidence, while tobacco and alcohol control remains essential for preventing the HPV-negative form.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>HPV-positive tumors commonly present with a painless enlarging neck mass from nodal metastasis, sometimes with a small or occult primary tumor. Other features across both subtypes include persistent sore throat, a sensation of a lump, odynophagia and dysphagia, referred ear pain (otalgia), voice changes, and unexplained weight loss.</p>

<p>HPV status fundamentally alters staging and prognosis, so it is determined in every case.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The principal risk factors are persistent high-risk HPV infection for the HPV-positive subtype and tobacco and heavy alcohol use — synergistically — for the HPV-negative subtype. Behaviors increasing oral HPV exposure and male sex are associated with HPV-positive disease.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Treatment is individualized by stage and HPV status. Many patients are treated with definitive radiotherapy, with concurrent cisplatin-based chemotherapy added for more advanced or higher-risk disease. Transoral surgery, including transoral robotic surgery, is an option for accessible tumors, followed by risk-adapted adjuvant therapy. For favorable HPV-positive disease, clinical trials are evaluating de-intensified regimens to preserve cure while limiting long-term toxicity. Prevention rests on HPV vaccination and tobacco and alcohol cessation.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Complications include locoregional recurrence and distant metastasis, as well as substantial treatment-related morbidity — dysphagia, xerostomia, mucositis, and long-term swallowing dysfunction with aspiration risk. Tobacco-related disease additionally carries a notable risk of second primary cancers.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis is strongly stratified by HPV status: HPV-positive, p16-positive tumors have markedly higher survival and respond well to chemoradiotherapy, whereas HPV-negative tumors carry a worse outlook. Extranodal extension, advanced stage, and continued smoking adversely affect outcomes even within the HPV-positive group.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>The incidence of HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer has risen substantially in many high-income countries, in some now exceeding cervical cancer incidence, while HPV-negative disease has declined alongside falling smoking rates. Recent data suggest the rise in HPV-positive incidence may be reaching a plateau in some populations.</p>
