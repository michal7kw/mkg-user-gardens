<h1>High Altitude Adaptation</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>High altitude adaptation is the set of <strong>physiological adjustments</strong> by which the human body responds to the <strong>decreased partial pressure of oxygen (hypobaric hypoxia)</strong> encountered at elevations above ~2500 m (8200 ft). At 5500 m (Everest Base Camp), the atmospheric pressure is approximately half of sea level, and the partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) is only ~45 mmHg (vs 100 mmHg at sea level). The body responds through a cascade of acute, subacute, and chronic adaptations:</p>
<p><strong>Acute adaptations (minutes to hours):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hyperventilation:</strong> the most important immediate response. Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid body sense hypoxia and stimulate the respiratory center -&gt; increased minute ventilation. This raises alveolar PO2 but also causes <strong>respiratory alkalosis</strong> (from CO2 washout), which initially limits the ventilatory response. The kidney compensates by excreting bicarbonate over 2-4 days (<strong>acclimatization diuresis</strong>), allowing ventilation to increase further. <strong>Acetazolamide</strong> (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) accelerates this bicarbonate excretion, speeding acclimatization.</li>
<li><strong>Increased heart rate and cardiac output:</strong> sympathetic activation maintains oxygen delivery. Resting tachycardia of 10-30 bpm above baseline is typical.</li>
<li><strong>Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV):</strong> pulmonary arterioles constrict in response to alveolar hypoxia, redirecting blood to better-oxygenated areas. At altitude, diffuse alveolar hypoxia causes <strong>generalized pulmonary vasoconstriction</strong> -&gt; pulmonary hypertension. This is beneficial for gas exchange but predisposes to <strong>high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE)</strong> in susceptible individuals.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Subacute adaptations (days to weeks):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Erythropoiesis:</strong> hypoxia stimulates EPO release within hours -&gt; increased red blood cell production over days to weeks. Hb increases by ~1 g/dL per week at altitude. This improves oxygen-carrying capacity.</li>
<li><strong>Increased 2,3-BPG:</strong> shifts hemoglobin-oxygen dissociation curve rightward, facilitating oxygen unloading at tissues.</li>
<li><strong>Increased capillary density and mitochondrial density:</strong> in muscle and other tissues.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Chronic/genetic adaptations (years to generations):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tibetans:</strong> have evolved genetic adaptations (EPAS1, EGLN1) that allow survival at &gt;4000 m without the extreme erythrocytosis seen in Andeans. They have higher ventilation rates, higher nitric oxide production, and lower hemoglobin levels than Andeans at the same altitude.</li>
<li><strong>Andeans:</strong> exhibit higher hemoglobin levels (adaptive erythrocytosis) and higher pulmonary arterial pressures than Tibetans.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Failure to acclimatize</strong> results in altitude illness:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acute mountain sickness (AMS):</strong> headache + at least one of nausea/vomiting, fatigue, dizziness, sleep disturbance. Affects ~25% of visitors to 2500 m and ~50% at 4500 m.</li>
<li><strong>HAPE:</strong> life-threatening non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema. Most common cause of death from altitude illness.</li>
<li><strong>HACE:</strong> life-threatening cerebral edema with ataxia and altered consciousness.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Acclimatization strategy (gradual ascent):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Above 3000 m: increase sleeping altitude by no more than 300-500 m per day</li>
<li>Include a rest day every 1000 m of altitude gained</li>
<li>&quot;Climb high, sleep low&quot; (daytime excursions to higher altitude, descend for sleep)</li>
<li>Hydration: 3-4 L/day (altitude increases insensible fluid loss from hyperventilation and low humidity)</li>
<li>Avoid alcohol, sedatives, and heavy meals on first nights</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pharmacological:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Acetazolamide</strong> 125-250 mg BID (start 24h before ascent; continue 2-4 days at target altitude): accelerates acclimatization by inducing metabolic acidosis; also prevents AMS. Side effects: paresthesias (hands/feet), altered taste (carbonated drinks taste flat), polyuria.</li>
<li><strong>Dexamethasone</strong> 4 mg q6h (for AMS/HACE treatment; reduces cerebral edema; does not speed acclimatization)</li>
<li><strong>Nifedipine</strong> 30 mg SR q12h (for HAPE prevention in susceptible individuals and treatment)</li>
<li><strong>Sildenafil</strong> 50 mg q8h (for HAPE; selective pulmonary vasodilation)</li>
<li><strong>Tadalafil</strong> 10 mg BID (for HAPE prevention; PDE5 inhibitor)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Altitude illness management:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>AMS:</strong> stop ascent; rest; hydrate; symptomatic treatment (analgesics, antiemetics); acetazolamide; descend if worsening</li>
<li><strong>HACE:</strong> immediate descent (&gt;500-1000 m); dexamethasone 8 mg then 4 mg q6h; supplemental oxygen; hyperbaric chamber if available</li>
<li><strong>HAPE:</strong> immediate descent; supplemental oxygen (target SpO2 &gt;90%); nifedipine; sit upright; avoid exertion; hyperbaric chamber</li>
</ul>
