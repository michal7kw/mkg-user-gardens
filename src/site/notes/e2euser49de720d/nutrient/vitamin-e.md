<h1>Vitamin E</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Vitamin E is a family of eight fat-soluble compounds: four tocopherols (alpha, beta, gamma, delta) and four tocotrienols (alpha, beta, gamma, delta). Alpha-tocopherol is the most biologically active form in humans and the only form maintained in plasma by hepatic alpha-tocopherol transfer protein (alpha-TTP). Gamma-tocopherol, the predominant dietary form in Western diets (from soybean and corn oils), possesses unique anti-inflammatory properties through its ability to trap reactive nitrogen species.</p>
<p>The primary biological function of vitamin E is protecting polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in cell membranes from lipid peroxidation by donating a hydrogen atom to lipid peroxyl radicals, breaking the chain reaction of oxidative membrane damage. This role is especially critical for cells with high PUFA content, such as red blood cells, neurons, and immune cells. Vitamin C regenerates the resulting tocopheroxyl radical back to active vitamin E, creating a synergistic antioxidant cycle at the membrane-cytosol interface.</p>
<p>Vitamin E also functions beyond antioxidation: it modulates gene expression, inhibits protein kinase C (relevant to vascular smooth muscle proliferation), enhances immune function (particularly T-cell-mediated responses in the elderly), and influences eicosanoid production. Alpha-tocopherol inhibits platelet adhesion and aggregation, contributing to anti-thrombotic effects. Supplementation should include mixed tocopherols rather than isolated alpha-tocopherol, as high-dose alpha-tocopherol depletes gamma-tocopherol levels.</p>
<h2>Key Functions</h2>
<ul>
<li>Protects cell membrane PUFAs from lipid peroxidation as the primary lipid-soluble antioxidant</li>
<li>Maintains red blood cell membrane integrity and prevents hemolysis</li>
<li>Enhances T-cell-mediated immune responses, especially in aging</li>
<li>Inhibits platelet aggregation and supports vascular health</li>
<li>Modulates gene expression and signal transduction via protein kinase C inhibition</li>
<li>Protects LDL cholesterol from oxidation, reducing atherosclerotic potential</li>
</ul>

<h2>Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p>Vitamin E deficiency is rare and usually results from fat malabsorption disorders (cystic fibrosis, celiac disease, cholestatic liver disease) or genetic defects in alpha-TTP. Symptoms include peripheral neuropathy, ataxia (spinocerebellar degeneration), skeletal myopathy, retinopathy, and impaired immune function. In premature infants, deficiency causes hemolytic anemia due to fragile red blood cell membranes.</p>
<p>High-dose vitamin E supplementation (&gt;400 IU/day of alpha-tocopherol) has been associated with increased all-cause mortality in meta-analyses, though this remains debated. Excess alpha-tocopherol increases bleeding risk by inhibiting platelet aggregation and vitamin K-dependent clotting factors, which is clinically relevant in individuals on anticoagulants (warfarin). High-dose supplementation depletes gamma-tocopherol, potentially losing its unique anti-inflammatory benefits. The tolerable upper limit is 1,000 mg/day of alpha-tocopherol.</p>
