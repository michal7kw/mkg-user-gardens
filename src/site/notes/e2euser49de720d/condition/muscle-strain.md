<h1>Muscle Strain</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>A muscle strain (commonly called a &quot;pulled muscle&quot;) is an injury to the muscle-tendon unit caused by excessive stretching or forceful contraction that exceeds the tissue's tensile strength. The injury typically occurs at the <strong>musculotendinous junction</strong> (the weakest point in the muscle-tendon unit). Muscle strains are among the most common injuries in sports, accounting for up to <strong>30% of all sports injuries</strong>. The <strong>hamstring</strong> is the most commonly strained muscle (especially in sprinting sports), followed by quadriceps, calf (gastrocnemius), and groin (adductor).</p>
<p><strong>Grading system (clinical severity):</strong></p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Grade</th>
<th>Description</th>
<th>Recovery Time</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Grade 1</td>
<td>Mild: microscopic tearing (&lt;5% of fibers); minimal strength loss</td>
<td>1-3 weeks</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Grade 2</td>
<td>Moderate: partial tear (5-50% of fibers); noticeable strength loss; palpable tenderness</td>
<td>3-6 weeks</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Grade 3</td>
<td>Severe: complete rupture (&gt;50% of fibers); significant weakness; palpable gap</td>
<td>6-12+ weeks (often surgical)</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>The injury mechanism is typically <strong>eccentric contraction</strong> (muscle lengthening under tension) -- for example, the hamstring decelerating the leg during sprinting. Less commonly, strains result from direct trauma or overstretching.</p>
<p><strong>Re-injury risk</strong> is the major clinical concern: without adequate rehabilitation, up to <strong>30% of hamstring strains recur</strong> within the first year. The strongest predictor of re-injury is <strong>previous injury</strong>, followed by inadequate rehabilitation, older age, and muscle imbalance.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Acute phase (0-72 hours) -- RICE protocol:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>R</strong>est: stop the precipitating activity; use crutches if unable to bear weight</li>
<li><strong>I</strong>ce: 15-20 min every 2-3 hours for first 48-72h (reduces swelling and pain)</li>
<li><strong>C</strong>ompression: elastic bandage (reduces swelling and hemorrhage)</li>
<li><strong>E</strong>levation: raise injured limb above heart level</li>
<li><strong>NSAIDs:</strong> ibuprofen 400-600 mg q6-8h or naproxen 500 mg BID for 3-7 days (analgesia + anti-inflammatory)</li>
<li><strong>Do NOT stretch or massage</strong> in first 72h (may worsen tissue damage)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Subacute phase (3-14 days):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Gentle mobilization:</strong> pain-guided; begin pain-free range of motion exercises</li>
<li><strong>Isometric strengthening</strong> (muscle contraction without movement)</li>
<li><strong>Progressive stretching</strong> (gentle; no pain)</li>
<li><strong>Gentle massage</strong> (after 72h; can promote healing)</li>
<li><strong>Heat therapy</strong> (after acute phase; promotes blood flow and flexibility)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Rehabilitation phase (1-6+ weeks):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Progressive strengthening: concentric -&gt; eccentric -&gt; plyometric</li>
<li>Sport-specific exercises: running, cutting, jumping (graded return to sport)</li>
<li>Proprioception and balance training</li>
<li><strong>Return-to-sport criteria</strong> (hamstring):
<ul>
<li>Pain-free full range of motion</li>
<li>Symmetric strength (&gt;=90% of uninjured side; handheld dynamometer)</li>
<li>Successful completion of sport-specific testing (e.g., 4 x single-leg hops)</li>
<li>No apprehension during sprinting</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Surgical intervention:</strong> for Grade 3 (complete) ruptures with significant functional loss, especially in athletes or proximal hamstring avulsions (tendon pulled off bone)</p>
