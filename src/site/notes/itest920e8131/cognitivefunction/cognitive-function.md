<h1>Cognitive Function</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Cognitive function is the collective term for the mental operations that allow an individual to perceive, attend to, remember, reason about, and act upon information. Rather than a single capacity, it is an umbrella spanning multiple interacting domains — attention, learning and memory, executive function, language, processing speed, and visuospatial/perceptual ability. These domains rely on widely distributed, overlapping brain networks rather than isolated regions.</p>
<p>Cognitive function is dynamic across the lifespan: it matures through childhood and adolescence as the prefrontal cortex and its connections develop, peaks in early-to-mid adulthood, and shows domain-specific change with aging (processing speed and some memory functions typically decline earlier than vocabulary or general knowledge). Because it integrates so many subprocesses, cognitive function is highly sensitive to brain health, sleep, vascular status, metabolic state, mood, and disease.</p>
<h2>Cognitive Domains</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Attention and focus</strong> — selecting and sustaining processing of relevant information</li>
<li><strong>Memory and learning</strong> — encoding, consolidating, and retrieving information</li>
<li><strong>Executive function</strong> — goal-directed control, working memory, inhibition, and flexibility</li>
<li><strong>Language</strong> — comprehension and production of spoken and written language</li>
<li><strong>Processing speed</strong> — the rate at which cognitive operations are performed</li>
<li><strong>Visuospatial ability</strong> — perceiving, analyzing, and manipulating spatial information</li>
</ul>
<h2>Assessment</h2>
<p>Cognitive function is measured with neuropsychological batteries and screening instruments such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and domain-specific tests of memory, attention, and executive control. Composite scores and longitudinal change are used to detect mild cognitive impairment and dementia, and to track the effects of interventions.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Global or domain-specific decline in cognitive function characterizes mild cognitive impairment and dementias such as Alzheimer's disease, and is also seen in vascular disease, depression, sleep disorders, and metabolic dysfunction. Modifiable factors including physical activity, cardiovascular risk control, sleep quality, and reduced sedentary behavior influence the trajectory of cognitive function with age, making it a key target for healthy-aging strategies.</p>
