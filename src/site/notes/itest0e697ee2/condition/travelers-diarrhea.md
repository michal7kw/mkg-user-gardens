<h1>Travelers Diarrhea</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Travelers' diarrhea (TD) is an acute diarrheal syndrome that develops during or shortly after travel from a high-income to a lower-income region, and it is the single most common illness affecting international travelers. The conventional case definition is the passage of three or more unformed stools in a 24-hour period accompanied by at least one additional enteric symptom such as abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting, fever, or fecal urgency. Attack rates can reach 30-70% of travelers depending on destination, season, and dietary behavior.</p>
<p>The vast majority of cases are bacterial in origin, with enterotoxigenic and enteroaggregative <em>Escherichia coli</em> the leading pathogens worldwide, followed by <em>Campylobacter</em> (especially in South and Southeast Asia), <em>Shigella</em>, <em>Salmonella</em>, and, less commonly, viral (norovirus) and protozoal (<em>Giardia</em>, <em>Cryptosporidium</em>) agents. Transmission is fecal-oral through contaminated food and water. Because pathogens vary geographically, the relative importance of each organism — and the most appropriate empiric antibiotic — differs by region.</p>
<p>TD is usually self-limited, resolving within three to five days even without treatment, and the central clinical priorities are rehydration and symptom control. Nonetheless it carries meaningful morbidity: it interrupts travel, and a minority of patients develop post-infectious sequelae including post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome and reactive arthritis. Rational, evidence-graded use of antibiotics is emphasized to avoid promoting antimicrobial resistance and disturbance of the gut microbiome.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Symptoms typically begin within the first week or two of travel. Most patients have watery, non-bloody diarrhea with cramping, urgency, and malaise; nausea and vomiting are common, and low-grade fever may occur. Features that point toward invasive (dysenteric) disease — and a stronger case for antibiotics — include high fever, gross blood or mucus in the stool, and significant systemic toxicity.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is clinical in the typical traveler and does not require microbiological confirmation. Stool testing (culture or multiplex gastrointestinal PCR) is reserved for severe, dysenteric, or persistent illness (lasting more than 14 days), where it helps distinguish bacterial, protozoal, and non-infectious causes and guides directed therapy.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Destination is the dominant determinant of risk, with the highest rates in South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Latin America and the Middle East. Ingestion of contaminated food and water — particularly street food, raw or undercooked items, and untreated water or ice — is the proximate exposure. Host factors that increase susceptibility include reduced gastric acidity (e.g., proton pump inhibitor use, which lowers the infectious dose), immunocompromise, and inflammatory bowel disease.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Oral rehydration is the foundation of care for all severities. Loperamide provides effective symptomatic relief in afebrile, non-dysenteric illness and can be combined with an antibiotic in moderate-to-severe cases. Among antibiotics, <strong>azithromycin</strong> is preferred empirically because of rising fluoroquinolone resistance (notably <em>Campylobacter</em> in Asia); fluoroquinolones and the non-absorbed agent rifaximin remain options for non-invasive disease. Bismuth subsalicylate is useful for mild symptoms and as chemoprophylaxis in selected high-risk travelers. Antibiotic prophylaxis is generally discouraged for routine travelers given resistance and microbiome concerns.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Dehydration is the principal acute complication, especially in older travelers and those with vomiting. Post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome develops in a measurable minority and can persist long after the acute episode, and reactive arthritis may follow infection with invasive organisms, particularly in HLA-B27-positive individuals.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Prognosis is excellent; most episodes resolve within days. Persistent diarrhea beyond two weeks warrants evaluation for protozoal infection, post-infectious malabsorption, or unmasked underlying gastrointestinal disease.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>TD affects tens of millions of international travelers annually and is the leading cause of travel-related morbidity. Incidence correlates strongly with destination risk tier and with dietary indiscretion, and the bacterial etiologic profile shifts by region.</p>
