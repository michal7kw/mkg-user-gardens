<h1>Corneal Ulcer</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>A corneal ulcer is an <strong>open sore on the cornea</strong> characterized by an epithelial defect with underlying stromal inflammation and infiltration. It is typically caused by infection (bacterial, viral, fungal, or parasitic) and represents a <strong>sight-threatening emergency</strong> requiring urgent ophthalmologic evaluation. Untreated corneal ulcers can progress rapidly to <strong>corneal perforation, endophthalmitis, and permanent vision loss</strong>.</p>
<p>The most common pathogens vary by risk factor:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Contact lens users:</strong> <em>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</em> (rapidly progressive; severe), <em>Acanthamoeba</em> (chronic, ring infiltrate)</li>
<li><strong>Non-contact lens:</strong> <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em>, <em>Streptococcus pneumoniae</em>, <em>S. pyogenes</em></li>
<li><strong>Trauma (vegetative matter):</strong> <em>Fusarium</em>, <em>Aspergillus</em></li>
<li><strong>Immunocompromised:</strong> <em>Candida</em>, <em>Aspergillus</em></li>
<li><strong>HSV:</strong> recurrent keratitis; dendritic ulcer pattern</li>
</ul>
<p>Contact lens use is the <strong>most important modifiable risk factor</strong> -- particularly overnight wear and poor hygiene. The risk of bacterial keratitis is <strong>6-8x higher</strong> in extended-wear contact lens users compared to daily wear users.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Severe, progressive eye pain</strong> (the hallmark; distinguishes from conjunctivitis which is typically mild discomfort)</li>
<li><strong>Photophobia</strong> (severe)</li>
<li><strong>Tearing and discharge</strong> (purulent or mucopurulent)</li>
<li><strong>Foreign body sensation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Redness</strong> (conjunctival and ciliary injection)</li>
<li><strong>Blurred vision</strong> (from corneal opacity or hypopyon)</li>
<li><strong>Eyelid swelling and blepharospasm</strong> (guarding)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Slit lamp findings:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Corneal epithelial defect</strong> (stains with fluorescein)</li>
<li><strong>Stromal infiltrate</strong> (white opacity beneath the epithelial defect)</li>
<li><strong>Hypopyon</strong> (pus layering in anterior chamber; indicates severe infection)</li>
<li><strong>Ring infiltrate</strong> (Acanthamoeba)</li>
<li><strong>Dendritic ulcer</strong> (HSV keratitis; branching pattern)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Immediate:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Discontinue contact lens use</strong></li>
<li><strong>Ophthalmology referral</strong> (urgent; same day)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Bacterial keratitis:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Corneal scraping</strong> for Gram stain and culture (severe ulcers or atypical presentation)</li>
<li><strong>Empiric topical antibiotics:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Fluoroquinolone monotherapy (moxifloxacin or levofloxacin) for less severe ulcers</li>
<li><strong>Fortified antibiotics</strong> (vancomycin 25-50 mg/mL + tobramycin or gentamicin 14 mg/mL) for severe, vision-threatening, or treatment-resistant ulcers</li>
<li>Dosing: every 15-30 minutes initially (around the clock for first 24-48 hours), then taper as clinical response improves</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Cycloplegia</strong> (atropine or cyclopentolate) for pain relief from ciliary spasm</li>
<li><strong>Do NOT patch</strong> the eye (worsens infection)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>HSV keratitis:</strong> topical antiviral (trifluridine 1%) or oral acyclovir/valacyclovir; <strong>avoid topical corticosteroids without antiviral coverage</strong></p>
<p><strong>Fungal keratitis:</strong> topical antifungals (natamycin 5%, amphotericin B 0.15%, voriconazole 1%); prolonged course (weeks-months)</p>
<p><strong>Acanthamoeba keratitis:</strong> topical chlorhexidine 0.02% or PHMB 0.02%; prolonged course (months)</p>
<p><strong>Surgical:</strong> corneal transplant (penetrating keratoplasty) if perforation, descemetocele, or dense scarring</p>
