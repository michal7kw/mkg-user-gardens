<h1>Hs Crp</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is a pentameric acute-phase protein synthesized predominantly by hepatocytes in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines, most notably interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1, and tumor necrosis factor. The &quot;high-sensitivity&quot; designation refers not to a different molecule but to the assay: standard CRP assays measure the marked elevations seen in acute infection (often 10-500 mg/L), whereas hs-CRP assays are calibrated to reliably quantify the much lower concentrations (well below 1 mg/L) that distinguish gradations of low-grade chronic inflammation relevant to cardiovascular risk.</p>
<p>CRP participates in innate immunity by binding phosphocholine on damaged cells and microbial surfaces, activating the classical complement pathway and promoting opsonization and phagocytic clearance. Circulating levels rise within hours of an inflammatory stimulus and fall as the stimulus resolves, giving CRP a relatively short half-life (~19 hours) and making it a dynamic, real-time index of systemic inflammatory tone. Because hepatic CRP production is driven by IL-6, hs-CRP serves as a convenient downstream readout of upstream cytokine activity originating from visceral adipose tissue, atherosclerotic plaque, and other inflamed tissues.</p>
<p>Clinically, hs-CRP is used chiefly for cardiovascular risk stratification in primary prevention, where it adds prognostic information beyond traditional lipid measures and helps identify &quot;residual inflammatory risk&quot; in people whose LDL cholesterol is already well managed. It is inexpensive, widely available, and reproducible, but it is non-specific: any source of inflammation raises it. For this reason interpretation requires excluding acute illness, and a single high value is typically confirmed with a repeat measurement (often the average of two readings taken weeks apart) before assigning chronic risk.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>The &lt;1 / 1-3 / &gt;3 mg/L tertiles are the AHA/CDC consensus thresholds for cardiovascular risk and are independent of, and additive to, LDL cholesterol.</li>
<li>Values above 10 mg/L generally reflect acute illness rather than chronic cardiovascular risk; the test should be repeated once the acute process has resolved.</li>
<li>Best practice is to average two fasting or non-fasting measurements taken about two weeks apart when the patient is metabolically stable and free of infection.</li>
<li>Levels are influenced by obesity, smoking, sex, and hormonal status; modest population differences exist but the same risk tertiles are applied broadly.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase hs-CRP:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Acute infection or recent illness (strong, transient cytokine-driven rise)</li>
<li>Obesity and excess visceral adiposity (adipose tissue secretes IL-6)</li>
<li>Cigarette smoking (chronic vascular and systemic inflammation)</li>
<li>Metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes</li>
<li>Chronic inflammatory and autoimmune disease (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis)</li>
<li>Physical trauma, surgery, or recent vigorous unaccustomed exercise</li>
<li>Estrogen/hormone therapy and pregnancy</li>
<li>Poor sleep and chronic psychological stress</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease hs-CRP:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Weight loss and reduction of visceral fat</li>
<li>Regular aerobic physical activity and improved fitness</li>
<li>Smoking cessation</li>
<li>Statin therapy (independent anti-inflammatory effect beyond LDL lowering)</li>
<li>Mediterranean-style and anti-inflammatory dietary patterns</li>
<li>Improved glycemic control</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular Disease</strong> -- hs-CRP independently predicts first myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death, adding risk information beyond LDL cholesterol.</li>
<li><strong>Coronary Artery Disease / Atherosclerosis</strong> -- reflects active vascular inflammation within atherosclerotic plaque, a driver of plaque instability.</li>
<li><strong>Type 2 Diabetes and Metabolic Syndrome</strong> -- low-grade inflammation accompanies insulin resistance and visceral adiposity.</li>
<li><strong>Rheumatoid Arthritis and autoimmune disease</strong> -- a marker of disease activity and systemic inflammatory burden.</li>
<li><strong>Sepsis and acute infection</strong> -- markedly elevated values (&gt;10 mg/L) signal an acute-phase response rather than chronic risk.</li>
<li><strong>Heart Failure and Hypertension</strong> -- inflammation contributes to adverse remodeling and outcomes.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Low systemic inflammatory burden and a more favorable cardiovascular risk profile.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Refining cardiovascular risk in adults at intermediate (borderline) calculated risk.</li>
<li>Identifying residual inflammatory risk in statin-treated patients with controlled LDL.</li>
<li>Tracking response to lifestyle change (weight loss, exercise, smoking cessation) and anti-inflammatory therapy.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Achieve and maintain a healthy weight</strong> -- loss of visceral fat is one of the most effective ways to lower hs-CRP because adipose tissue is a major source of IL-6.</li>
<li><strong>Exercise regularly</strong> -- consistent moderate aerobic activity (e.g., 150+ minutes/week) reduces chronic inflammation over weeks to months.</li>
<li><strong>Quit smoking</strong> -- smoking cessation removes a sustained pro-inflammatory stimulus and lowers hs-CRP over time.</li>
<li><strong>Adopt a Mediterranean / anti-inflammatory diet</strong> -- emphasize vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fish, and olive oil while reducing refined carbohydrate and processed foods.</li>
<li><strong>Optimize omega-3 intake</strong> -- EPA/DHA supplementation (commonly 1-2 g/day) can modestly lower inflammatory markers in adults with elevated inflammation.</li>
<li><strong>Consider curcumin</strong> -- curcumin (often with piperine for absorption) reduces CRP in several inflammatory and metabolic conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Correct nutrient deficiencies</strong> -- repleting low vitamin D and magnesium status may modestly reduce inflammation.</li>
<li><strong>Improve glycemic control and sleep</strong> -- stabilizing blood glucose and treating poor sleep both lower systemic inflammatory tone.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p>Note: statin therapy lowers hs-CRP independently of LDL and is a clinician-directed intervention, not a self-care strategy.</p>
</blockquote>
