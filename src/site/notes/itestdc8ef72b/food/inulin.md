<h1>Inulin</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Inulin is a soluble dietary fiber belonging to the fructan family — linear chains of fructose units linked by β(2→1) bonds, typically capped with a terminal glucose. It occurs naturally in chicory root (the main commercial source), Jerusalem artichoke, garlic, leeks, onions, asparagus, and bananas. Because the β(2→1) linkage is not cleaved by human digestive enzymes, inulin passes intact to the colon, where it is fermented by the resident microbiota — the defining feature of a prebiotic.</p>
<h2>Nutrient Profile</h2>
<p>Inulin contributes negligible digestible energy (~1.5 kcal/g) and has a glycemic index near zero, making it well tolerated by people managing blood glucose. Its functional value lies in being a fermentable substrate rather than a source of macronutrients. At higher intakes it can cause gas and bloating because of rapid colonic fermentation.</p>
<h2>Health Effects</h2>
<p>Colonic fermentation of inulin selectively stimulates <em>Bifidobacterium</em> and other saccharolytic taxa, yielding short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, and butyrate) that acidify the lumen, nourish colonocytes, and modulate host immune and metabolic signaling. Controlled trials report improvements in stool frequency, calcium absorption, and modest favorable shifts in glycemic and lipid markers, though effect sizes are variable and dose-dependent (Gibson; Valdes et al. 2018).</p>
