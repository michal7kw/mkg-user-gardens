<h1>Pyruvate Kinase</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Pyruvate kinase (PK) is the enzyme that catalyzes the tenth and final step of <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/pathway/glycolysis/" class="internal-link">Glycolysis</a>: the irreversible transfer of a phosphate group from phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/adp/" class="internal-link">ADP</a>, producing <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/pyruvate/" class="internal-link">Pyruvate</a> and <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> via substrate-level phosphorylation. As one of three irreversible, highly regulated steps of glycolysis (alongside <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/protein/hexokinase/" class="internal-link">Hexokinase</a> and <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/protein/phosphofructokinase/" class="internal-link">Phosphofructokinase</a>), it is a major control point governing carbon flux through the pathway. Four tissue-specific isoforms exist in mammals (PKL, PKR, PKM1, PKM2); the PKM2 isoform plays a key role in cancer metabolism and the Warburg effect.</p>
<h2>Mechanism of Action</h2>
<p>Pyruvate kinase transfers the high-energy phosphate of PEP to <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/adp/" class="internal-link">ADP</a>, generating <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> and an enolpyruvate intermediate that spontaneously tautomerizes to the more stable keto form of <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/pyruvate/" class="internal-link">Pyruvate</a>. Because the tautomerization is strongly exergonic, the overall reaction is highly favorable and effectively irreversible under cellular conditions — this is why <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/pathway/gluconeogenesis/" class="internal-link">Gluconeogenesis</a> must bypass the pyruvate kinase step (via pyruvate carboxylase and PEP carboxykinase) rather than simply running it in reverse.</p>
<p>The enzyme requires divalent <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/supplement/magnesium/" class="internal-link">Magnesium</a> (and monovalent K+) as cofactors for catalysis, coordinating the phosphoryl transfer in the active site. Activity is allosterically controlled: it is feed-forward <strong>activated</strong> by <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/fructose-16-bisphosphate/" class="internal-link">Fructose-1,6-Bisphosphate</a> (the product of the <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/protein/phosphofructokinase/" class="internal-link">Phosphofructokinase</a> step upstream), which couples the rate of glycolytic completion to upstream commitment. Conversely it is <strong>inhibited</strong> by <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> (signalling energy abundance) and, in the liver isoform, by alanine and by phosphorylation under glucagon/cAMP signalling.</p>
<h2>Role in Metabolism</h2>
<p>Pyruvate kinase sits at the junction between <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/pathway/glycolysis/" class="internal-link">Glycolysis</a> and the mitochondrial pathways of energy production. The <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/pyruvate/" class="internal-link">Pyruvate</a> it produces is the entry substrate for <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/protein/pyruvate-dehydrogenase/" class="internal-link">Pyruvate Dehydrogenase</a>, which converts it to <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/acetyl-coa/" class="internal-link">Acetyl-CoA</a> for the <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/pathway/citric-acid-cycle/" class="internal-link">Citric Acid Cycle</a>; alternatively, under anaerobic or high-flux conditions, pyruvate is reduced to <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/lactate/" class="internal-link">Lactate</a> to regenerate <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/nad/" class="internal-link">NAD+</a>. The <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a> generated here, together with that from the <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/protein/phosphofructokinase/" class="internal-link">Phosphofructokinase</a>/aldolase arm, represents the net ATP yield of glycolysis before <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/pathway/oxidative-phosphorylation/" class="internal-link">Oxidative Phosphorylation</a>.</p>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>Inherited deficiency of the red-cell isoform (PKR) is the most common enzymatic cause of hereditary non-spherocytic hemolytic anemia: erythrocytes, which depend entirely on glycolysis for <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/atp/" class="internal-link">ATP</a>, cannot maintain membrane integrity and are destroyed prematurely.</p>
<p>The <strong>PKM2</strong> isoform is central to the <strong>Warburg effect</strong> in cancer. Its lower, more regulatable activity lets proliferating tumor cells back up glycolytic intermediates for biosynthesis (nucleotides, amino acids, lipids) rather than fully oxidizing glucose, favoring aerobic glycolysis to <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/lactate/" class="internal-link">Lactate</a> even when <a href="/garden/itestdc8ef72b/metabolite/oxygen/" class="internal-link">Oxygen</a> is available. This metabolic rewiring makes PKM2 an actively studied oncology target.</p>
