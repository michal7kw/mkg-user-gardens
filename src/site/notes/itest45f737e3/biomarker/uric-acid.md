<h1>Uric Acid</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Uric acid is the terminal product of purine nucleotide catabolism in humans. Adenine- and guanine-derived purines are degraded to hypoxanthine and xanthine, which the enzyme xanthine oxidase (xanthine oxidoreductase) then oxidizes to uric acid. Unlike most mammals, humans and higher primates lack functional uricase (urate oxidase), so uric acid is the end point of the pathway rather than being further broken down to the highly soluble allantoin. As a result, humans maintain serum urate concentrations roughly an order of magnitude higher than uricase-competent species.</p>
<p>At physiological concentrations uric acid is one of the most abundant antioxidants in plasma, scavenging peroxynitrite, hydroxyl radicals, and singlet oxygen, and accounting for a substantial fraction of total plasma antioxidant capacity. This dual nature underlies the so-called &quot;urate paradox&quot;: modest levels may be protective, but chronically elevated levels become pathogenic. Serum urate reflects the balance between production (purine turnover, dietary purine and fructose intake, cell breakdown) and excretion, of which roughly two-thirds occurs through the kidneys (via the URAT1 and GLUT9 transporters) and one-third through the gut.</p>
<p>Clinically, uric acid is measured to diagnose and monitor gout, to assess risk of uric acid nephrolithiasis, to evaluate tumor lysis syndrome in oncology, and increasingly as a cardiometabolic risk marker. When serum urate exceeds its solubility limit of approximately 6.8 mg/dL, monosodium urate can crystallize in joints and soft tissues. It is an inexpensive, widely available test that complements creatinine, eGFR, and the metabolic panel in evaluating renal and metabolic health.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Most laboratories report in mg/dL; SI units are µmol/L (1 mg/dL ≈ 59.48 µmol/L).</li>
<li>Premenopausal women have lower values because estrogen promotes renal urate excretion; the sex gap narrows after menopause.</li>
<li>The &quot;gout treatment target&quot; in clinical guidelines is generally &lt; 6.0 mg/dL (or &lt; 5.0 mg/dL with tophi), which is lower than the upper limit of the population reference range.</li>
<li>Values rise transiently with dehydration, fasting, strenuous exercise, alcohol, and high-fructose intake.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Uric Acid:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>High-purine diet (organ meats, red meat, shellfish, anchovies, sardines)</li>
<li>High fructose intake (sweetened beverages — fructose metabolism consumes ATP and accelerates purine degradation)</li>
<li>Alcohol, especially beer and spirits (increases purine turnover and lactate-driven urate retention)</li>
<li>Dehydration and volume depletion (reduced renal clearance)</li>
<li>Thiazide and loop diuretics, low-dose aspirin, cyclosporine (reduce renal excretion)</li>
<li>Tumor lysis syndrome and rapid cell turnover (massive purine release)</li>
<li>Insulin resistance and obesity (hyperinsulinemia reduces renal urate excretion)</li>
<li>Renal impairment / chronic kidney disease (reduced clearance)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Uric Acid:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinol, febuxostat) reduce production</li>
<li>Uricosuric agents and losartan increase renal excretion</li>
<li>Vitamin C supplementation (mild uricosuric effect)</li>
<li>Estrogen (premenopausal women, hormone therapy)</li>
<li>Low-purine, low-fructose diet and weight loss</li>
<li>Adequate hydration and dairy (milk proteins are mildly uricosuric)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Gout / Gouty Arthritis</strong> — sustained hyperuricemia above the 6.8 mg/dL saturation point promotes monosodium urate crystal deposition, triggering acute inflammatory arthritis and, over time, tophi (Evidence Level 1a).</li>
<li><strong>Kidney Stones</strong> — uric acid is a primary constituent of urate stones and also promotes calcium oxalate stone formation, especially in acidic, concentrated urine (Evidence Level 1a).</li>
<li><strong>Chronic Kidney Disease</strong> — hyperuricemia is both a consequence of reduced clearance and a putative contributor to renal injury via endothelial dysfunction and afferent arteriolopathy (Evidence Level 2a).</li>
<li><strong>Hypertension</strong> — elevated urate is independently associated with incident hypertension, particularly in younger patients (Evidence Level 2a).</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular Disease</strong> — observational data link higher serum urate to coronary disease, heart failure, and mortality, though causality and the benefit of lowering urate for cardiovascular endpoints remain debated (Evidence Level 2a).</li>
<li><strong>Metabolic syndrome / Type 2 Diabetes / NAFLD</strong> — hyperuricemia clusters with insulin resistance, central obesity, dyslipidemia, and hepatic steatosis (Evidence Level 2b).</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Hereditary xanthine oxidase deficiency, Fanconi syndrome, and Wilson disease (renal urate wasting)</li>
<li>SIADH and overhydration</li>
<li>Uricosuric or high-dose salicylate therapy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Titrating urate-lowering therapy to a target of &lt; 6.0 mg/dL in gout</li>
<li>Surveillance for tumor lysis syndrome before and during chemotherapy</li>
<li>Cardiometabolic risk stratification alongside lipids, glucose, and renal markers</li>
<li>Evaluating recurrent nephrolithiasis</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Reduce dietary purine and fructose load</strong> — limit organ meats, shellfish, and sugar-sweetened beverages; emphasize vegetables, low-fat dairy, and whole grains.</li>
<li><strong>Moderate or eliminate alcohol</strong> — beer and spirits raise urate most; even moderate intake can trigger gout flares.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain hydration</strong> — adequate fluid intake supports renal urate clearance and reduces stone risk.</li>
<li><strong>Achieve and maintain a healthy weight</strong> — weight loss improves insulin sensitivity and lowers serum urate.</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C supplementation</strong> — roughly 500 mg/day produces a small but measurable reduction in serum urate via a mild uricosuric effect (modest as monotherapy).</li>
<li><strong>Tart cherry / cherry products</strong> — associated with reduced gout flare frequency and small reductions in serum urate.</li>
<li><strong>Urate-lowering medication when indicated</strong> — xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinol, febuxostat) for production, or uricosuric agents; losartan offers a uricosuric benefit in hypertensive patients. Pharmacologic therapy should be physician-directed.</li>
</ol>
