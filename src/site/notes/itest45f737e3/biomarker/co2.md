<h1>Co2</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>CO2 (carbon dioxide), measured on a standard metabolic panel, represents the total carbon dioxide content in venous blood, which is predominantly in the form of bicarbonate (HCO3-). Approximately 95% of total CO2 is bicarbonate; the remainder is dissolved CO2, carbonic acid, and carbamino compounds. This measurement reflects the body's acid-base buffering capacity via the bicarbonate-carbonic acid system (Henderson-Hasselbalch equation), which maintains blood pH within the narrow physiological range of 7.35-7.45. It is distinct from arterial blood gas pCO2, which measures dissolved CO2 gas and reflects respiratory function.</p>
<p>The kidneys and lungs work in concert to regulate blood CO2/bicarbonate levels. The kidneys control bicarbonate reabsorption (90% in proximal tubule) and regeneration (distal nephron), while the lungs regulate excretion of volatile CO2 through alveolar ventilation. Abnormalities in CO2 levels therefore point to dysfunction in one or both organ systems, or to metabolic disturbances that overwhelm the body's buffering capacity. CO2 is a fundamental component of the comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) and basic metabolic panel (BMP) -- among the most commonly ordered blood tests worldwide.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational clinical chemistry test; universal clinical use; WHO essential laboratory test).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Venous CO2 (metabolic panel) is typically 1-2 mEq/L higher than arterial bicarbonate</li>
<li>Mild variations with altitude (chronic respiratory alkalosis at high altitude lowers bicarbonate)</li>
<li>CO2 declines slightly with age</li>
<li>Pregnancy: CO2 declines by 2-4 mEq/L due to compensatory respiratory alkalosis for progesterone-driven hyperventilation</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase CO2 (metabolic alkalosis or compensated respiratory acidosis):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prolonged vomiting or nasogastric suction</strong> (loss of HCl)</li>
<li><strong>Diuretic use</strong> (thiazides, loop diuretics -- contraction alkalosis)</li>
<li><strong>Excessive bicarbonate or antacid ingestion</strong> (milk-alkali syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Cushing syndrome or corticosteroid therapy</strong> (mineralocorticoid effect)</li>
<li><strong>Severe hypokalemia</strong> (drives renal bicarbonate retention)</li>
<li><strong>Compensated chronic respiratory acidosis</strong> (COPD -- kidneys retain bicarbonate)</li>
<li><strong>Primary hyperaldosteronism</strong> (Conn syndrome)</li>
<li><strong>Bartter syndrome, Gitelman syndrome</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease CO2 (metabolic acidosis or compensated respiratory alkalosis):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)</strong></li>
<li><strong>Lactic acidosis</strong> (sepsis, shock, intense exercise, metformin)</li>
<li><strong>Renal tubular acidosis</strong> (types I, II, IV)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic kidney disease</strong> (impaired bicarbonate regeneration)</li>
<li><strong>Diarrhea</strong> (bicarbonate loss via GI tract)</li>
<li><strong>Methanol, ethylene glycol, or salicylate poisoning</strong> (increased anion gap)</li>
<li><strong>Compensated respiratory alkalosis</strong> (chronic hyperventilation; pregnancy; high altitude; anxiety)</li>
<li><strong>Acetazolamide</strong> (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor)</li>
<li><strong>Renal failure</strong> (uremic acid)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated CO2 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Metabolic alkalosis:</strong> vomiting, diuretics, antacid excess; check pH, pCO2 (ABG) for confirmation</li>
<li><strong>Chronic respiratory acidosis compensation</strong> (COPD, neuromuscular disease, obesity hypoventilation syndrome): kidneys retain bicarbonate to compensate for chronic CO2 retention</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Low CO2 is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Metabolic acidosis:</strong> DKA, lactic acidosis, renal failure, renal tubular acidosis, diarrhea, poisoning</li>
<li><strong>Anion gap metabolic acidosis:</strong> DKA, lactic acidosis, uremia, methanol, ethylene glycol</li>
<li><strong>Non-anion gap metabolic acidosis:</strong> renal tubular acidosis, diarrhea, saline resuscitation</li>
<li><strong>Chronic respiratory alkalosis compensation:</strong> pregnancy, high altitude, anxiety</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Venous CO2 is not a substitute for arterial blood gas (ABG)</strong> when precise acid-base diagnosis is needed</li>
<li><strong>Single value is non-specific</strong> -- interpret with clinical context, electrolytes (Na, K, Cl), and ABG if needed</li>
<li><strong>Anion gap calculation</strong> (Na - (Cl + HCO3)) is essential for differentiating causes of metabolic acidosis</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Routine health screening (with CMP/BMP)</li>
<li>Acid-base disturbance diagnosis and monitoring</li>
<li>DKA management (with glucose, ketones, ABG)</li>
<li>CKD progression (bicarbonate declines as eGFR falls; supplement when &lt;22 mEq/L)</li>
<li>Diuretic therapy monitoring</li>
<li>Critically ill patients (with ABG, lactate)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>CO2 reflects underlying acid-base status; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Metabolic acidosis treatment:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>DKA:</strong> IV fluids, insulin, electrolyte replacement; bicarbonate only if pH &lt;6.9</li>
<li><strong>Lactic acidosis:</strong> treat underlying cause (sepsis, shock, metformin)</li>
<li><strong>CKD-related acidosis:</strong> oral sodium bicarbonate 0.5-1 g TID; target CO2 &gt;=22 mEq/L (improves outcomes per KDIGO)</li>
<li><strong>Renal tubular acidosis:</strong> oral bicarbonate or citrate; type II requires large doses (10-15 mEq/kg/day)</li>
<li><strong>Diarrhea:</strong> treat underlying cause; IV bicarbonate if severe</li>
<li><strong>Toxic ingestion:</strong> specific antidotes (fomepizole for methanol/ethylene glycol; sodium bicarbonate for salicylates)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Metabolic alkalosis treatment:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vomiting/NG suction:</strong> IV normal saline; potassium replacement</li>
<li><strong>Diuretic-induced:</strong> reduce diuretic dose; potassium-sparing diuretic (spironolactone, amiloride); acetazolamide</li>
<li><strong>Hyperaldosteronism:</strong> spironolactone, eplerenone; adrenalectomy for adenoma</li>
<li><strong>Antacid excess:</strong> discontinue antacid; calcium and vitamin D for milk-alkali syndrome</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Chronic respiratory acidosis compensation (COPD):</strong>
<ul>
<li>Treat underlying lung disease (bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, oxygen if hypoxic)</li>
<li>Non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP) for chronic hypoventilation</li>
<li>Avoid excessive oxygen (can worsen CO2 retention in COPD)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Chronic respiratory alkalosis compensation (pregnancy, high altitude):</strong>
<ul>
<li>Usually no treatment needed (physiologic)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Acetazolamide</strong> for altitude sickness prevention (250 mg BID; start 24-48 h before ascent)</li>
</ol>
