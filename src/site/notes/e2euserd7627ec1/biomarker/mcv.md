<h1>Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV)</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV) is the average physical volume of a single mature red blood cell (erythrocyte), expressed in femtoliters (fL). It is calculated as <strong>MCV = Hematocrit (%) × 10 / RBC count (×10^6/uL)</strong>, or measured directly via automated hematology flow cytometry. MCV is the <strong>foundational branching point</strong> for any diagnostic algorithm confronting anemia -- when hemoglobin or RBC count is low, the MCV immediately filters the differential diagnosis into one of three categories: <strong>microcytic</strong> (&lt;80 fL), <strong>normocytic</strong> (80-100 fL), or <strong>macrocytic</strong> (&gt;100 fL).</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Microcytic anemia (MCV &lt;80 fL):</strong> indicates impaired hemoglobin synthesis -- most commonly from <strong>iron deficiency</strong> (the most common cause worldwide) or <strong>thalassemia</strong> (alpha or beta trait). Also anemia of chronic disease, sideroblastic anemia, lead poisoning.</li>
<li><strong>Normocytic anemia (MCV 80-100 fL):</strong> indicates bone marrow failure or peripheral RBC loss -- acute blood loss, hemolytic anemia, anemia of chronic disease, aplastic anemia, MDS, CKD.</li>
<li><strong>Macrocytic anemia (MCV &gt;100 fL):</strong> indicates defective DNA synthesis with continued cytoplasmic growth -- <strong>vitamin B12 deficiency</strong> (pernicious anemia, malabsorption), <strong>folate deficiency</strong>, liver disease, alcoholism, hypothyroidism, drug-induced (hydroxyurea, methotrexate, zidovudine).</li>
</ul>
<p>Because the lifespan of an RBC is ~120 days, MCV is <strong>not a volatile, rapidly shifting metric</strong>; it reflects a long-term rolling average of the bone marrow's red blood cell production environment over the last 3-4 months. MCV is reported on every standard CBC and is among the most commonly ordered hematologic tests.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (foundational CBC index; WHO essential).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference range is analyzer-specific (impedance vs flow cytometry methods differ slightly)</li>
<li>Newborns have higher MCV (~98-118 fL); declines to adult range by 6 months</li>
<li>Always interpret with hemoglobin, MCH, MCHC, RDW, peripheral smear</li>
<li>High reticulocyte count (hemolysis, recovery) raises MCV (reticulocytes are larger)</li>
<li>Cold agglutinins can falsely elevate MCV (warm sample and retest)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase MCV (macrocytosis):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 deficiency</strong> (pernicious anemia, malabsorption, gastric bypass, vegan diet, metformin)</li>
<li><strong>Folate deficiency</strong> (poor diet, alcoholism, malabsorption, pregnancy, methotrexate, phenytoin)</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease / cirrhosis / alcoholism</strong> (macrocytosis from altered lipid metabolism)</li>
<li><strong>Reticulocytosis</strong> (immature RBCs are larger; hemolytic anemia, acute blood loss recovery)</li>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism</strong></li>
<li><strong>Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)</strong></li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced:</strong> hydroxyurea, zidovudine (AZT), methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin, chemotherapy</li>
<li><strong>Aplastic anemia, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria</strong></li>
<li><strong>Cold agglutinin artifact</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease MCV (microcytosis):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency anemia</strong> (most common cause; dietary, malabsorption, blood loss)</li>
<li><strong>Thalassemia trait</strong> (alpha or beta; low MCV often &lt;75 fL)</li>
<li><strong>Sideroblastic anemia</strong> (impaired heme synthesis)</li>
<li><strong>Anemia of chronic disease</strong> (hepcidin-mediated iron sequestration; mild microcytosis)</li>
<li><strong>Lead poisoning</strong> (impaired heme synthesis)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic kidney disease</strong> (functional iron deficiency)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated MCV (&gt;100 fL, macrocytic anemia) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 deficiency:</strong> megaloblastic anemia; hypersegmented neutrophils, pancytopenia, neurologic symptoms (subacute combined degeneration, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive impairment)</li>
<li><strong>Folate deficiency:</strong> megaloblastic anemia; identical hematologic picture to B12 deficiency; no neurologic symptoms</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease / alcoholism:</strong> non-megaloblastic macrocytosis; target cells, acanthocytes</li>
<li><strong>Reticulocytosis:</strong> hemolytic anemia, acute blood loss recovery</li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced:</strong> hydroxyurea, zidovudine, methotrexate, chemotherapy</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced MCV (&lt;80 fL, microcytic anemia) is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Iron deficiency anemia:</strong> the most common cause of microcytic anemia worldwide</li>
<li><strong>Thalassemia trait:</strong> low MCV with high/normal RBC count; ethnic predisposition</li>
<li><strong>Anemia of chronic disease:</strong> low-normal MCV with high ferritin</li>
<li><strong>Sideroblastic anemia, lead poisoning</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Not independently diagnostic</strong> -- always interpret with hemoglobin, MCH, MCHC, RDW, ferritin, B12, folate, peripheral smear</li>
<li><strong>Reticulocytosis raises MCV</strong> (immature cells are larger)</li>
<li><strong>Cold agglutinins</strong> falsely elevate MCV</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Anemia classification (with MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW)</li>
<li>Iron deficiency anemia treatment monitoring (MCV rises with iron repletion over 4-8 weeks)</li>
<li>B12/folate deficiency treatment monitoring (MCV falls with repletion)</li>
<li>Thalassemia trait identification (with MCH, Hb electrophoresis)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>MCV reflects underlying erythropoiesis; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For microcytic anemia (iron deficiency):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Oral iron supplementation:</strong> ferrous sulfate 325 mg TID (65 mg elemental TID) on empty stomach; alternate-day dosing improves absorption</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C</strong> 200-500 mg with iron enhances non-heme iron absorption</li>
<li><strong>Heme-iron dietary emphasis:</strong> red meat, organ meats, shellfish</li>
<li><strong>Cast-iron cookware</strong></li>
<li><strong>Avoid calcium, coffee, tea, bran</strong> at the same meal as iron</li>
<li><strong>Investigate and treat underlying cause:</strong> celiac disease, H. pylori, menorrhagia, GI bleeding</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For macrocytic anemia (B12/folate deficiency):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 (methylcobalamin)</strong> 1000 mcg IM daily x 1 week, then weekly x 1 month, then monthly; or oral 1000-2000 mcg daily</li>
<li><strong>Folate (methylfolate preferred)</strong> 400-1000 mcg daily</li>
<li><strong>Always check B12 before treating with folate alone</strong> (folate can mask B12-deficiency anemia while allowing neurologic damage to progress)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For liver disease / alcohol-related macrocytosis:</strong> hepatology referral; alcohol cessation; nutritional support</li>
<li><strong>For thalassemia trait:</strong> no treatment needed (carrier state); genetic counseling</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue</strong> offending drugs (methotrexate, hydroxyurea) if possible</li>
</ol>
