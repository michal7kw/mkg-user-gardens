<h1>Kynurenic Acid</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Kynurenic acid (KYNA) is the neuroprotective counterpart of <a href="/garden/e2euser36c74751/metabolite/quinolinic-acid/" class="internal-link">Quinolinic Acid</a> within the <a href="/garden/e2euser36c74751/pathway/kynurenine-pathway/" class="internal-link">Kynurenine Pathway</a>. It is synthesized when kynurenine aminotransferases (KAT I-IV) — expressed mainly in astrocytes — transaminate <a href="/garden/e2euser36c74751/metabolite/kynurenine/" class="internal-link">Kynurenine</a>. Unlike the neurotoxic KMO branch, the KAT branch is a metabolic dead end that does not feed NAD+ synthesis; its products are instead exported to modulate neurotransmission.</p>
<p>KYNA is broadly inhibitory at excitatory receptors, which gives it endogenous neuroprotective and anticonvulsant properties. However, the relationship between KYNA and brain function is U-shaped: too little permits excitotoxicity, while chronically elevated KYNA impairs glutamatergic and cholinergic signaling and is associated with cognitive deficits and schizophrenia.</p>

<h2>Formation</h2>
<p>Kynurenine + KAT (I-IV) → kynurenic acid. Astrocytic localization of the KATs spatially separates KYNA production from the microglial, neurotoxic quinolinic-acid branch — a key determinant of the pathway's net effect on neurons.</p>
<h2>Neuropharmacology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>NMDA-receptor antagonism:</strong> KYNA blocks the obligatory glycine co-agonist site of the NMDA receptor, dampening <a href="/garden/e2euser36c74751/neurotransmitter/glutamate/" class="internal-link">Glutamate</a>-mediated excitation and protecting against excitotoxicity.</li>
<li><strong>Alpha7-nicotinic antagonism:</strong> KYNA non-competitively inhibits the alpha7-nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, modulating <a href="/garden/e2euser36c74751/neurotransmitter/acetylcholine/" class="internal-link">Acetylcholine</a> signaling — a mechanism linked to cognitive and sensory-gating effects.</li>
<li><strong>GPR35 / AhR signaling:</strong> KYNA is an agonist of the orphan receptor GPR35 and a ligand of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, contributing to anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects in the periphery.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p>KYNA is broadly neuroprotective and anticonvulsant at physiological levels. Elevated brain KYNA is consistently reported in schizophrenia, where it is thought to contribute to NMDA hypofunction and cognitive impairment. Conversely, a deficient KYNA/quinolinic-acid balance is implicated in the excitotoxic component of <a href="/garden/e2euser36c74751/condition/alzheimers-disease/" class="internal-link">Alzheimer's Disease</a> and <a href="/garden/e2euser36c74751/condition/parkinsons-disease/" class="internal-link">Parkinson's Disease</a>. KYNA levels are modulated by exercise (PGC-1alpha-driven KAT expression in muscle) and diet.</p>
