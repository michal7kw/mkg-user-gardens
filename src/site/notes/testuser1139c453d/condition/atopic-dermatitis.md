<h1>Atopic Dermatitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Atopic dermatitis (AD), commonly called eczema, is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease and the most prevalent chronic skin condition in children. It is defined clinically by intense pruritus and recurrent eczematous lesions in a characteristic, age-dependent distribution. AD is the cutaneous expression of the broader &quot;atopic&quot; tendency and frequently coexists with—or precedes—allergic rhinitis, food allergy, and asthma.</p>
<p>The disease arises from the interplay of two converging defects: a <strong>dysfunctional epidermal barrier</strong> and a <strong>dysregulated type 2 immune response</strong>. Loss-of-function mutations in the filaggrin gene (FLG) are the strongest known genetic risk factor; impaired filaggrin reduces the integrity of the stratum corneum, increasing transepidermal water loss and allowing allergens and microbes to penetrate. This barrier breach drives a Th2-skewed inflammatory cascade (IL-4, IL-13, IL-31, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin) that produces itch, inflammation, and further barrier damage in a self-perpetuating cycle.</p>
<p>Clinically, AD matters because of its high prevalence, substantial impact on quality of life and sleep, and its role as the typical first step in the &quot;atopic march.&quot; Modern management has shifted from symptom suppression alone toward barrier restoration and targeted immunomodulation, with biologics and JAK inhibitors transforming care of moderate-to-severe disease.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>AD presents differently across the lifespan. In <strong>infants</strong>, lesions favor the face, scalp, and extensor surfaces. In <strong>children and adults</strong>, disease localizes to the flexural folds (antecubital and popliteal fossae, neck, wrists). Hallmarks include intense pruritus (often the most distressing symptom), xerosis, erythema, weeping and crusting during flares, and lichenification with chronicity. Secondary infection with <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em> is common and can trigger flares.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis is <strong>clinical</strong>, based on recognized criteria sets (Hanifin and Rajka; UK Working Party). Essential features are pruritus plus a typical morphology and distribution, a chronic relapsing course, and a personal or family history of atopy. There is no single diagnostic laboratory test, although allergen-specific IgE testing can help characterize concurrent sensitization and identify triggers in selected patients.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The dominant genetic contributor is FLG loss-of-function variation, alongside other barrier and immune polymorphisms. Environmental contributors include irritant exposure, low humidity, reduced early-life microbial diversity, and concurrent allergen sensitization. A strong family history of atopic disease markedly increases risk.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>The cornerstone of treatment is <strong>liberal daily emollient use</strong> to repair and maintain the skin barrier, combined with trigger avoidance and gentle skin care. Flares are treated with topical corticosteroids or topical calcineurin inhibitors. Moderate-to-severe or refractory disease is increasingly managed with targeted systemic therapy—biologics blocking IL-4/IL-13 signaling (e.g., dupilumab) and oral or topical JAK inhibitors—which directly interrupt the type 2 inflammatory axis.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Many children experience improvement or remission by adolescence, though a significant minority have persistent or adult-onset disease. Prognosis is influenced by severity, FLG genotype, and the degree of concurrent atopic sensitization. Even when skin disease resolves, the underlying atopic predisposition often manifests as respiratory allergy.</p>
