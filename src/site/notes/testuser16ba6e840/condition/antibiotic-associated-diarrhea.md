<h1>Antibiotic-Associated Diarrhea</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD) is diarrhea that occurs in temporal relationship to antibiotic therapy and has no other identifiable cause. It is one of the most common adverse effects of antimicrobial treatment, complicating an estimated 5-25% of antibiotic courses depending on the agent, the population, and the care setting. By definition the diarrhea may begin during treatment or appear weeks after the antibiotic has been stopped — conventionally up to about eight weeks.</p>
<p>The underlying mechanism is disruption of the normal colonic microbiota. Antibiotics reduce microbial diversity and impair colonization resistance and carbohydrate fermentation, which can produce an osmotic, functional diarrhea, and — more importantly — permits overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens. The most clinically significant of these is <em>Clostridioides difficile</em>, a toxin-producing, spore-forming anaerobe that accounts for roughly 10-20% of AAD cases and the great majority of its severe presentations, including pseudomembranous colitis.</p>
<p>Clinically, AAD spans a wide spectrum. Many patients have only mild, self-limited diarrhea that resolves once the antibiotic is stopped, requiring nothing more than supportive care. At the other extreme, <em>C. difficile</em> infection can progress to fulminant colitis, toxic megacolon, and death, and is prone to recurrence. Distinguishing simple AAD from <em>C. difficile</em> infection — using clinical severity and stool testing — is the central diagnostic task because it determines whether targeted antimicrobial therapy is needed.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Simple AAD presents as mild-to-moderate watery diarrhea without systemic features, often with cramping and bloating, and typically improves within days of stopping the antibiotic. Features that raise concern for <em>C. difficile</em> infection include fever, leukocytosis, lower abdominal pain, more frequent and voluminous stools, and a recent or current course of a high-risk antibiotic. Severe disease may manifest with marked leukocytosis, hypoalbuminemia, hemodynamic instability, and signs of colitis.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>AAD is recognized clinically by the temporal link to antibiotic exposure. When <em>C. difficile</em> infection is suspected — on the basis of severity, fever, or risk profile — stool testing is performed, commonly a two-step algorithm combining glutamate dehydrogenase antigen and toxin immunoassay with nucleic acid amplification (PCR) as needed. Endoscopic identification of pseudomembranes confirms pseudomembranous colitis but is not routinely required.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>The strongest risk factor is exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, classically clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, and broad-spectrum penicillins. Patient-level risks include advanced age, hospitalization, prolonged or repeated antibiotic courses, proton pump inhibitor use, recent gastrointestinal surgery, and significant comorbidity.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>The first step is to stop or narrow the inciting antibiotic whenever clinically feasible, alongside oral rehydration. Mild simple AAD usually needs only supportive care. Confirmed or strongly suspected <em>C. difficile</em> infection is treated with oral <strong>fidaxomicin</strong> or <strong>vancomycin</strong>; metronidazole is now reserved for limited situations. Antimotility agents should be avoided when <em>C. difficile</em> colitis is suspected because of the risk of toxic megacolon. For recurrent <em>C. difficile</em> infection, fecal microbiota transplantation is highly effective at restoring colonization resistance.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The dominant complication is <em>C. difficile</em> infection with pseudomembranous colitis, which can escalate to toxic megacolon and bowel perforation in fulminant cases. Recurrence after initial treatment is common. Across the spectrum, dehydration and electrolyte disturbance are the main acute risks.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>Simple AAD has an excellent prognosis and resolves with antibiotic cessation. <em>C. difficile</em> infection carries a more guarded prognosis, with appreciable recurrence and, in severe disease, significant mortality — particularly in elderly and comorbid patients.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>AAD is among the most frequent drug-related gastrointestinal adverse events. <em>C. difficile</em> is the leading recognized infectious cause and a major healthcare-associated pathogen, with incidence influenced by antibiotic stewardship, infection-control practices, and host vulnerability.</p>
