<h1>Renal Colic</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Renal colic refers to the acute, severe pain caused by a <strong>kidney stone (urolithiasis)</strong> obstructing the urinary tract. The pain is typically sudden in onset, excruciating, and classically <strong>radiates from the flank to the groin</strong> (following the distribution of the ureter). Patients with renal colic are often writhing and unable to find a comfortable position -- in contrast to patients with peritonitis who lie still. It is one of the most painful non-surgical conditions, with patients frequently rating the pain as worse than childbirth.</p>
<p>Kidney stones affect approximately <strong>1 in 11 people</strong> in the United States, with a lifetime risk of ~12% in men and ~7% in women. The recurrence rate is <strong>~50% at 5 years</strong> and ~75% at 10 years without preventive measures.</p>
<p><strong>Stone types:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Calcium oxalate</strong> (~70-80%; most common)</li>
<li><strong>Calcium phosphate</strong> (~10%)</li>
<li><strong>Uric acid</strong> (~5-10%; acidic urine; radiolucent on X-ray)</li>
<li><strong>Struvite</strong> (~5%; magnesium ammonium phosphate; &quot;infection stones&quot;; associated with urease-producing bacteria like Proteus)</li>
<li><strong>Cystine</strong> (~1%; cystinuria; genetic)</li>
</ul>
<p>Stone size is the most important predictor of spontaneous passage:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>&lt;5 mm:</strong> ~70-80% pass spontaneously</li>
<li><strong>5-10 mm:</strong> ~50% pass spontaneously</li>
<li><strong>&gt;10 mm:</strong> &lt;10% pass spontaneously; usually require intervention</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p><strong>Classic symptoms:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sudden, severe flank pain</strong> (unilateral; often waking from sleep)</li>
<li><strong>Radiating pain</strong> from flank to lower abdomen, groin, testicle, or labia (following ureter course)</li>
<li><strong>Colicky</strong> (comes in waves; fluctuates in intensity)</li>
<li><strong>Patient writhing</strong> (unable to find comfortable position; key differentiator from peritonitis)</li>
<li><strong>Nausea and vomiting</strong> (common; from visceral afferent stimulation)</li>
<li><strong>Hematuria</strong> (gross or microscopic; from ureteral trauma)</li>
<li><strong>Dysuria, urgency, frequency</strong> (if stone near the ureterovesical junction)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Red flags (urgent urology referral):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fever and sepsis</strong> (indicates obstructed infected system; requires immediate decompression)</li>
<li><strong>Solitary kidney with obstruction</strong> (risk of AKI)</li>
<li><strong>Bilateral obstruction</strong> (anuria, AKI)</li>
<li><strong>Intractable pain or vomiting</strong></li>
</ul>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p><strong>Urinalysis:</strong> hematuria (microscopic or gross); leukocyte esterase/nitrite if concurrent UTI; pH (acidic favors uric acid; alkaline favors struvite)<br />
<strong>CT abdomen/pelvis without contrast</strong> (gold standard; sensitivity &gt;95%; identifies stone size, location, density/HU, hydronephrosis)<br />
<strong>Kidney-ureter-bladder (KUB) X-ray</strong> (identifies radiopaque stones; calcium and struvite visible; uric acid and cystine radiolucent)<br />
<strong>Ultrasound</strong> (first-line in pregnancy and children; shows hydronephrosis; less sensitive for ureteral stones)<br />
<strong>Blood tests:</strong> CBC (leukocytosis if infection), creatinine (AKI if obstruction), electrolytes, uric acid, calcium (hyperparathyroidism screen)</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p><strong>Analgesia:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>NSAIDs</strong> (first-line): diclofenac 75 mg IM/IV, ketorolac 30 mg IV, ibuprofen 600 mg PO</li>
<li><strong>Opioids</strong> (if NSAIDs insufficient): morphine 5-10 mg IV, fentanyl 50-100 mcg IV, or tramadol</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Medical expulsive therapy (MET):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tamsulosin</strong> 0.4 mg daily for 2-4 weeks (alpha-1 blocker; relaxes ureteral smooth muscle; modest benefit for stones 5-10 mm)</li>
<li>Evidence is mixed (some recent RCTs show limited benefit)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Hydration:</strong> oral or IV fluids (do not over-hydrate; does not accelerate stone passage)</p>
<p><strong>Surgical intervention:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ESWL</strong> (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy): for stones &lt;2 cm in renal or upper ureter</li>
<li><strong>Ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy:</strong> for stones in lower/mid ureter, larger stones, or ESWL failure</li>
<li><strong>Percutaneous nephrostomy</strong> or <strong>stent placement:</strong> emergency decompression for obstructed infected system (pyonephrosis) or AKI</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Prevention of recurrence:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hydration</strong> (&gt;2-3 L/day; goal urine output &gt;2.5 L/day)</li>
<li><strong>Reduce dietary sodium</strong> (&lt;2.3 g/day)</li>
<li><strong>Adequate dietary calcium</strong> (1000-1200 mg/day; paradoxically reduces oxalate absorption)</li>
<li><strong>Reduce animal protein</strong> (if uric acid or calcium stones)</li>
<li><strong>Potassium citrate</strong> (for hypocitraturia; uric acid stones)</li>
<li><strong>Thiazide diuretics</strong> (for hypercalciuria)</li>
<li><strong>Allopurinol</strong> (for hyperuricosuric calcium stones or uric acid stones)</li>
</ul>
