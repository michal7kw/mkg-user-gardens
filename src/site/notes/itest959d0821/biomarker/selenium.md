<h1>Selenium</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Selenium is an essential trace mineral incorporated into proteins as the 21st amino acid, selenocysteine. Serum or plasma selenium concentration is the most widely used biomarker of short-to-medium-term selenium intake and status. The element's biological activity is mediated almost entirely through ~25 selenoproteins, of which the glutathione peroxidase family (GPx1-4) and thioredoxin reductases provide front-line defense against hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides, while the three iodothyronine deiodinases convert the prohormone thyroxine (T4) into the active hormone triiodothyronine (T3) and degrade reverse T3.</p>
<p>Selenium is absorbed efficiently in the duodenum, transported in plasma largely bound to selenoprotein P (the principal selenium transport and storage protein, synthesized by the liver), and excreted mainly in urine. Serum selenium responds to dietary intake and supplementation but plateaus once selenoprotein synthesis is saturated, so very high intakes raise serum levels without proportionate functional benefit. Major dietary sources include Brazil nuts (~70-90 mcg per nut), seafood, organ meats, eggs, and cereals grown in selenium-replete soils; geographic soil content drives wide population differences in status, with notably low-selenium belts in parts of China, New Zealand, and parts of Europe.</p>
<p>Clinically, serum selenium is measured to investigate suspected deficiency (in malnutrition, malabsorption, prolonged parenteral nutrition, or endemic-deficient regions), to evaluate autoimmune and other thyroid disorders, to monitor supplementation, and to confirm suspected toxicity (selenosis). Both ends of the distribution carry risk: deficiency is linked to Keshan and Kashin-Beck diseases and impaired immunity, while chronic excess produces selenosis and has been associated in some trials with increased type 2 diabetes risk. The US RDA is 55 mcg/day for adults; the tolerable upper intake level is 400 mcg/day.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Plateau effect: glutathione peroxidase activity is optimized at serum levels around 90-100 mcg/L and selenoprotein P somewhat higher (~110-120 mcg/L); intakes that push serum above the optimal band provide little additional antioxidant benefit.</li>
<li>Assay method matters: ICP-MS and graphite-furnace atomic absorption spectrometry are standard; results vary modestly between laboratories, so trend within one lab is more informative than absolute cutoffs.</li>
<li>Reference ranges are population- and region-dependent because soil selenium drives baseline intake; New Zealand and parts of Europe sit lower than the US.</li>
<li>Serum/plasma reflects recent status; toenail selenium reflects longer-term (months) exposure and is used in epidemiological studies.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Selenium:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Selenium supplementation (selenomethionine, sodium selenite, selenium-enriched yeast)</li>
<li>High dietary intake (Brazil nuts, seafood, organ meats, cereals from selenium-rich soil)</li>
<li>Living in selenium-replete geographic regions</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Selenium:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Low dietary intake / residence in selenium-poor soil regions</li>
<li>Malabsorption syndromes (Crohn's disease, celiac disease, short-bowel syndrome)</li>
<li>Prolonged total parenteral nutrition without selenium supplementation</li>
<li>Chronic systemic inflammation and critical illness (selenium acts as a negative acute-phase reactant)</li>
<li>Advanced liver disease (reduced selenoprotein P synthesis)</li>
<li>Hemodialysis and chronic kidney disease</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Keshan disease</strong> -- an endemic, often fatal dilated cardiomyopathy in selenium-deficient regions of China, mechanistically tied to low selenoprotein P and impaired antioxidant defense; supplementation programs sharply reduced incidence.</li>
<li><strong>Kashin-Beck disease</strong> -- a deforming osteoarthropathy occurring in selenium- (and iodine-) deficient areas.</li>
<li><strong>Autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's)</strong> -- low selenium impairs deiodinase and antioxidant selenoprotein function; supplementation can lower thyroid peroxidase antibody titers in selected patients.</li>
<li><strong>Hypothyroidism and impaired thyroid hormone conversion</strong> -- deficient deiodinase activity reduces peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion.</li>
<li><strong>Impaired immune function and increased infection susceptibility</strong> -- selenoproteins modulate T-cell and innate immune responses.</li>
<li><strong>Increased oxidative stress</strong> -- reduced glutathione peroxidase capacity raises lipid peroxidation.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Selenosis (chronic toxicity)</strong> -- garlic breath odor, hair loss, brittle/streaked nails, peripheral neuropathy, fatigue, and GI distress, typically from chronic intake above 400 mcg/day.</li>
<li><strong>Increased type 2 diabetes risk</strong> -- some randomized trials in selenium-replete populations linked high-dose supplementation to higher diabetes incidence.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Assessment of suspected deficiency in malabsorption, parenteral nutrition, or critical illness</li>
<li>Adjunctive evaluation of autoimmune thyroid disease</li>
<li>Confirmation and follow-up of suspected selenium toxicity</li>
<li>Verifying adequacy and avoiding overshoot during supplementation</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Selenium supplementation</strong> -- 100-200 mcg/day of selenomethionine or selenium-enriched yeast restores low status; avoid sustained intake above the 400 mcg/day upper limit to prevent selenosis.</li>
<li><strong>Dietary correction</strong> -- one to two Brazil nuts daily provides ~70-180 mcg; seafood, organ meats, and eggs are reliable food sources.</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying malabsorption</strong> -- managing celiac, Crohn's, or short-bowel disease improves selenium uptake.</li>
<li><strong>Ensure selenium in parenteral nutrition</strong> -- patients on long-term TPN require explicit selenium supplementation.</li>
<li><strong>Pair with adequate iodine in thyroid contexts</strong> -- selenium and iodine status interact in thyroid hormone metabolism; correct both rather than selenium alone.</li>
<li><strong>Avoid over-supplementation in selenium-replete individuals</strong> -- additional selenium offers no antioxidant benefit once selenoproteins are saturated and may raise diabetes risk.</li>
</ol>
