<h1>Post-Herpetic Neuralgia</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a chronic neuropathic pain syndrome that follows an episode of herpes zoster (shingles). It is most commonly defined as pain that persists in the affected dermatome for at least three months after the acute zoster rash has healed. PHN is the most frequent and most feared complication of zoster, and it can be profoundly disabling, eroding sleep, mood, and daily function — especially in older adults, who are both more likely to develop it and more likely to suffer prolonged courses.</p>
<p>The condition arises from damage inflicted by varicella-zoster virus reactivation on peripheral sensory nerves, dorsal root ganglia, and central pain-processing pathways. This injury produces a mix of peripheral sensitization (spontaneously firing, damaged nociceptors) and central sensitization (amplified spinal and supraspinal pain signaling), which together explain the syndrome's characteristic features: spontaneous burning pain, paroxysmal shock-like jolts, and allodynia in which ordinarily innocuous stimuli such as clothing or a light breeze provoke pain.</p>
<p>Because PHN is difficult to treat once established, the emphasis has shifted strongly toward prevention — through vaccination against zoster and prompt antiviral treatment of the acute episode — and toward early, multimodal pain management. No single therapy is uniformly effective, and care often combines pharmacologic, topical, and, in refractory cases, interventional approaches.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>Patients describe persistent pain confined to the dermatome of their prior shingles outbreak. The pain has several overlapping qualities: a constant burning or aching component, intermittent sharp or lancinating jolts, and prominent allodynia and hyperalgesia. The overlying skin may be scarred or show pigment change from the healed rash. The chronic, unrelenting nature of the pain frequently leads to sleep disruption, fatigue, anxiety, and depression.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>PHN is a clinical diagnosis based on neuropathic pain persisting for three months or more after a healed zoster rash, localized to the involved dermatome, in a patient with a documented or clearly recalled prior episode of shingles. The principal task is to exclude other regional pain causes; confirmatory testing is rarely needed.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Age is the dominant risk factor — PHN is uncommon under 50 and increasingly likely with each decade thereafter. Other predictors include greater severity of acute zoster pain, a more extensive or severe rash, the presence of a painful prodrome, and ophthalmic involvement.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Management is multimodal and individualized. First-line oral agents are the gabapentinoids gabapentin and pregabalin and the tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline. Topical therapies — lidocaine patches and high-concentration capsaicin patches — are effective and well tolerated, particularly in localized disease and in older patients who tolerate systemic drugs poorly. Opioids are reserved for refractory pain because of their adverse-effect and dependence profile. Interventional options including nerve blocks and spinal cord stimulation are considered for severe, treatment-resistant cases. Prevention via the recombinant zoster vaccine and prompt antiviral therapy of acute zoster substantially reduces PHN risk.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>The principal burdens are the pain itself and its downstream effects: chronic disability, depression and anxiety, sleep disturbance, social isolation, and functional decline. In the elderly, these can accelerate loss of independence.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>For most patients PHN gradually improves over the course of months, with many achieving substantial relief within a year. A minority, however, endure pain that persists for years and is incompletely responsive to treatment, underscoring the value of prevention.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>PHN complicates a clinically significant fraction of zoster cases, with risk rising steeply with age; older adults account for the great majority of persistent cases. The widespread adoption of the recombinant zoster vaccine is expected to lower its population burden.</p>
