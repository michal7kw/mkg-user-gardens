<h1>DHEA-S</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) is the sulfated, long-half-life (7-10 h vs 30 min for unsulfated DHEA) form of DHEA, the most abundant circulating steroid hormone in humans. DHEA-S is synthesized primarily by the <strong>zona reticularis</strong> of the adrenal cortex (with small contributions from the gonads and brain), and serves as an inactive circulating reservoir that peripheral tissues convert locally into androgens (testosterone, dihydrotestosterone) and estrogens (estradiol, estrone) via the enzyme steroid sulfatase. This &quot;intracrinology&quot; -- local tissue-specific conversion -- allows tissues like prostate, breast, brain, and bone to autoregulate their sex steroid exposure independently of circulating levels.</p>
<p>DHEA-S levels follow a distinctive age-related pattern: they rise during adrenarche (age 6-8), peak in the mid-20s (men ~400-500 mcg/dL; women ~250-300 mcg/dL), and decline progressively at a rate of ~2-3% per year, such that by age 70-80 levels are only 10-20% of peak values. This age-related decline -- termed &quot;adrenopause&quot; -- has been the subject of considerable research regarding its role in aging, immune function, cognitive decline, bone density, cardiovascular health, and frailty. DHEA-S is often considered a <strong>biomarker of biological aging</strong> and adrenal reserve, though its direct causal role in aging processes remains debated.</p>
<p>Clinically, DHEA-S is the principal biomarker of adrenal androgen production. Its principal clinical uses: (1) <strong>androgen excess workup in women</strong> -- hirsutism, acne, virilization, oligomenorrhea; elevated DHEA-S specifically points to an adrenal source, helping distinguish from ovarian causes (PCOS); (2) <strong>adrenal tumor screening</strong> -- markedly elevated DHEA-S (&gt;700 mcg/dL) suggests adrenal carcinoma or virilizing adenoma; (3) <strong>congenital adrenal hyperplasia</strong> -- elevated in 21-hydroxylase and 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency; (4) <strong>adrenal insufficiency</strong> -- low DHEA-S supports diagnosis of primary or secondary adrenal insufficiency; (5) <strong>functional medicine / anti-aging</strong> -- DHEA-S monitored in longevity panels and DHEA supplementation protocols.</p>
<p>Because DHEA-S is produced almost exclusively by the adrenal glands (with minimal gonadal contribution), an elevated DHEA-S specifically indicates an <strong>adrenal</strong> source of excess androgen, helping distinguish from ovarian causes of hyperandrogenism (PCOS, ovarian tumor). A normal DHEA-S with elevated testosterone suggests an ovarian source; an elevated DHEA-S suggests adrenal.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence Level: 5/5</strong> (universally measured; standard endocrine workup component).</p>

<p><strong>Caveats:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Strongly age-dependent</strong> -- always interpret with age-specific reference range</li>
<li><strong>Diurnal variation</strong> modest (less than cortisol); morning preferred but not strictly required</li>
<li><strong>Pregnancy</strong> lowers DHEA-S (increased maternal clearance and placental utilization)</li>
<li><strong>Estrogen therapy</strong> raises DHEA-S slightly (estrogen inhibits hepatic clearance)</li>
<li><strong>Reference ranges vary significantly across labs</strong> -- always use lab-specific reference</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase DHEA-S:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adrenal hyperandrogenism</strong> (the principal cause of marked elevation)</li>
<li><strong>Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS):</strong> modest elevation (~25-50% of cases); helps distinguish adrenal PCOS from ovarian</li>
<li><strong>Congenital adrenal hyperplasia:</strong> 21-hydroxylase deficiency (classical and non-classical), 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency, 3-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase deficiency</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal tumors:</strong> virilizing adenoma or carcinoma -- markedly elevated &gt;700 mcg/dL is essentially diagnostic; rule out with CT/MRI</li>
<li><strong>Cushing disease</strong> (ACTH-dependent -- bilateral adrenal hyperplasia drives DHEA-S)</li>
<li><strong>Exogenous DHEA supplementation</strong></li>
<li><strong>Premature adrenarche</strong> (children)</li>
<li><strong>Acromegaly</strong> (GH stimulates adrenal androgen production)</li>
<li><strong>Hyperprolactinemia</strong></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease DHEA-S:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adrenal insufficiency</strong> (primary autoimmune / Addison's; or secondary hypopituitarism)</li>
<li><strong>Hypopituitarism / panhypopituitarism</strong></li>
<li><strong>Normal aging</strong> (adrenopause) -- the principal cause of low DHEA-S in elderly</li>
<li><strong>Chronic glucocorticoid therapy</strong> (adrenal suppression via HPA-axis suppression)</li>
<li><strong>Chronic illness, critical illness, severe stress</strong> (transient suppression)</li>
<li><strong>Anorexia nervosa</strong></li>
<li><strong>Adrenal hemorrhage / Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome</strong></li>
<li><strong>Drugs:</strong> exogenous corticosteroids, ketoconazole, metyrapone, mitotane (adrenal enzyme inhibitors)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated DHEA-S is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adrenal source of hyperandrogenism:</strong> the principal clinical use; helps distinguish from ovarian causes</li>
<li><strong>PCOS:</strong> modest elevation in ~25-50% of cases; usually &lt;500 mcg/dL in PCOS; levels &gt;700 mcg/dL suggest adrenal tumor</li>
<li><strong>Congenital adrenal hyperplasia:</strong> markedly elevated in 21-hydroxylase deficiency (especially classical); the principal screening test for non-classical CAH</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal carcinoma:</strong> markedly elevated (&gt;700 mcg/dL) is highly suggestive; confirm with CT/MRI; check androgen and cortisol co-secretion</li>
<li><strong>Adrenal adenoma (virilizing):</strong> variable elevation; often less than carcinoma</li>
<li><strong>Cushing disease (ACTH-driven):</strong> DHEA-S may be elevated; ACTH-independent Cushing (adrenal adenoma) typically has LOW DHEA-S</li>
<li><strong>Premature adrenarche</strong> (children): DHEA-S elevated before age 8 with pubic/axillary hair development</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Low DHEA-S is associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adrenal insufficiency:</strong> low DHEA-S supports diagnosis of primary (Addison's) or secondary (hypopituitarism) adrenal insufficiency; the basal morning DHEA-S has high sensitivity for adrenal insufficiency in elderly</li>
<li><strong>Hypopituitarism</strong></li>
<li><strong>Normal aging</strong> (adrenopause) -- DHEA-S declines progressively with age; low values in elderly may not be pathological</li>
<li><strong>Chronic glucocorticoid therapy:</strong> DHEA-S suppressed within weeks; recover on cessation</li>
<li><strong>Increased all-cause mortality:</strong> low DHEA-S in elderly predicts mortality in observational cohorts</li>
<li><strong>Cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, depression, cognitive decline:</strong> low DHEA-S associated with these in observational studies (causal role uncertain)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limitations:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Strongly age-dependent</strong> -- always interpret with age-specific reference range</li>
<li><strong>DHEA-S is not bioactive</strong> -- it must be converted to DHEA then to androgens/estrogens peripherally; levels do not directly reflect tissue androgen/estrogen action</li>
<li><strong>DHEA-S decline with age is normal</strong> -- supplementation in elderly with low DHEA-S has shown mixed results in trials</li>
<li><strong>Functional medicine popularity</strong> despite limited outcome evidence</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Androgen excess workup (with total/free testosterone, LH, FSH, SHBG)</li>
<li>PCOS evaluation (with LH, FSH, androgen panel)</li>
<li>Congenital adrenal hyperplasia screening</li>
<li>Adrenal tumor evaluation</li>
<li>Adrenal insufficiency workup (with morning cortisol, ACTH stimulation test)</li>
<li>Functional medicine / anti-aging monitoring of DHEA supplementation</li>
<li>Research: aging biomarker</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Manage</h2>
<p>DHEA-S levels reflect adrenal androgen production; management targets the cause:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>For elevated DHEA-S:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adrenal tumor:</strong> surgical resection (adrenalectomy); oncology follow-up for carcinoma</li>
<li><strong>CAH (21-hydroxylase deficiency):</strong> hydrocortisone (glucocorticoid replacement) suppresses ACTH and adrenal androgen production; mineralocorticoid (fludrocortisone) for classical CAH</li>
<li><strong>PCOS:</strong> combined oral contraceptives suppress ovarian and adrenal androgens; spironolactone (androgen receptor blocker); lifestyle (weight loss improves insulin sensitivity, reduces androgen production)</li>
<li><strong>Cushing disease:</strong> transsphenoidal pituitary surgery; ketoconazole, metyrapone for medical management</li>
<li><strong>Avoid exogenous DHEA</strong> supplementation</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>For low DHEA-S (adrenal insufficiency):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's):</strong> hydrocortisone 15-25 mg/day + fludrocortisone 0.05-0.2 mg/day</li>
<li><strong>Secondary (hypopituitarism):</strong> hydrocortisone replacement; treat underlying pituitary cause</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or discontinue chronic glucocorticoids</strong> if possible (HPA-axis recovery takes months)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>DHEA supplementation</strong> (functional medicine / anti-aging; off-label in most countries except US where DHEA is OTC):
<ul>
<li><strong>Dose:</strong> 25-50 mg/day oral (women), 50-100 mg/day (men); start low and titrate</li>
<li><strong>Indications:</strong> controversial; some evidence for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and depression; mixed evidence for aging, cognition, bone density, sexual function</li>
<li><strong>Risks:</strong> androgenization in women (acne, hirsutism, voice deepening), potential hormone-sensitive cancer risk, liver toxicity (rare)</li>
<li><strong>Monitor:</strong> DHEA-S, testosterone, estradiol, lipid panel, LFTs at baseline and 3 months</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Lifestyle interventions (observational evidence):</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Regular aerobic exercise</strong> slows age-related DHEA-S decline</li>
<li><strong>Caloric restriction</strong> (animal studies; mixed human evidence)</li>
<li><strong>Stress management</strong> (chronic cortisol elevation suppresses DHEA-S)</li>
<li><strong>Adequate sleep</strong> (sleep deprivation lowers DHEA-S)</li>
<li><strong>Mediterranean diet</strong></li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Treat underlying chronic illness</strong> that suppresses DHEA-S</li>
</ol>
