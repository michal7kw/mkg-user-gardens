<h1>Ulcerative Colitis</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, relapsing-remitting inflammatory bowel disease defined by <strong>continuous mucosal inflammation</strong> that begins in the rectum and extends proximally for a variable distance through the colon. Unlike Crohn's disease, inflammation is limited to the mucosa and submucosa (not transmural) and does not show &quot;skip lesions,&quot; making the extent of disease continuous and contiguous from the rectum.</p>
<p>UC results from a dysregulated immune response to luminal antigens and the gut microbiota in genetically predisposed individuals, with environmental factors modulating risk. A distinctive epidemiological observation is that, in contrast to Crohn's disease, current smoking appears protective while UC onset can follow smoking cessation. The chronic mucosal inflammation drives the cardinal symptoms of bloody diarrhea, urgency, and tenesmus.</p>
<p>Clinically, UC matters both for its impact on quality of life during flares and for its long-term complications, particularly the elevated risk of colorectal cancer in patients with extensive, long-standing disease and the risk of toxic megacolon during severe attacks. Management ranges from topical and oral 5-aminosalicylates for mild disease to biologics, small molecules, and ultimately colectomy, which is curative of the colonic disease.</p>
<h2>Clinical Presentation</h2>
<p>The hallmark presentation is bloody diarrhea mixed with mucus, accompanied by rectal urgency, tenesmus, and lower abdominal cramping. Disease severity ranges from mild proctitis to fulminant pancolitis with fever, tachycardia, anemia, and systemic toxicity. Extraintestinal manifestations include peripheral and axial arthritis, uveitis, erythema nodosum, and a strong association with primary sclerosing cholangitis.</p>
<h2>Diagnostic Information</h2>
<p>Diagnosis relies on colonoscopy with biopsy demonstrating continuous mucosal inflammation that begins at the rectum, with histology showing crypt architectural distortion and crypt abscesses confined to the mucosa. Stool studies exclude infectious causes, and fecal calprotectin plus serum inflammatory markers gauge activity. Disease extent — proctitis, left-sided colitis, or extensive colitis/pancolitis — guides therapy and cancer-surveillance strategy.</p>
<h2>Risk Factors</h2>
<p>Risk factors include a family history of IBD, former (rather than current) smoking, a Westernized diet, and an industrialized environment. Onset peaks in young adulthood with a smaller later-life peak. Prior infectious gastroenteritis and alterations in the gut microbiota are also implicated.</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>Treatment is stratified by disease extent and severity. 5-aminosalicylates (mesalazine, sulfasalazine), delivered topically and/or orally, are first-line for mild-to-moderate disease and maintenance. Corticosteroids treat flares but are not used for maintenance. Immunomodulators (azathioprine), anti-TNF and anti-integrin biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab), and JAK inhibitors are used for moderate-to-severe or refractory disease. Colectomy is curative of the colonic disease and is indicated for medically refractory disease, dysplasia/cancer, or fulminant colitis.</p>
<h2>Complications</h2>
<p>Severe flares can progress to toxic megacolon and colonic perforation, both surgical emergencies. Chronic mucosal bleeding leads to iron-deficiency anemia, and acute hemorrhage can be life-threatening. Long-standing extensive colitis substantially raises colorectal cancer risk, mandating endoscopic surveillance. Primary sclerosing cholangitis is a notable hepatobiliary association.</p>
<h2>Prognosis</h2>
<p>With modern therapy most patients achieve and maintain remission, and overall life expectancy approaches that of the general population. Prognosis is influenced by disease extent and the frequency and severity of flares. Colectomy, while major surgery, eliminates the colonic disease and the associated cancer risk.</p>
<h2>Epidemiology</h2>
<p>Ulcerative colitis is most common in North America and Northern Europe, with rising incidence in newly industrialized regions. It usually presents in the second to fourth decades of life and affects men and women roughly equally.</p>
