<h1>Creatine Kinase</h1>
<h2>Overview</h2>
<p>Creatine kinase (CK), also historically called creatine phosphokinase (CPK), is an intracellular enzyme that catalyzes the reversible transfer of a phosphate group between phosphocreatine and adenosine diphosphate (ADP) to regenerate ATP. This reaction provides a rapid energy buffer for tissues with high and fluctuating energy demands, most notably skeletal muscle, the myocardium, and the brain. Because the enzyme is normally confined to the cytoplasm and mitochondria of these cells, its appearance in the bloodstream signals that cell membranes have been damaged and intracellular contents have leaked into circulation.</p>
<p>CK exists as three cytosolic isoenzymes formed from two subunits (M for muscle, B for brain): CK-MM (predominant in skeletal muscle), CK-MB (enriched in cardiac muscle), and CK-BB (brain and smooth muscle). Total serum CK chiefly reflects CK-MM and therefore tracks skeletal muscle injury, whereas the CK-MB fraction was historically the workhorse marker of myocardial infarction before high-sensitivity cardiac troponin assays became standard. Serum levels are influenced by muscle mass, sex, ethnicity, and physical activity, so reference intervals are sex-specific and men typically run higher than women.</p>
<p>Clinically, CK is one of the most frequently ordered enzymes when muscle injury is suspected. It is central to the diagnosis and monitoring of rhabdomyolysis, where massive enzyme release accompanies a risk of acute kidney injury, and to surveillance of statin-treated patients who develop muscle symptoms. CK is also used to evaluate inflammatory myopathies, muscular dystrophies, trauma, prolonged seizures, and exertional muscle damage, and it is interpreted alongside myoglobin, aldolase, troponin, and electrolytes such as potassium.</p>

<p><strong>Notes:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reference intervals are method- and laboratory-specific; values are typically reported at 37 degC.</li>
<li>CK is higher in men, in individuals of African ancestry, and in those with greater muscle mass; these are physiologic, not pathologic, differences.</li>
<li>Strenuous exercise, recent intramuscular injection, surgery, or trauma can transiently raise CK independent of disease.</li>
<li>A single value should be interpreted in trend: CK in rhabdomyolysis usually peaks 24-72 hours after the insult and then declines.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Factors Affecting Levels</h2>
<p><strong>Increase Creatine Kinase:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Skeletal muscle injury (trauma, crush, surgery, intramuscular injections)</li>
<li>Rhabdomyolysis (toxins, drugs, prolonged immobilization, heat injury, infection)</li>
<li>Strenuous or unaccustomed exercise (especially eccentric and endurance exertion)</li>
<li>Myocardial injury (raises CK-MB fraction)</li>
<li>Inflammatory myopathies (polymyositis, dermatomyositis) and muscular dystrophies</li>
<li>Statin and fibrate therapy (muscle toxicity)</li>
<li>Hypothyroidism (reduced enzyme clearance and myopathy)</li>
<li>Seizures and prolonged tonic-clonic activity</li>
<li>Greater muscle mass and male sex (physiologic)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Decrease Creatine Kinase:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Low muscle mass and sarcopenia</li>
<li>Sedentary lifestyle</li>
<li>Advanced liver disease</li>
<li>Corticosteroid therapy</li>
<li>Early pregnancy</li>
<li>Connective tissue disease with reduced muscle bulk</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clinical Significance</h2>
<p><strong>Elevated levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rhabdomyolysis</strong> -- the hallmark finding; CK often exceeds 5,000-10,000 U/L, and the degree of elevation correlates with the risk of myoglobinuric acute kidney injury. Serial CK guides hydration and renal monitoring.</li>
<li><strong>Myocardial infarction</strong> -- the CK-MB isoenzyme rises within hours of cardiac injury; although high-sensitivity troponin is now preferred, CK-MB remains useful for estimating infarct size and detecting reinfarction.</li>
<li><strong>Statin-associated muscle damage</strong> -- CK elevation accompanies myalgia, myositis, and, rarely, statin-induced rhabdomyolysis; CK monitoring informs decisions to continue, reduce, or stop therapy.</li>
<li><strong>Inflammatory myopathies and muscular dystrophies</strong> -- markedly elevated CK supports the diagnosis of polymyositis, dermatomyositis, and dystrophinopathies and tracks disease activity.</li>
<li><strong>Exertional and traumatic muscle injury</strong> -- CK quantifies the magnitude of exercise-induced muscle damage and crush injuries.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Reduced levels are associated with:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Low muscle mass, advanced liver disease, and corticosteroid use; low CK is rarely clinically actionable but can mask muscle pathology in patients with little muscle bulk.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Monitoring use cases:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Serial CK during suspected or confirmed rhabdomyolysis to track resolution and renal risk</li>
<li>Baseline and symptom-driven CK in patients starting or escalating statins</li>
<li>Disease-activity monitoring in inflammatory myopathy</li>
<li>Assessment of training load and recovery in athletes</li>
</ul>
<h2>Strategies to Improve</h2>
<p>These strategies target abnormal CK elevation by addressing the underlying muscle insult; CK itself is a marker, not a therapeutic target.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat the underlying cause</strong> -- in rhabdomyolysis, aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation and correction of electrolyte disturbances are first-line; CK then falls as muscle injury resolves.</li>
<li><strong>Adjust offending medications</strong> -- if statin-associated muscle damage is confirmed, dose reduction, switching agents, or discontinuation under medical supervision lowers CK.</li>
<li><strong>Consider Coenzyme Q10 in statin myopathy</strong> -- supplementation (commonly 100-200 mg/day of ubiquinone or ubiquinol) has shown a modest benefit on statin-associated muscle symptoms and CK in some trials and meta-analyses, though evidence remains mixed.</li>
<li><strong>Allow muscle recovery after exertion</strong> -- reducing eccentric and unaccustomed exercise volume and permitting adequate recovery returns exercise-elevated CK toward baseline.</li>
<li><strong>Optimize thyroid status</strong> -- treating hypothyroidism corrects the associated myopathy and CK elevation.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain hydration and avoid muscle-toxic exposures</strong> -- adequate fluid intake and avoidance of heat injury, prolonged immobilization, and recreational toxins reduce the risk of muscle breakdown.</li>
</ol>
